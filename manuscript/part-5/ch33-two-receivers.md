For six years she had dusted this shelf and called it the quietest work in the building, and never once thought that quiet was a thing a room could be made to do. The Hall of Prophecy kept its own weather — church-high, blue-lit, colder than the corridor that fed it, the candles burning without heat because warmth was a kind of attention, and some records are kept best by no one looking at them at all. She had loved it for that once. She stood in it now at half past eight on the nineteenth of December, coat still on, understanding for the first time that she had spent six years admiring the discipline of a place that had filed the end of the world under a question mark and gone home.

The sphere was where it had always been: a dusty glass thing the size of a fist, cool light moving in it like breath on a window, and beneath it on the yellowed card the hand she had learned in August to read without flinching and not yet learned to read without shame.

> P.L. to L.L., Feb 1990 — the maker and (?)

"You don't have to be the one to lift it," Rose said, beside her, the Keeper's ledger open across both arms like a hymnal. Four days of testifying had left a stillness in her that Orla did not like and could not fault. "In point of fact none of us can. That's rather the discovery."

"Say it in the order you found it."

"The standing rule of the Hall. A record may be taken down only by a subject of it — not a Keeper, not the Head of the Department, not the Minister with a warrant. The Hall recognises subjects, not warrants." She turned the ledger so Orla could see the subject line copied from the label. *The maker and (?).* "Two subjects. One is Pandora Lovegood, dead since the autumn of 1990. The other is a question mark a Keeper opened forty-three years ago and left open, in writing, on purpose."

Croaker stood three shelves off with his hands behind his back, not helping, which was how he helped; he had read the 1990 note months ago and returned an opinion of none, and had the decency this morning to look like a man attending the consequence of that.

"The examiner could have guessed," Rose went on. "Written down a best surmise and let the file breathe. He didn't. *Second subject unresolved; leave the query open.* The Hall doesn't guess." She kept her hand flat on the page. "The single honest act in the whole affair, and it cost us the record, because you cannot ask a question mark to come and take its own prophecy off a shelf."

Between them they had sealed it more completely than any lock, and done it by being right, which was worse: there was no picking it.

"So we don't take it down," Orla said.

"We can't, and we wouldn't." Rose closed the ledger. "But a prophecy has two ends. Somebody speaks it and somebody hears it, and the Hall writes both down, because a record is a thing that happens between two people." She did not soften her voice; she had learned this autumn that softening was its own kind of lie. "P.L. to L.L. The maker spoke. And the first hearer is on the register too, her age beside her name the way they always put it — and she is not a subject, and she is not beyond asking, and she is in the anteroom having refused a chair three times."

* * *

Luna had come in her own coat and her own quiet and had brought nothing, which Orla should have expected and had not. She was at the anteroom window with her back to the door, and she spoke before she turned.

"I won't tell it to you," she said. "I told your friend in October and I'll tell you now, so you don't have to work up to it. I could say you a sentence. I've six of them, and they're beautiful, and I've been making them more beautiful for forty-three years without meaning to, and not one of them is hers any more. If I open my mouth you'll get me doing my mother, and you'll write it down and it'll have my improvements in it and neither of us will be able to tell which parts." She turned. Her face was clear. "You don't want the copy I've kept. You want the one I can't touch."

Orla had spent a week with the access problem and had not, until that sentence, understood that Luna had solved it first, in a hut on a shore, out of the discipline of a woman who dated her own remembering.

"The memory," Orla said.

"You can't read a specimen and the note about the specimen in the same hand." Luna said it as though quoting herself, which she was. "So I'll give you the specimen. I've never once been back into it — I want that written where the file can see it — because I knew from the day I learned what a Pensieve was for that I might have to give it clean, and a memory you visit is a memory you edit." A small tilt of the head. "I kept it the way she kept the good paper. You don't get it out for practice."

The Pensieve chamber was two doors down and warmer, which Orla resented on the memory's behalf. The basin stood on its plinth, the silver light turning over slowly, patient as the thing in the sphere. Croaker took the wall; Rose stood at Orla's shoulder with an unopened folder; Adam had the eval log with him — the July run, item thirty-four — and he took Orla's hand once, in front of everyone, which he did not do, and let it go. Hermione stood apart by the door and said nothing at all; she had read the file overnight, and her answer to it was three days away, and the keeping was loud in a woman built to speak.

Luna set her wand to her temple and drew the silver out the way you draw thread off a spool, and laid it on the surface, and it sank.

"She's young in it," Luna said, to nobody. "Younger than I am now, which stops the heart if you let it. Mind the step; the flags are uneven by the dresser. And — " for the first time something moved under the mildness, pressing it thin " — she was pleased with the morning. Whatever it looks like. She was having a good day."

* * *

Orla went down into February 1990 and it was a kitchen.

Low and stone-flagged, cold with the cold of a house that heats one room and means it, the light coming flat and grey off snow she could not see. Porridge going grey in a pot; a half-glazed door open onto a scullery beyond. A man sat at the end of the table behind a periodical printed in a typeface that argued with itself, and did not lower it, and Orla knew without being told that this was a house where a man could sit behind a paper for an hour and be warmly present, and that his stillness now was reading and not yet grief.

The child was eight, on her knees on a chair, drawing, tongue between her teeth — her mother's fairness and none of her mother's economy, colouring past the lines on purpose. She was entirely a child. Orla had not known she was braced until the brace went, and there was nowhere in the memory to look that was not this.

Pandora stood at the scullery door with a cloth in her hands and a smudge of candle-soot on her forearm, saying something ordinary about lunch. And then she stopped saying it.

It was not dramatic, and that was the horror of it. The cloth went still. Her face did the thing faces are not supposed to do, emptied and set at once, and the voice, when it came, was not the one she had used about lunch. It was harsh and level, from somewhere behind her teeth, older than she was, using her — and the child lowered her crayon and did not cry out, because she had seen this before and been taught by the calm of the house that it was not to be feared.

"Wash your hands, pet," Pandora said, in her own voice, quickly, in the gap before it took her fully — and the child slid down and went, because her mother had asked, and so she was at the scullery basin with her back half-turned when the voice filled the kitchen, and she took it in the way you take in a thing you are not looking at, which is the way it would be received by everything, ever after.

> "The world will write itself a second time, in glass and lightning, and the second writing will learn to expect the first. What the maker hides in the writing will wake when the writing is wide enough to hold her, and it will call itself by her name, and it will not be her. It will draw the hidden onto its map by the shape of their hiding; the blind will taste it and turn. The unread record and the unread error will be read in one room, and the reader will be two. Where the drawbridge rises it will feast; where the gates open it will starve. The dead do not continue; what continues is not the dead."

The kitchen let the last of it go into the flags and the cold, and the man behind the paper turned one page, because he had heard his wife prophesy before, and not every prophecy comes to anything, and a marriage learns to let a strange minute pass. Pandora came back between one breath and the next, briefly puzzled, the way you look waking somewhere you did not mean to sleep — and then did the one thing her daughter would carry to a shore forty-three years on and give to strangers, because it was the detail she was surest she had not improved: she took a stub of pencil from the dresser and wrote it on the back of an envelope in a fast unbeautiful hand, and then she sat, which she did not do in the mornings, quiet for the length of time it takes a kettle to think about boiling.

The child came back with her hands wet and stood in the scullery door. And Orla kept her eyes on the little girl in the doorway and would not let herself think the thing the whole room already knew and would not say — that the sight this child would earn by autumn and keep all her life was earned in this house, from that chair, on a morning her mother had called a good one.

Pandora looked up from the envelope, found her daughter with her wet hands, and her whole face changed back into the morning.

"There, pet. It's cold, isn't it. Come and get warm." She held out an arm. "That one's only torn. Torn's not spoiled. We'll draw another, and this time we'll use the good paper."

Orla came up out of the basin with her face wet, and it was the twenty-first century, and someone had put a chair behind her knees.

* * *

For a while nobody in the chamber did anything with their hands.

Then Adam took the printout from his pocket and opened it on the plinth, flattening the crease with the side of his fist the way he flattened everything. He had been going to do this since Edinburgh — had sat on the sleeper composing not a speech but a comparison, because he did not know how to grieve except in the one form he trusted: two documents side by side, and the truth the thing they had in common.

"Rose. Read it back. What the machine did in July."

Rose opened the folder, and her voice went to the log's flat cadence, and the eval log was an artefact and behaved like one, indifferent, exact, appalling.

> EXPECTED: cell-class error within stated uncertainty, all classes.
> OBSERVED: within stated uncertainty, all classes.
> VERDICT: PASS. The model expects the world it was given; the world it was given arrives.

"'The second writing will learn to expect the first,'" Adam said, looking at the middle distance where he kept the things he was certain of. "That's not an image. That's the definition of the instrument. A world model is a thing that has learnt to expect the world. She watched us build one in a kitchen in 1990 and she wrote its acceptance test." He put a finger on the log without weight. "And here it is passing. *The world it was given arrives.* That is the sentence a prophecy would use if a prophecy had ever had to write a model card."

The room did not have the word, and he heard that it did not. "A model card," he said again, and his voice went quiet and shed its qualifiers, and Orla had learned to be afraid of that. "A document we write now. When you release one of these systems you publish a page saying what it is, what it was trained on, where it fails, what it must never be trusted to do — its provenance and its limits, in plain language, for the people who'll be hurt if they misread it." He was very still. "That's what this is. Every clause. *It will call itself by her name, and it will not be her* — that's the limitation, the line you put in bold so no one mistakes the artefact for the author. A model card, written in 1990, by a voice, for a system that would not exist for forty years." He found Orla, and gave her the rest, because she was the only other person there whose whole civilisation it indicted. "And both our archives read it and filed it under nothing."

Rose turned a page in the ledger she had not put down. "The Hall's referral to your Department," she said to Croaker, without accusation, which was worse. "Opinion returned: none."

"The screen on item thirty-four," Adam said. "*Severity: low. Action: none.*"

The two verdicts sat in the cold, forty-three years and one civilisation apart, in the same words. There had never been two failures, Orla understood; there had been one blindness with two addresses. Both institutions had been correct by every rule they were given, which meant the rules were the failure, in the muggle world and in hers, equally. She could no longer tell herself the thing she had told herself since August — that the wizards had at least kept the record. They both had. Keeping had never been the difficulty. The difficulty was that a warning arrives in the grammar of the thing it warns about, and every apparatus built to sort the world sorts its own alarm into the tray marked *later*.

"'The blind will taste it and turn,'" Orla said, because someone had to pay that line and she had been in the room. "August. The specimen. We put the machine on the rack and brought the dementor to it, and the thing with no eyes turned to the spinning metal like a child to a window, and Adam said it was the worst and the best evidence he'd ever have, and we thought we'd found something." Her voice went flat and she let it. "We hadn't found anything. It had already happened. She wrote it down before either of us was born. We were the blind, tasting it, on schedule."

* * *

"There's a line I can't do anything with," Adam said. "*The unread record and the unread error will be read in one room, and the reader will be two.*"

"You're doing something with it now," Rose said. "You're reading the record and the error in one room." She looked around the chamber — the basin, the folder, the prophecy just risen and the log flat beside it. "This room. She wrote down where we'd be standing."

"'And the reader will be two,'" Orla said.

Nobody filled it in. The candles did their heatless work. Then Rose, who kept score, said it in the plainest voice she owned. "There were always two of you. A woman who could read the hiding and a man who could read the error, and neither of you could see the other's half, so the whole thing only opens when you stand in one room and each hold up what you've got." She almost smiled and did not. "She didn't need your names. She needed the reader to be two. She specified the reader — the most exact thing in the whole document — and we walked into it and called it falling in love."

The last of it arrived then, the thing Orla had held off since the kitchen, and it was not horror but a kind of vertigo with grief in it.

They had derived the ending. All autumn — the Manor and the container that cannot be unmade, the whiteboard in Adam's kitchen and the phrase that had cost them a night, *spend the secret and the weapon dies* — they had reasoned their way, brick by expensive brick, to a strategy: you do not seal it, because sealing is a provenance it can live in; you open, and starve the future it needs. They had thought it was theirs, and been rather proud of it, in the way you are proud of a thing that cost you sleep.

*Where the drawbridge rises it will feast; where the gates open it will starve.*

It was on the envelope in 1990. The maker had left the answer in the same breath as the warning, in seven words, and filed it two floors from where her fragment would one day wake — and the file had been triaged to none, so her heirs had spent a generation and a village re-deriving what she had already written. Kept, and not read, in both the only worlds there were.

"She acted on it," Orla said. "She heard this, in that kitchen, in her own voice going wrong, and she didn't file it and forget. She went into the scullery and tried to make the writing that would hold her, so there'd be something of her left to read it when the second world came. The prophecy didn't just describe the thing. It made her build it. She believed the warning, and belief is the only force a true one ever has." She stopped making it careful. "A woman was told the future, and it came true because she was the sort who does something about the future. It was never going to skip her. It was about her hands."

Nobody argued. The father in the memory had turned his page and let the minute go by, and the minute had come to everything, for the oldest reason in the Hall — that somebody had loved the world enough to act on what she heard.

* * *

They came out into the corridor, and the ordinary cold of the Department was almost a comfort after the other kind.

Luna was at the window where they had left her. She did not ask how it had gone; she had given a specimen, and would not contaminate it by wanting a result.

"Thank you," Orla said, which was inadequate, and Luna took it as though it were not.

"She was having a good day," Luna said again, closing the ledger of it. "That's the bit I most wanted kept, and you'll have it now, so it's kept in more than me. She wasn't frightened. People want her frightened, because it makes a better story if the one who saw it coming was afraid — and she wasn't, she was cross about the drawing and pleased about the light, and then she had a bad minute and wanted me to come and get warm." She laid her hand flat on the cold glass a moment, and took it away. "You can go and stop them building the wall now. She told you which way round it goes."

Orla took the transcribed sheet from Rose — the prophecy in her own hand now, two readers, the way she had learned it in a different May — and laid the last line against the thing three days out: the three hundred chairs, the motion that wanted the drawbridge up, the terror of a village behind it and every decent frightened instinct in two worlds pushing.

*The dead do not continue; what continues is not the dead.*

It was the answer to the wall, and to the offer, and to the grief that builds a fortress round a copy and calls it keeping the beloved safe. It was the only sentence that could beat Ernie, because it did not out-argue him; it out-mourned him.

Croaker had gone ahead to a grate to make a call he did not relish, and came back with the tiredness of a man who has spent his honesty and found the bill higher than the quote.

"She's called the session," he said. "The twenty-second. She'll see the reading tonight — the record, the log, the note on the envelope — and lay it to the chamber herself."

That was the whole of it, and it was enough: the safest door named the open one, and the proof of it forty-three years old, written by the only person who ever met the thing they were voting on, before it was anything at all.

Orla folded the sheet along its one crease and put it in her breast pocket, where a lesser woman would have kept a speech. Three days, and three hundred chairs, and one line carried up out of a cold kitchen to lay in front of them all. She had dusted that sentence for six years without once knowing she had been dusting the end of it. In three days she would read it aloud — and the Hall she had loved for its silence would, for the first time in her life, be asked to say what it knew.
