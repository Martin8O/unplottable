They left Edinburgh at five, in the last of the dark, the car packed to the roof-lining and the rain deciding, junction by junction, whether it meant it.

Orla had been standing at the corner of his street at ten to five, on foot, dry in a way he had elected not to audit, holding a canvas satchel and a paper bag of pastries with the top folded over twice. She had never, it emerged, been in a car for longer than twenty minutes. She rode the first hour the way a Victorian might have taken to the railways — upright, delighted, a little scandalised — and asked questions he had never thought to ask of his own vehicle. Why it chimed at her. What she had done to it. Why the dashboard had opinions about his eyes.

"There's a camera watching where I look," he said. "It's the law. Drivers kept dying of their phones."

She was quiet for a junction and a half. "And your lot worried about us," she said, to the windscreen, and he had no answer that improved on the wipers.

At the services outside Perth she inspected a meal deal with the attention her colleagues presumably kept for cursed manuscripts, pronounced the tea adequate — he had brought a flask anyway, which she pointed out was a vote of no confidence in the entire twenty-first century — and stood a while at the window, hands around the cup, saying nothing, while the car park filled with people who did not know what country they were parked in. Her Friday existed in no diary anywhere. His said annual leave. It was, they had agreed on Tuesday, the most honest either calendar had looked in weeks; it was also a thing no rule in either country had a category for, which is what forbidden means when the rulebook is proud of itself.

The last hour was single-track, passing places, sheep with no theory of traffic. The road gave out at a forestry gate streaked with lichen, and he backed the car onto the verge and shouldered the first load, and the glen took them in.

It was the first time in seven weeks that the work had weather. He had lived the whole affair indoors — seminar rooms, his kitchen, the corridors of two governments — and here it finally was outside, eleven kilos of it on his back, bog myrtle and cold peat, a burn running loud somewhere under its own banks, curlews making their long question mark of a call over the moor. The wind had an edge May hadn't signed off on. His boots went in to the laces twice in the first kilometre. It was, he noted, with something adjacent to happiness, fieldwork: the word the whole discipline used and almost none of it did.

At the lower cairn he built the science. Reference receiver on its tripod, levelled, logging; the rover unit on his chest strap, paired, both channels writing to cards, because the archive said electronics lied up here — lied worse the closer you stood — and he had believed the archive even before he had met the reason. Two small cameras on clamp mounts, one on the cairn, one on a post from a fence otherwise long surrendered, four-second interval, aimed up the glen. A film camera, mechanical, his own contribution to the question of what survives; and his grandfather's watch, wound that morning at the kitchen table, because his own watch was a computer and he wanted one witness with no opinions.

He had pulled the imagery on Thursday night: every vintage of the upper glen that money or an academic licence could reach, forty years of orbital photography of a country that was photographed entire, every day, from several altitudes at once. Every frame was fine. Moor, scree, a dull knoll, the pale thread of the burn — a competent picture of nowhere in particular, available in any weather he cared to order. It was the album that was impossible. Frame against frame, the glen declined to reconcile: ridgelines that agreed to a metre and then, next vintage, didn't; shadows in quiet minor disagreement with the sun that was supposed to have cast them; feature points the matching software planted with confidence and could never find again. The photogrammetric bundle for these six grid cells carried the worst residuals in Britain — that sentence was in the paper; it was Figure 2, panel c — and the radar stack was worse, coherence rotten across decades of moor that hadn't moved since the ice left it. Ten thousand photographs of this glen existed, and no two of them had been taken of quite the same place, and not one of them was of anything. He stood where all of them had been pointed and felt the hair on his arms attend.

Orla came back down the slope from wherever she had walked to, unhurried, reading the ground the way he read a diff.

"Conditions," she said.

"You said that in October tones."

"You'll hear the terms before you argue them." She held up one finger. "The southern edge only — the long way from anything that matters. Two hours by your grandfather's watch, and when I say done, we're done." A second finger. "I keep you in sight the whole time. All of it." The third finger stayed down; she looked at it, and then at him. "And I take you up on my arm. That's the one you'll fight."

"Escorted."

"Escorted. Muggles cross warded ground on somebody's arm every day of the year — parents at the station, a da walked in to his daughter's wedding. Company changes what the working does; it leans on you less. On my arm you'd reach the line and keep your hour."

"And the record would be worthless," Adam said. "One transect, one variable: me, the slope, the charm. If you're on my arm, I'm measuring you. I didn't drive four hours to collect a chaperoned null."

"The instrument you're proposing to send in unaccompanied," she said, "is you."

"Yes. It's the only instrument I own that can log its own interference." He heard the sentence, and heard what was wrong with it, half a beat before she said it.

"No," she said. "It can't. That's what the working is." She wasn't loud. She had never once been loud. "There'll be no moment, Adam. Nothing will happen to you. You'll just be a man who's remembered something at the car, and the remembering will feel like yours, because it will be yours — your own reasons, in your own voice, every one of them arriving finished. It doesn't stop you. It makes leaving your idea." The wind moved between them. "I've spent six years in a building where that's called gentle. I've heard it called kind. I'd rather not stand on a hill and watch it be either. But you'll do it anyway, so." She put out her hand for the notebook, opened it to the flyleaf, and handed it back with the pencil. "Your name. Your own hand. If a record's going up that hill, it'll have an owner."

He wrote his name. It felt, absurdly, like the most binding signature of his adult life, and his adult life included a mortgage.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I sign consent forms for a living. Other people's."

"I know," she said. "That's the worth."

* * *

The protocol was his own, drafted at the kitchen table on Wednesday, and it had the modest aim of turning one man's attention into a dataset: walk the bearing at a steady pace; count paces aloud in fifties; at each fifty, a notebook line — time from the watch, wind, footing, and a self-report on a scale he had invented and already distrusted, attention, nominal or otherwise. At any flagged deviation: stop, face uphill, read the compass aloud, plant a peg. It had looked rigorous in the kitchen. It had looked, if he was honest, like the kind of thing you build so that the building is the courage.

For twenty minutes it was only a hill. Pace, count, line. The rover pinged its small conscience against his sternum; the burn got underfoot again and was crossed on flat stones the colour of old pewter. At 08:47 he wrote *A-transect start, ref OK, wind WSW fresh*, and the handwriting was his Tuesday-seminar hand, quick and even.

The first deflection announced itself as diligence. The reference receiver — he ought to go back and check the reference receiver. The thought arrived with its coat on, reasonable, fully formed: multipath by the cairn, soft ground under the tripod, twenty sound reasons to walk back down and be sure, because what was the rover without the reference; the whole survey stood on that tripod—

He stopped. Faced uphill. Read the compass aloud to the moor like a man saying grace, and wrote, with a pencil that wanted very slightly to be doing something else: *09:12 — attention: first deflection, est. 180 m to line. Logged. Proceeding.* The wanting was the finding. He noted that the sentence pleased him, and that the pleasure had a short half-life against what it was evidence of.

After that they came on a schedule that wasn't his. The ground ahead was turned-ankle country, and the sensible route — any hill man would say so — bent away downslope. The light was going; he checked; it was morning; the certainty that the light was going survived the check by several seconds, sitting in his chest, fat and unbothered, a guest who knew the house better than he did. The renewal draft wanted a paragraph moved. The car might not be locked. Each reason arrived assembled, no draft stage, no seam — which was how his own conclusions had always arrived, all his life, and there the resemblance stopped being interesting and became the point. Somewhere ahead of him stood a working three centuries deep in production, and its entire interface was the feeling of having thought of something yourself.

At 09:16 the rover flagged its integrity twice and inflated its error bars, and he was grateful to it — one colleague on the hill still willing to say, in figures, *something here is off*. He planted pegs twelve, thirteen, fourteen. His fifty-counts were coming up short; he counted to fifty and had walked, by the pegs, more like forty; the arithmetic of his own legs had developed a rounding error, and all of it rounded downhill.

He faced uphill. He read the compass aloud.

What he remembered afterwards was this: the burn crossing under his boots again, flat stones, old pewter — and the puzzlement it raised being mild, professional, unhurried — because the burn was behind him.

* * *

He was standing at the lower flags with the notebook shut in his hand and the settled, comfortable knowledge of a job finished.

That was the whole of it. No seam, no headache, no missing-time shimmer off a film he had never liked anyway. Just: done here. The glen was surveyed, the glen was empty, there was a bacon roll in the cool-box at the car and the drive home would want daylight. The conviction had furniture. It had a *mood*, mild and satisfied, the specific after-flavour of work completed to spec, and he stood inside it the way you stand in your own kitchen while somewhere at the back of the house a door you did not open is quietly closing.

Orla was twenty yards off, coming down the slope towards him, and her face was doing something for which he had no baseline at all.

"We should get lunch," he heard himself say. "There's nothing up there."

"What time do you make it?" she said.

He didn't reach for the watch. That was the part he would keep returning to, in the weeks after: the question was trivial, and he answered it from conviction instead, because the conviction was standing nearer. "Quarter past nine. Twenty past."

"Your grandfather disagrees," she said, and it was her Tuesday voice with something wrong in it, and he looked.

The watch said 10:41.

He stood very still. His hands — he noticed; noticing was suddenly the entire remaining content of his profession — were cold in the bone-deep way that hands get in two hours of Highland wind and not in twenty-five minutes; the cold had been there all along, filed and unread, a measurement his body had taken that his mind had declined to ingest. Eighty-two minutes. He turned and looked up the glen, at moor and scree and a dull knoll and the pale thread of the burn, a competent picture of nowhere in particular, and the picture pushed back at his eyes with the gentlest imaginable pressure, and his gaze went — smooth, frictionless, *helpful* — to the reference receiver on its tripod instead.

"All right," he said. Quietly. The qualifiers had gone somewhere, and he let them go. "Show me."

They walked to the cairn camera and he crouched over the field laptop with rain starting again on the back of his neck, and scrubbed the morning at four seconds a frame. There he was, small and blue-jacketed, going up the glen in the jerky honest gait of timelapse. There was the first stop — 09:12, facing uphill, comic and dear, a stick figure reading a compass to a hill. And then, for fifty-one minutes, there was a man at the edge of resolution doing the survey: walking the line, station to station, planting pegs, writing — writing at length, notebook braced on his forearm, head down in the wind. At 09:54 the fence-post camera browned out for six frames and came back. At 10:12 the man stood without transition, walked thirty metres downslope, stopped, turned, went back up — the gait unhurried, the turns clean, three times over, each approach shallower than the last, like a negotiation. At 10:27 the negotiation ended. The man tucked the notebook under his arm and came down the glen at an easy pace, a hill-walker with a train to catch and time in hand, and did not look back once, and became him.

The track told the same story in coordinates: a tidy arc along an invisible contour, station-keeping loops, error bars swollen but the record intact — the loggers had sat through the entire event with the bovine loyalty of things that cannot be talked out of anything. Nothing had stopped recording, because nothing up there had ever objected to recording; it objected to *attending*. His instruments had kept him. He had not.

"I was working," he said. "Up there. Fifty minutes. I was doing the survey."

"You were," Orla said. "You talked to it. You told the fourteenth peg it was load-bearing and should act like it." A breath. "You were good, Adam. That's the part I'll not forgive it. You were entirely yourself, the whole hour — and it won anyway, and you folded the book shut and gave me a nod like a colleague leaving a meeting, and came down."

His family had a doctrine about lists, carried out of Leipzig in 1938 in lieu of luggage: know what is written about you; know who holds the pen. He had built a career on its second face — never be the man who runs the check — and spent that career, every working day of it, in other people's exhaust: the pings, the tracks, the shed residue of attention that people scattered and never once got to audit. He had thought, until 10:41 this morning, that he understood the asymmetry from both ends. But the lists only ever took your name. This had taken the hour itself — hands, handwriting, the small competences he thought of as *him* — run them well without him, and returned the body with a cover story and a mood. He had been edited. Not his file: him. The redaction was seamless from the inside, mildly pleased with itself, and if she had not been standing on the hill with her unbearable profession, he would have defended it over dinner as his own opinion.

"Your world calls that one gentle," he said.

"My world calls that one the polite one," she said, and then she stopped, and the plainness he had seen once in his kitchen came down over her face. "I keep the ledgers of an office that does this on purpose, to order, at scale. I've initialled the counts. I knew every word of what I just watched, and I had never once stood where it happens, and I'd like it noted somewhere unerasable that I hate every civilised inch of it." She looked at the moor. "Note taken?"

"Two readers," he said. "It's a record now."

The work they did after that was, by any sane accounting, the best of his year. She walked the boundary — walked it the way a woman walks her own street in the dark, certain to the arm's length — and called it, and he stood a hundred metres off with the spare logger slung on her satchel strap, doing the one thing her craft could not: writing it down in a coordinate system. Her perception, his bookkeeping. Peg by peg the southern edge of Site 01 came into existence as numbers — a polyline with honest error bars, the first instrumental record of a ward boundary in the history of either civilisation, sixty-one vertices of treason on a memory card. She had taken his instrument; he had taken her word for where the world folded; both records agreed. The agreement felt like a first sentence of the paper neither government knew it wanted.

They packed up at two, in rain that had finally committed. At the boot of the car she handed him the flask lid full of tea he hadn't asked for and said, not looking at him, "The hour's not gone, you know. I was there. I have it — every minute, what you did, what you said to the pegs. When you want it back, I'll read it back to you properly. Not here. It's a long record, and you'll want to be sitting down, and I'll want to do it right."

"Next Tuesday," he said.

"Next Tuesday."

He believed her, and filed it, and did not yet understand what he had been promised. Then he opened the notebook against the boot lid to log the pack-up time, which is how he came to be standing in the rain looking at the facing pages.

The left page was dense. It was his best field hand, disciplined under wind, sixteen entries between 09:16 and 10:09 — bearings, peg numbers, the rover's flagged error bars, one wry marginal note about peg fourteen: a good surveyor having a good morning at the edge of the impossible.

The right page had four lines.

> 10:14 west leg complete. conditions good.
>
> 10:19 resurveyed upper stations. no anomaly obs.
>
> 10:24 no further entries required.
>
> nothing here

The hand was his all the way down, and it never shook; it eased, line by line, the way a voice trails off when the speaker decides the room already agrees. The survey notes were better than the ones he made on purpose. The last entry was two words long, in handwriting he had known for thirty years, in a book with his own name in the front, and it was the neatest sentence anyone had ever written in his voice.

*Nothing here.*
