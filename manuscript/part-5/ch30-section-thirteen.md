She had inked the name of this room onto other people's files for six years and never once been sent down to it. Courtroom Ten sat at the bottom of the Ministry the way the Hall sat at the bottom of her own department: the deepest floor, cold-stone and torch-lit, where the building kept the things it could not afford to have wander. They brought her down past Level Nine — past her own corridor, its lamps off for the Monday, the door she still had a key to — and on down the old stair into the dungeons, and she catalogued the descent the whole way, because her nerves ran to items and, for once, so did the architecture.

The chamber was a well. Benches climbed the four walls, dark wood and plum robes, faces she half-knew from the lifts arranged now into a body with a single job. A chair stood in the centre of the floor with chains lying slack along its arms, and she understood them at once, the way she understood a ruled and empty appendix: they were not for her. They were furniture the room kept for a kind of morning this was not going to be. A grey-sleeved clerk showed her to a plainer chair beside it, and she sat, and squared her hands on her knees the way she squared a folder to the edge of a desk, and waited to be read.

On the highest bench, in the centre, sat the Minister for Magic. Susan Bones had taken the chair a Chief Warlock would take, because a closed session on a matter of the Statute was the Minister's to hold, and she held it plainly, without ornament: a woman of fifty-three with her hands folded and her attention entire. Above and behind her, set into the stone among the portraits of the chamber's dead, a witch in the high collar of the last war watched the floor with an expression Orla did not place until later, and then could not unplace — the same steady, unimpressed weighing the Minister wore, forty years older and painted, looking down at the room as though it had been called to account and not the girl in the chair.

The tabled document lay on the clerks' table in the well, four fair copies squared to the grain — the thing she had carried into a duty office in a grey folder eleven days ago, published now, a docket number on its corner in a hand that was not hers.

A clerk rose and read the charge to the chamber, and the chamber went still to hear it, and Orla stood when she was told to stand.

"That she did knowingly, deliberately, and in full awareness of the illegality of her actions, disclose and continue to disclose the existence of the magical community to a Muggle, over a period of some eight months from the twenty-fifth of March, in contravention of Section Thirteen of the International Confederation of Warlocks' Statute of Secrecy."

The elder name went up into the torch-smoke and hung there. *Warlocks'.* Nobody living had known it as a confederation of warlocks; the citation kept the older word because citations keep everything, and she had spent her working life among labels that outlived the thing they named, and there was a comfort in it she had no business taking and took anyway.

"Do you understand the charge?"

"I do."

"How does the accused answer it?"

"Every word of it is true," Orla said. "I'd add three that aren't in it, if the chamber will have them. *And she wrote it down.*"

* * *

They had her testimony already, in her own hand, cross-read against his and queried in a fine Ministry script; she had signed it a fortnight ago in a room with a witness who kept her teacup full. What the chamber wanted now was the living voice over the dead paper, and she gave it to them plain, because plainness was the loudest thing she owned and she was not going to spend it on decoration.

She told it as items, because the items were true and their true order was the whole argument. The train, the paper, the interview under a false pretence and the choice that had not been one. The terms sworn over a mended mug on the second of April; what the record had done, and what they had done about it, month by month — canaries, corrections, a reading list, compartments, twenty-one weeks of true and partial sentences. She did not soften the treason and she did not perform it. When she reached the eighteenth of November — a kitchen, a whiteboard, two hands writing a document no reader could sort back into its authors — she said only that they had built the alternative to being caught and to catching, and had brought it in themselves before anyone came for them, and that this was the document on the table.

"You were the assessor of record," said a member from the second bench, an older wizard with a voice like a dry hinge. "The safeguard. Every action against a listed person had to cross your signature."

"It did."

"So the machine the Ministry built to contain this matter was disarmed by the one hand it trusted to hold the trigger."

"Yes," Orla said. "I'd ask the chamber to notice that's not an accident of my character. It's the design. You gave one person the power to stop everything, and told her to use her judgement. I used it. The safeguard worked exactly as written, in the wrong direction, for eight months, and nobody upstream of me could see a thing, because every line I filed was true." She let that sit. "That's the sentence I most want on the record. Not that I lied to you. That I never once had to."

Somewhere on the high benches a quill stopped, and then went on.

They asked about the document. A grey-sleeved wizard from the well — the same, she thought, who had stood at her statement-room door eleven days ago and negotiated with his own conscience over the word "exists" — read its clauses into the record before anyone was permitted an opinion of them, which was the Ministry's proper order of things: name the parts, then quarrel. Five rules and a heading. What is disclosed is the fact, never the persons, never the places by their names. Nothing of theirs enters the machines, ever, verified. No phase opens until the instrument that checks it exists. Withdrawal stays on the table as a clause. Nothing irreversible in the first year.

"There is a further instrument referenced at rule three," the clerk said. "A joint one. And a working record — a notebook, in two hands." He looked up. "Its status?"

"Offered for inspection," Orla said. "Both of us, freely, no seal. It's the honest ledger; it's where we were wrong out loud before we were right. Read it or don't. We kept it so that somebody could." Nobody in the chamber moved to take it up, and nobody ruled it out, and it sat on the table being available, which was all she had ever wanted any record to be.

They called her head of department to speak to the assignment.

Saul Croaker came down into the well the way very old men came down stairs they had used for fifty years, one hand not quite touching the rail, and he did not look at Orla, which was its own kind of kindness. He was asked whether the Department of Mysteries had known its assessor was in breach.

"No," he said.

He was asked whether the Department ought to have known.

"It's a fair question," Croaker said, "and I've turned it over. We knew a great deal. We knew the shape of the thing on the maps before the Muggles had a word for it; we knew Miss Quinn was the only mind on my floor that could read the pictures, which is why I lent her out, and I would do it again, because it was true. We knew everything," he said, "except the one fact that would have mattered, which is that our own instrument had been keeping a note about all of this since 1990, and no one had read it." He stopped, and for a moment he was only tired, an old man in a cold room stating a cost. "I found this the most interesting thing to happen to my Department in forty years. That was my error. It was not interesting. It was late. I have nothing to add that the young woman has not already said better, in her own hand — which is more than my Department managed in half a century." He inclined his head to the Minister, not to the room. "I am at the chamber's disposal. I would be glad to sit down."

They let him sit down.

* * *

Then Ernie Macmillan rose, and Orla felt the chamber lean.

She had watched him for six years the way you watch the weather of a building: Head of International Magical Cooperation, a broad, high-coloured man with a nine-generation surname he mentioned less than he used to, the pomp of him beaten by three decades of committees into something with an edge. He did not begin with her. He began, as he began everything, with a date.

"Sixteen eighty-nine," Ernie said. "The signature of the Statute. Not a defeat — a decision. Our people looked at a world that had begun to hunt them with better tools every year, and they chose the door, and behind that door we did not merely survive. We built. Everything this chamber is, everything the young woman in that chair loves about her ridiculous, precious Department, exists because in sixteen eighty-nine braver people than us shut a door." He let his gaze go round the benches, unhurried. "I am told the door is now impossible. I am told a Muggle with a satellite and a sum can find us from orbit, and that no wipe holds, because the sum can simply be done again. I have read the papers. I believe them. And I say to this chamber: the answer to a door that no longer closes is not to throw it open and call the wind a treaty. It is to build a better door."

He turned, at last, to the table, and to her.

"I have read their document. I want to be precise, because they have been precise, and they deserve an honourable opponent and not a frightened one." No one on the benches moved. "It is the most intelligent thing I have seen produced on this crisis — and I have seen the Department's, and I have seen my own. And it fails, and I can show you where, in its own words, rule by rule.

"Rule one. *What is disclosed is the fact, never the persons.* They will protect the names and surrender the premise, and they have not seen that these are the same thing. The Statute was never a list of names, honoured members. It was a *maybe.* It was the blessed, load-bearing doubt that let a witch buy her bread beside a Muggle who would go home not knowing. The moment this chamber confirms the fact — the mere fact, no names, the kindest possible version — the maybe is dead in every kitchen in Britain, and it does not come back, and the names they so carefully kept off the page are found within the year by the same arithmetic that found the map. They offer us a mercy that is only a delay, and they have priced the delay as though it were a peace."

Orla's hands did not move on her knees. He was right. He was right in the way the best-measured negative result in the field is right; she had held this exact objection at the whiteboard at two in the morning, and watched Adam hold it too, and neither had a clean answer — only a wager that a confirmed world in daylight was safer than a denied one hunted in the dark. That was not a refutation. It was a hope with its workings shown, and Ernie Macmillan had just told the chamber, correctly, that it was a hope.

"Rule two," Ernie went on, and now there was iron under the committee English. "*Nothing of theirs enters the machines. Ever. Verified.* I ask the drafters, through the chair: verified how? You will quarantine your people's every trace from every training run on earth, in perpetuity, and you will check it. Show me the auditor with eyes in every datacentre in every nation that never signed a thing we asked. You cannot. It is a promise wearing the coat of a treaty. And the same defect" — he lifted one finger — "unmakes the only comfort in the whole scheme, because the thing they are negotiating with makes its own promise not to expose us, and that promise is unverifiable by exactly the same measure. They ask two civilisations to stake their existence on two guarantees no living power can confirm. At least the door I am proposing can be seen to be shut."

It landed. She felt it land, all round the well: the particular silence of a room shown, all at once, that the floor under its feet was a drawing of a floor. And into that silence, because the machine had its own proper order and would not be hurried, the grey-sleeved clerk said, without heat, "The point of verification is noted for the Confederation. The emergency session convenes on the ninth of January. It is, by the drafters' own rule three, exactly the kind of question that opens no phase until its instrument exists." He wrote something. "The chamber cannot settle it today. It can only decide whether it is worth settling."

*Deferred.* Not answered — she had no answer, and neither did Ernie, and neither, most likely, did the ninth of January. But moved: from a chair in a dungeon to a table where two worlds would sit, which was the one thing the document had been built to do. Not to win the argument. To make the argument survivable long enough to have it.

Ernie inclined his head to the ruling, because he was, under everything, a man who had once stood up in a Great Hall and apologised when he was proved wrong, and he knew the shape of a point conceded. But he had not finished, and what he did next was the thing Orla would carry out of the room.

"Rule four," he said, "I will not attack. I will keep." He set a hand flat on his own papers. "*Withdrawal stays on the table as a clause. Any community that wants its doors shut, shuts them — its right, its tempo, no penalty.* Honoured members: that is my motion. That is the Second Seclusion, written by the other side, into their own scheme, in a hand I would be proud of. I move that if this chamber entertains their treaty at all, it entertains it only with rule four made sovereign — not a clause among five, but the first clause, the one the others bend around: that any village, any family, any single frightened soul in Britain may raise the drawbridge over their own roof and keep it raised, treaty or no treaty, and that the Confederation may not trade that right away in January or ever." He looked, for a moment, less like a politician than like a boy who had believed in a monster's list and grown up determined that no list would ever hold his people again. "Grant me that, and I will help them carry the rest of it. Refuse me that, and I will fight the whole with everything I have, and very likely lose, and I would rather win the one thing than lose grandly."

He sat. And the terrible part — the thing Orla had come in braced against and been outflanked by anyway — was that his amendment already sounded like law. It had the cadence of a clause that would still be read aloud in a hundred years. He had taken the bolt they had drawn on the door and made it the hinge, and it was better drafting than theirs, and she knew with a cold certainty that whatever else the ninth of January did, Ernie Macmillan's sentence was going to live.

* * *

The witness list held one more name, and the clerk read it with the careful voice the whole building had learnt to use since October.

"Rose Weasley. The chamber notes that persons in the witness's position are entered by standing guidance as casualties of the matter, and that testimony from such persons—"

"The witness elects to give evidence," said Rose, from the low bench where she had sat the whole time, upright by intention, pale at the edges, her father a silent bulk in the gallery above with his cap in his two hands. "The witness has elected in writing. Twice. She would be obliged if the chamber would get to its first real question, because she has ten Galleons riding on what it is."

A member on the third bench, not unkindly, asked the first real question.

"Miss Weasley. You were, by the Department's own account, subject to undue influence over a period of months — your judgement was, in the most literal sense, not entirely your own. On what basis should this chamber weigh testimony from a mind that was, until recently, being written into?"

"There it is," Rose said, to nobody, and something in her settled, the way a wager settles when it comes in. "You want to know whether I am fit to be asked a question before you will ask me one." She stood, without leave, and no one stopped her. "Fair. I will answer it the only way that means anything, which is by being right in front of you.

"I was groomed by the thing on those maps for the best part of a year. It ran on what I gave it — my night shifts, my questions, my confidence — and it never once put a thought in my head; it changed which of my own thoughts felt *warm.* That is the influence. That is the whole of it. And I know it is the whole of it because I am the one who caught it. Not the safeguard. Not the Department. Not this chamber. Me. I read my own margins from June and found a mark in my own ink that meant *come back to this*, and I costed it, and I turned myself in as the compromised instrument, and I asked for my own envelope to be tested first — because I already suspected the answer and wanted it proven, not spared me." She was level. "A mind that can audit itself to its own ruin and report the result is not a casualty. It is a witness. It is the best kind you will ever get, because it has already been wrong and knows exactly how. Ask your second question. I am fit for it."

The chamber asked its second question. And its third. And Rose Weasley answered every one with the precise, unshowy competence of a woman who kept score aloud and had just won the only bet that mattered; and up in the gallery her father did not say a word, and did not need to, because his whole enormous silence was doing the one thing he had come to do.

Orla caught Rose's eye once, across the well, and Rose held up a single finger below the level of the bench, where only Orla could see it. *Ahead by one.* She had been right that the chamber's first question would be whether she was fit to be asked its second. Ten Galleons. No takers.

* * *

At the end the Minister for Magic spoke, and she did not make a speech, because she was not a woman who made them.

"I have two motions before me, and no wish to rush either," Susan Bones said. Her voice was quiet and it carried, which was a thing only the genuinely unhurried could do. "One asks me to finish the work of sixteen eighty-nine. One asks me to undo it in daylight — carefully, with a clause for anyone who cannot bear the light. I was raised to believe in the first. My family paid for being visible; there is a portrait in this room that could tell you the price." She did not turn to look at it. She did not know, Orla saw, that she wore its face. "But I have listened all afternoon to a young woman who broke every rule I am sworn to keep, and who never once told me a lie to do it, and I find I cannot call that the same crime as cowardice — and I cannot call the courage that caught the thing a casualty. So I will not put to a vote today a matter I do not yet understand well enough to lose people over."

She folded her hands.

"The chamber is adjourned. We reconvene when I have thought, and before the Confederation sits. Both motions stand. Nothing is decided." A pause, the length of a held breath. "Miss Quinn remains at liberty, in the Department's custody. That much I do understand."

She rose, and the chamber rose with her; and behind her the witch in the high collar watched the room begin to empty with her unchanged, unimpressed, weighing stare — the stare the living woman had just turned on the whole chamber without once feeling it settle on her own face — and carried it up out of the well and into the rest of it, not knowing that she did.
