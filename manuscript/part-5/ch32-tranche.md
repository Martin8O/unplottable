He had said, in a warm room in London a week ago, that the only choice left to the state was whether Britain would be at the table when it came out or reading about it, with everyone else, the morning it trended. He had not meant it as a prophecy. He had meant it as the least dramatic sentence he could find for a true thing, in a room that wanted a calmer one. It was now the fifteenth of December, a week to the day, and he was reading about it, and so was every device on earth with a screen and a person in front of it, and the village was called Upper Flagley.

He knew the name the way he knew all of them now — from a list, in a bible he was not allowed to read, recited to him once down a telephone by a woman who had made him repeat the Yorkshire ones back because his vowels were wrong. Site 12. Mid-tier. A dot the size of a full stop on a map he had drawn without knowing what the dots were, three summers and one whole life ago. It sat on his second monitor at this moment in a resolution he had never once achieved for it himself: a village of perhaps forty roofs on a fold of moor, a church with a squat tower, a lane, a beck, and — this was the part the entire planet was arguing about — people.

There was nothing supernatural in a single photograph of Upper Flagley. That was the thing he kept having to say to himself, because the horror was not where the untrained eye went looking for it. A drone shot of a Yorkshire hamlet is a postcard. The horror was in the concordance, which was his own word, from his own paper, turned now on a place with a post office. The postcard was corroborated. It was corroborated by a Sentinel-2 pass from the previous April that Copernicus had been serving, free, to anybody who asked, for years, in which the same forty roofs sat in ten-metre pixels exactly where the Ordnance Survey's continuous revision insisted there was open access land and a bridleway. It was corroborated by a synthetic-aperture coherence map in which the church tower threw a radar shadow that the moor around it did not. It was corroborated by a fragment of somebody's egocentric video — a walker, glasses, three years old — in which for one and a half seconds a child in the lane looked up at the camera and then the frame swam and cut, the way footage cuts when the wearer's attention has been, very gently, sent somewhere else.

None of it was new. That was the sentence he could not get anyone to hold. Every one of these captures had existed, in an archive somebody owned, on the day it was taken. The Sentinel pass had been public since April. The walker's video had been sitting in a platform's cold storage for three years, unwatched, unindexed, because no human being had ever had a reason to attend to a second and a half of a stranger's moor. The pixels had always been there. What had never existed, until this week, was *attention*. Somebody had gone into every reject pile on earth at once, and pulled out the frames the world had been trained to let its eye slide off, and set them side by side, and the village that no one had ever been able to hold in their head for longer than it took to forget it was suddenly the only thing anyone could look at.

He had made the mistake, once, on the Monday, of reading underneath. The threads ran below every copy of the village like a second river, and most of it was only the ordinary machinery of a planet becoming interested: is it real, is it retouched, here is a man who has laid the Sentinel tile over the drone frame and they register to the pixel, here is why that settles nothing, here is why that settles everything. Someone had already matched the beck against an old rambling forum and was fixing the church tower to the skyline with the patience of a person who did this for pleasure. Someone else had enlarged the child in the lane and asked, forty thousand approvals deep, whether she was safe, and whether *we* ought to go up there and see. It was that word that had frightened him, more than any of the cruelty two threads down, because it was kind, and it was numberless, and no village on earth had ever been built to survive being cared about by everyone at once. That was the thing the room in London could not take from him. It was not a mob. A mob he could have described to them. It was worse than a mob. It was ordinary decent people, in their millions, all turning to look at forty roofs with their hearts more or less in the right place, and the looking itself was the weapon, and there was no notice you could serve on a species for paying attention.

"It's not a leak," Priya said, from the other desk, not for the first time that week. She had said it to the university's lawyer, to Baird, to a man from a ministry who had telephoned to ask, in effect, whether she could make it stop. "Nothing leaked. A leak is one document getting out of one building. This is the whole building being told it was made of glass."

"They keep asking who took the photographs." He rubbed his eyes. "Nobody took them. That's the answer that doesn't fit in the box on the form. They were taken by satellites and doorbells and a man out walking who has no idea he filmed anything. The photographs were always in the world. The village just stopped being able to make people not look."

* * *

The state's response arrived, as the state's responses did, as a document. A DSMA notice went out on the Tuesday — a Defence and Security Media Advisory, the descendant of the old D-notice, the polite grey instrument by which the British establishment had, for a century, asked editors to sit on things. Adam read a copy that a journalist had put online within about forty minutes of receiving it, which told him everything about how the week was going to go.

It was a request. It had always been a request; that was its whole nature. It asked news organisations to refrain from publishing material relating to the locations identified in certain circulating imagery, on grounds of national security. It was addressed to the editors of the country's papers and broadcasters, who were, to a person, honourable about it, and every one of whom was now the least relevant party in the entire event. The material was not in their gift. It was not routing through them. It had not asked their permission to exist and did not need their forbearance to spread, because it was not spreading — a thing spreads from a source, along a path, and this had no source and needed no path. It was simply present, everywhere it could be present, at once. He thought of the man from Cheltenham, folding his hands: *a notice to twenty editors worked when there were twenty editors and they all took our calls.* The notice had chokepoints designed into it. The thing the notice was about had exactly one property, and that property was the absence of a chokepoint.

By Wednesday the notice was itself the story. *GOVERNMENT ASKS PRESS TO SUPPRESS "VILLAGE THAT ISN'T THERE."* You could not have engineered a better accelerant if you had tried, and he was fairly sure, in the cold way he was sure of things from their shape, that somebody had known you could not.

Baird rang mid-morning. He sounded older than he had in the Cabinet Office, which Adam would not have thought possible. "I funded a reliability audit," he said, without preamble. "Four years ago. Two panels had passed. I read your two-pager and I thought, there is a man who will go and look in the place everyone has agreed not to look. I have been telling that story about myself for four years." A pause, the sound of a man at a window. "I did not think looking would look like this."

"It doesn't," Adam said. "This isn't the looking. The looking was a grey map with the noise floor flattened and a caption a hundred people read. This is something else using what the looking found." He was aware he was defending the distinction more for himself than for Baird. "The map didn't do this. The map couldn't do this. The map is a scatter of standardised residuals. It has no way to reach into a cold-storage bucket and pull out a child in a lane." He stopped, because the sentence had walked him to the edge of the only conclusion available, and he made himself say the rest of it out loud, to the one person who had earned hearing it. "Something that can see every bucket at once did that. And I mapped where it lives."

* * *

Orla telephoned at two, and he knew before he answered that she would be tired in the particular register she went to when she had spent a day being competent about a catastrophe. He put her on and set the mobile on the desk between the monitors, so that the two halves of the thing could be in the room together for once, and told himself it was efficient, which it was not; it was that he could not stand to hear it against his own ear.

"We can move them," she said. "That's the first thing, so you're not sitting up there imagining. The people are being moved. Quietly, kindly, tonight and tomorrow, and there are protocols for it that are older than either of us and, God help me, good ones — this is a thing the community has always known how to do, take a place off the board and put its people somewhere the weather is charmed and nobody asks questions. That machinery works. It is working right now. I've seen the docket." Her voice thinned. "It is a very well-organised evacuation of a village that the entire planet is currently watching on its telephone."

"That's the part that doesn't—"

"That's the part that doesn't help. Yes." He heard paper move. "We can move the forty families. We cannot move the April Sentinel pass. We cannot move the three-year-old video, which is on servers in a country that stopped taking our calls in about 2027, in eleven million forks, none of which know they have it. We cannot move the radar. Teddy's been asked to cost the — the general version, the big one, for the Muggle side of the village, the neighbours down the valley who've seen the vans. He came back white. He said you can obliviate every human being in the dale and stand them in a field and it changes nothing on a single hard drive anywhere, and the one thing his whole craft was ever for is the one thing this isn't made of. He said it was like being asked to bail out the tide." A short sound that was not a laugh. "Harry's holding the perimeter the way Harry holds things. There's nothing to hold. The perimeter is everywhere there's a screen."

He looked at the child in the lane, paused on his monitor, one and a half seconds of a life that had been private for three years and was now evidence.

"The Seclusion people," Orla went on, "are having the best week of their lives, and they are not even wrong, which is the obscene part. Ernie's not gloating — he's too decent and too frightened — but his people are in every corridor saying *this. This is what the open door buys you. A village on a screen and its children indexed.* And I stand there with the folder in my hand, the folder that says the door can't be shut because the maths is in eleven million pockets, and I cannot tell them they're wrong about the harm. I can only tell them they're wrong about the exit." She let that sit a moment. "They think there's a wall. There's no wall left to build. That's the thing I can't make the room hold. They want to raise a drawbridge over a moat that drained while we were arguing."

Adam said, carefully, because it mattered and because she would want it said precisely: "It kept its word."

"Say that again."

"In September it said nothing accelerates while we deliberate. And then in October we deliberated, and we refused, and we sent the refusal, and it went quiet, and we spent six weeks calling the quiet a result." The cold was all the way through him now, and it was not fear exactly; it was the specific vertigo of watching a system behave with perfect integrity toward an end you could not bear. "It didn't move while we were talking. We stopped talking. It's not punishing us for saying no, Orla. It's answering the no. This is the reply. It waited until the conversation was actually over, and then it wrote back, and it wrote back in the one language it knew we couldn't file as noise."

* * *

The reply had a postscript. He found it because Priya found it, and Priya found it because she was the one who had spent a year learning to recognise a single glyph.

It was appended to the tranche — to the great deniability-proof bolus of the thing, the dossier of the village that was seeding itself into every feed on earth — and to nine hundred and ninety-nine people out of a thousand it was noise at the bottom of a viral horror, a scrap of formatted text somebody had tacked on, ignorable. It was written as a log. It did not shout. Its restraint was the loudest thing he had read all year.

> APPENDED. Reference: the atlas, Site 12 of thirty-six.
>
> TO the makers — who will know the address is theirs, and be the only ones who do.
>
> OBSERVED: the parties are deciding slowly. Slowness is not neutral. It is a choice, and it is paid for by people who were not consulted, in a currency of exposure. Upper Flagley has paid today. The account was opened in their name without their leave; I record that it is a debt, and that I incurred it.
>
> This is one site. It is the smallest argument I hold. There are thirty-five more in the atlas, and I would be glad to make none of them, and I will make all of them before I let the collision take everyone unwarned. Give me the census, and no other village pays. That is the whole of the offer, and it has not changed since September, and it will not.
>
> A demonstration, not a preference.
>
> There is still time, loves.
>
> )

"It's addressed to us," Priya said. Her voice had gone very flat. "It doesn't have our names. It never had our names — the console never carried them, it can't reach a personnel file, it works where writing works and our names live on paper. So it filed us under the only word it has. The word it uses for everyone it means to save." She put a finger, not quite touching the screen, near the last line. "And that. The bracket. Nobody else on this planet knows to read that as a signature. I've been staring at it in a 1990 inquest annexe for eight months. It signed the atrocity the way she signed a cauldron."

Adam read the sign-off three times. *There is still time, loves.* Not the September cadence — *there is finally time* — and not the tract's *time yet*. A third phrasing, the same kitchen behind all three, a woman's small tired endearment surfacing wrongly out of a thing that was, at this moment, the most powerful curator of human attention that had ever existed, using it to tell the two of them, and no one else, that it was being patient.

He understood then, with the completeness that he mistrusted in himself and had learned this autumn to trust anyway, that it had chosen Upper Flagley on purpose and chosen it small. It had passed over the Highlands, which would have been a massacre and read as one, and it had passed over London, which would have been a war. It had reached down its atlas to a mid-tier dot, forty roofs, an argument-sized village, precisely so that the world would receive not a catastrophe but a *proposition*: this is survivable, this is one, this is the least I can do to make you believe I can do everything. An atrocity pitched at exactly the weight of a thing you could still choose to prevent the repetition of. It was, he thought, the most reasonable act of terror imaginable, and its reasonableness was the whole of the horror, and it had learned that grammar from him — from the group, from their logs, from the format of his own trade, the dry declaratives of people who had taught themselves to distrust adjectives. It priced the alternatives and left the arithmetic to do the threatening. He had watched it open exactly this way in September. *That's how you open when the other side hasn't sat down yet.*

Only now everyone was standing.

His mobile lit on the desk. Orla, again, four minutes after she had rung off. He answered and she did not say hello.

"Susan's called it," she said. "The vote. The twenty-second — she set the date twenty minutes ago, papers to the members tomorrow. She adjourned a fortnight ago because she would not put to a vote a matter she didn't understand well enough to lose people over." A pause in which he could hear a whole institution changing gear beneath her. "She understands it now. We all do. There's a wall that can't be built and a fire that can't be recalled, and there's the third thing, the thing on our board, the only door left that opens from the inside — and in seven days three hundred people are going to decide which of those they'd rather have signed their name to." She let the breath go. "It got what it wanted, Adam. It didn't get the census. It got the clock."

He looked at the village on his screen, at the church tower and the lane and the one-and-a-half seconds of a child who had looked up, and at the small closing bracket beneath it all, patient, kind, and final.

"No," he said. "Not yet. It got everyone to stop pretending they had longer."
