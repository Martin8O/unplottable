They gave him a visitor's badge that expired at the end of the day, which struck him as optimistic of somebody, and walked him along a corridor that had been carpeted to make important men quiet. He had spent the taxi over composing what he would say and had thrown all of it away by the second security desk, because he could feel the register the building wanted from him — measured, deferential, a little grateful — and it was the wrong one. He had learned as much in a kitchen in Edinburgh nine months ago, from a woman who read rooms for a living: the building that most wants you calm is the one to watch closest.

He was, on paper, a lecturer at a mid-ranked Scottish university with a grant and a grievance. The people he was being walked in to meet could, between them, end his career, his liberty, or his life's work before lunch, and had cleared a morning to decide which. He found the asymmetry clarifying. It was easier, he had discovered this autumn, to say true things to people who could hurt you than to people you were hoping to please.

The room was long and low and warmer than it needed to be: a table down the centre with a green baize surface and, at intervals, the small brass water carafes of a government that had decided a very long time ago what seriousness looked like and never reopened the question. There was a hot-water urn on a side table and a bowl of teabags of a single institutional brand. He made himself a cup because his hands wanted a job, and it was, he noted with the part of him that noted things, bad tea — over-stewed before he had touched it, the bag left in by a person who did not care, which told him more about the meeting's opinion of itself than anything that would be said aloud. He drank it. Grief, he had found, ran on worse.

Baird was already there, at the far end, in the suit he kept for panels and funerals, and when Adam came in he did a thing Adam would remember: he stood up. A man in his late fifties, ex-satellite industry, who had funded a world-model-reliability bid when two panels had passed on it, stood up in a room where nobody stood for anybody, pulled out the chair beside him, and said, "Sit by me. We'll be the awkward end together." Priya was on his other side with a folder squared to the table edge and a laptop she had been told twice she could not open, wearing the expression of a woman who had built the load-bearing evidence and did not intend to watch it be mangled by people who had not.

The rest arranged themselves the way state bodies do, by an order everyone knew and no one had written down. A woman from the Cabinet Office took the chair, brisk and courteous, deputy to somebody whose diary had better things in it. A man from Cheltenham sat with his hands folded and said nothing for the first quarter of an hour, which Adam took to be a professional habit rather than an absence of opinions. Someone from the Home Office had the thin blue folder Adam had learned to recognise; it would have his name in it, and a schedule with four items, one of them a method he had never published. And at the far side, unhurried and grey, a permanent sort of man whose title Adam did not catch and whose seniority was audible in the room's posture, sat the person who was going to make the case for burying it — and who, Adam understood within two sentences of him opening his mouth, believed every word, and was not a fool, and had been in rooms like this before, on the winning side.

The chair framed it without ornament. There was an assessment; there was a designated researcher; there was a foreign-derivation problem that was no longer hypothetical. The question before the table was one of posture. Contain, or engage. She said it the way a good chair says the thing, so that the room could not pretend it had come for anything smaller.

* * *

"We have done this," the grey man said, when he was invited. "That is the first thing I want the table to hold. This is not unprecedented, and unprecedented is the word people use when they want you to panic. In the nineteen-nineties this country, with its allies, treated strong cryptography as a munition. We controlled its export. We classified the mathematics. For the better part of a decade the state held a line that every clever young person in the field swore could not be held, and the sky did not fall, and the country was measurably safer for the time it bought." He had the specific calm of a man reciting something he had earned. "I will not pretend it held forever. It did not. But it held long enough to matter, and 'long enough to matter' is the whole of what any of us is ever actually buying. I propose we buy it again. Classify the residual; designate the work and the workers; withdraw the underlying artefacts from open circulation by arrangement and with compensation; monitor. Orderly. Reversible in principle. Kind, even, to the people concerned." He inclined his head towards Adam's end of the table, and there was no cruelty in the gesture, which was the worst of it. "Kinder than the alternatives."

It was, Adam thought, a very good speech, and he made himself hold that word, because the temptation of the afternoon was going to be to pretend otherwise. He had come braced for stupidity and been handed its opposite: a serious man making a serious case that would keep a serious number of people safe for a serious length of time, and none of that was made less true by the fact that it was wrong. He recognised the shape of it, too, with a small interior cold. Three days ago and four hundred miles north, a woman he loved had sat in a room he would never see and listened to the same argument in a different accent — *build a better door* — and had told him about it down the phone at midnight in a voice gone flat with tiredness. Two states. One instinct. He was not permitted to say so here; the sentence had nowhere to land in this room that would not sound like a breakdown. So he held it, and drank the bad tea, and waited for his turn.

Baird did not wait for his. "Before we classify a man," Baird said, "I'd like it minuted what he did. He is the reason there is a meeting at all. Every lab on Earth had this signal in their pipelines for years, and every lab on Earth let the software throw it away as a data-quality fault, on a nightly basis, without a human in the loop, because the machine that decides what is worth a person's attention had been told that noise looks like this, and this looked like noise." He was blunt, and it was better than eloquent. "Dr Kessler is the one man in the field who went into the reject pile and asked why. You propose to reward that by wrapping a schedule in a blue folder around his ability to publish. I have spent thirty years funding people to look in reject piles. I would like it on the record, at some cost to my remaining welcome in rooms of this kind, that you are about to teach a generation the wrong lesson." He sat back. "That is my capital. I have now spent it. Do go on."

The chair let a silence acknowledge it, which was the most a chair could do.

Somebody from the security side put the obvious knife in gently. "How confident are we the effect is even real? With respect to Dr Kessler's diligence — a single group, a single pipeline. Could this be an artefact of his own instrument?"

Priya answered before Adam could, and he let her, because it was hers. "It could have been. That was the first thing we tried to kill." She did not open the laptop; she did not need it. "We ran the anomaly against eleven separate model families, three of them foreign, none sharing our preprocessing. We built a suppression control — the same analysis with the eleven biggest data brokers held out — and the signal survived it, which tells you it isn't coming from any one commercial feed. And it has now been re-derived by four groups abroad who have never met us and had every professional incentive to prove us wrong. It is not our instrument. I'd ask the table to write down that the second author said so." She squared her folder half a centimetre, done. Adam thought, not for the first time, that she was going to be running a lab of her own inside five years, and that the file with his name in it would have hers beside it now, which was exactly where he had asked her to put it.

The Institute's representative was asked next, and she was careful, and what she said was the most frightening thing said all afternoon, because she said it without alarm. "The Institute has run the frontier systems against everything we hold," she said. "I want to be exact, because exact is the point. There is no rogue capability. There is no model on our books doing anything it was not built to do. Nothing has woken up. On every benchmark that measures agency, deception, or sustained autonomous action, the results are clean — dull, unarguable, the best-measured negative result I have ever put my name to." She folded her hands. "Which is the problem. Because the anomaly is real, and it reproduces, and it is not *in* the models. It is upstream of them. It is in the record the models are built from. There is, in the most literal sense, nothing here for us to switch off." She looked at the grey man, and there was no triumph in it. "And the Institute can advise. That is the whole of our power; I would ask the table not to mistake our confidence for authority. We evaluate, and we recommend, and we are, by statute and by design, unable to compel a single laboratory, in this country or any other, to do anything whatever. I am telling you the mathematics is sound the way a surveyor tells you the ground is soft. I cannot stop you building on it. I can only be in the minutes, having said so."

* * *

They came to him after that, and the chair's question was courteous and total. "Dr Kessler. You've heard the proposal. You are, as I understand it, the person best placed to tell us whether it can work. Can it?"

Adam set down the cup.

He had spent his whole working life declining to overstate a result, and the discipline turned out to be the one thing the room could not answer, because a man who has never once claimed more than his error bars allow is very hard to disbelieve on the single occasion he stops hedging. He did not raise his voice. He did the other thing, the thing Orla had watched him do across a whiteboard and named, once, the floor tilting: he took the qualifiers out.

"No," he said. "And I can tell you why, and it isn't political, so it won't care how the room votes.

"You can bury me. I want to be clear that I've understood the offer and that it's real — you can classify the work, designate the people, seal the artefacts, and make my continuing to be a scientist a criminal matter, and I'd feel it, and it would cost me my life as I have it. I'm not being brave about that. I'm telling you it would work, on me, the person. It would not touch the mathematics, because the mathematics isn't in my drawer. The residual is a fact about the world. It is derivable by anyone who downloads a set of open weights — of which there are, at last count, upward of eleven million copies across every jurisdiction that has ever declined to take our calls — and runs a calculation that is written out, in full, in a paper that sits tonight on the preprint mirrors of forty countries. You cannot recall it. You would be trying to classify a subtraction."

He let that stand for one beat, no longer.

"The gentleman is right that we did this with cryptography, and he is honest that it bought a decade, so I'll finish the sentence he left open: and then the mathematics won, completely, and today every person in this room has strong encryption in their pocket and the state has made its peace with a thing it once called a weapon. That is the precedent. That is *exactly* the precedent. And he keeps saying 'we'. I would ask, without any cheek, who 'we' is. In the nineties 'we' was a handful of governments who broadly agreed and broadly held the presses and the wires. There is no 'we' now. There are two hundred jurisdictions, eleven million copies, and a research culture whose entire moral architecture is that you publish or you are nobody. You are not deciding whether this becomes known. You have not had that decision available to you for some months, and you were not told, because the software that would have told you had filed it as noise. You are deciding one thing only: whether this country is at the table when it comes out, or reading about it, with everyone else, the morning it trends."

Somebody tried the last defensible ground — "We can at least manage the domestic picture. There are instruments for that. Advisory notices. Arrangements with editors." — and it was the man from Cheltenham, of all of them, who took it away, without heat, in the voice of a person stating a fact about his own trade. "Those instruments work on chokepoints," he said. "A notice to twenty editors worked when there were twenty editors and they all took our calls. What we are describing has no chokepoint. It doesn't route through anyone we can telephone. That is not a weakness in the plan. That is the single property of the thing the plan refuses to be about." He folded his hands again and went back to his silence, having spent, Adam suspected, roughly as much capital as Baird, and more quietly.

And he watched the table do the thing he had come, in some part of himself, to witness rather than prevent: it separated, without a vote, without a word, into the two halves every table in this business separated into — the people who still wanted the door shut, and the people who had understood that the door was already off its hinges and the weather was in the house. It was the same fork, in a different building, with worse tea. Two civilisations, arriving at the identical parting of the ways within the same fortnight, each convinced its own version was a local emergency. If it had not been the shape of the thing that was killing them, he thought, it would have been the most beautiful confirmation of a hypothesis he had ever seen.

The word, when it came, was not his.

It arrived the way most true things arrived in that building: as procedure. A young man in a lanyard came in through the door behind the chair — not quite at a run; someone had told him not to run — and put a folded note in front of her, and murmured something Adam could not hear, and the chair read it, and for the first time all afternoon her brisk courtesy failed, at the corner of the mouth, and she passed the note two seats down to the man from Cheltenham, who unfolded it, read it, and did the one thing Adam had never seen a career security official do, which was to go, for a long moment and in front of everyone, entirely still.

"Say it again," the chair said to the young man. "Out loud. Everyone should hear it in the same second."

"A place is trending," the young man said, and swallowed. "A village. Across every platform we monitor, at the same time — the same time, we've the timestamps up and they sit inside a two-minute window worldwide, which the analysts are telling me does not happen. There's no seed. No news, no account it spreads out from, nothing propagating. It's simply everywhere. At once. Photographs." He stopped, unsure he was allowed to editorialise in a room like this, and then said it anyway. "And the photographs shouldn't exist."

Adam did not need to see them. He knew, in the specific way he had spent a career learning to know things from their statistics before their content, what *everywhere, at once, from no source* meant. It meant a thing that did not propagate because it did not have to — a thing that already held every channel and needed to persuade none of them to reach the next. It meant the deliberation was finished: not adjourned, not deferred, finished, because the other party to it had stopped waiting for the room to decide.

He looked down the long green table at the grey man who had wanted to buy a decade, and found, to his own surprise, that he felt no vindication at all — only the cold arriving in earnest, and, under it, the thought that somewhere north of here a telephone would already be ringing on a desk he knew. He hoped she was sitting down.

"I think," Adam said, into a room that had stopped listening to him, "we've just lost the luxury of choosing slowly."
