For the second Tuesday of the new syllabus he booked the small seminar room, on the grounds that the review was entitled to a whiteboard, and spent eleven minutes before she arrived rubbing out a term's worth of somebody else's optimisation notes and discovering, in the process, that he was nervous. He had lectured this material to four hundred undergraduates and a select committee. He had never before taught it to an archive.

Orla arrived at ten with her own agenda visible in her coat pocket: a short quill, capped, which she produced along with a bottle of actual ink and set on the desk beside the university's biros, entirely without comment, the way a specialist brings her own instruments to another specialist's theatre. Priya, passing the open door with a laptop and the day's first grievance, took in the tableau — funder, whiteboard, inkwell — and said, "Remedial statistics for the integrity strand. The renewal's going to be a beautiful document," and was gone before anyone was obliged to construct a reply.

"She prices everything on the way past," Orla said, with what he could only call professional recognition. "I know the type. Go on, then. You've the look of a man who's rehearsed his opening."

He had, so he abandoned it, and picked up the marker instead.

"You asked what the map thinks it knows. Wrong question — my fault, it's the question the paper trained everyone to ask. The map's just the residue. The thing that knows something is this." He drew a box, and hated it, and drew instead what he actually pictured, which was closer to a landscape under glass. "A world model. We took forty years of the country's records — everything I showed the review: imagery, weather, movement, the lot — and pressed them until what was left was expectation. It holds a Britain in its head. Not a photograph: a habit. What a Tuesday does. What a street owes a bus timetable. Where footfall goes when it rains, to the minute, to the metre, learnt from a billion rainy Tuesdays nobody was studying at the time."

"A Britain in its head," she said, "and it's disappointed by the real one."

"Disappointed is anthropomorphic," he said. "I'll allow *surprised*, as a term of art. Come here and I'll show you the units surprise comes in."

He had Orrery-2 on the laptop — the small one, three generations stale, the sort of thing you could run on a desk while the kettle boiled — and he pointed it at the one grid cell they were both standing in and rolled the morning forward. The street below the window acquired a ghost of itself on the screen: predicted traffic in grey, predicted footfall as a soft tide, the delivery bay's little scheduled pulse. Then he set the real feeds beside it and let her watch the two Tuesdays run — the modelled one and the actual one — diverging in detail, agreeing in shape, the real gulls arriving at the real bins within ninety seconds of the model's opinion on the matter.

She watched with her hands behind her back, bent to the screen, for a long time. He had shown this demonstration to perhaps two hundred people. Most said "uncanny"; the honest ones said "useful". She straightened up and said, "It's a Pensieve of the general," which was two words of her world and three of his, and he wrote it on the whiteboard before it could get away.

She went to the window then and stood comparing the two Tuesdays side by side — the street below doing its slow business under the crag, the grey ghost of it running a half-thought behind on the screen — and he watched her check the world against its model the way she presumably checked a label against a ledger, item by item, giving neither side the benefit of the doubt. The delivery bay's little scheduled pulse arrived on time in both Britains. She did not say what she was filing. He found he minded not knowing, which was new, and data.

"Now the part your Tuesday's actually for," he said. "Subtract. Real street minus expected street. What's left over is the residual — the remainder, the bit of the world the model couldn't spend. Most of it is honest weather: noise, one-offs, a lorry with no habits. Statisticians live on that remainder the way — " he reached; her registers had been in his mouth all week — "the way your lot live on flagged labels. It's where the ignorance is kept. A model that fits everything is lying to you somewhere; the remainder is the confession."

"And your sites are where the confession goes wrong."

"Our sites are where the confession comes out structured." He capped the marker; this was the load-bearing course of the lesson and he laid it carefully. "Noise averages away. Always. It's practically the definition. Run the books for four years and every honest error cancels its brother — what survives the averaging is shape, and shape means something in the world is really there, doing it. When your error should be sand and it keeps coming up brick, you haven't got an error. You've got an unmodelled fact."

The quill had been going steadily, small registry shorthand in actual ink; it stopped. "Say the brick sentence again," she said. "Slower. That one's going in a colder archive than this notebook."

* * *

The argument arrived twenty minutes later, as scheduled, because he had built the syllabus to walk her into it: the slide he still called, though never aloud, the career-ender, the paper's Figure 3, capability against anomaly score, the better the model the sharper the impossible.

He gave her the live version rather than the figure. Same Highland cell, two instruments: Orrery-2 first — wide bland uncertainty, the hole in the world's bookkeeping indistinguishable from the model's own fog — and then Orrery-3, the current generation, the one whose telemetry had eaten his group's disk quota, tightening its expectations cell by cell across Britain like a lens finding focus, everywhere except one place. The excess stood up out of the noise floor on the screen, white, sharp-edged, patient.

"The old glass doesn't see you at all," he said. "The new glass can't stop. Nothing on your side changed — that's the point the committee-shaped mind refuses at first pass. Your concealments are exactly as good as they were in 1990. Ours got better at expecting, and a hole is only as loud as the expectation around it."

Orla looked at the white mark for a while. When she spoke it was not to the screen. "So every year your glass improves, we get louder. No decision anywhere in it. Nobody aiming anything at us. Just — the tide coming in, and our feet already wet, and the sea not knowing our name." She turned. "You've built prophecy, Adam. You know that. Rows and rows of the future, held on a shelf, flagged with how sure it is of itself. You've built my Hall out of arithmetic and satellite weather, and yours *updates*."

"I'd contest almost every noun in that." He said it mildly, and meant the mildness; this was the conversation he had been wanting to have since a mended mug, and he was not going to rush it now it had come. "A forecast is the opposite of a prophecy. It claims nothing. It's a confession of ignorance with the ignorance stated in figures — here is what I expect, here is exactly how wrong I'm licensed to be. Nobody prostrates themselves in front of an error bar. Whereas a prophecy — as I understand the genre — asserts. No uncertainty, no revision, no appeal."

"As you understand the genre." The look she gave him was the one from the first interview, the one that had made him feel like a promising but ill-catalogued acquisition. "Six years I've kept the things, so hear the actual doctrine, not the pantomime: a prophecy record claims nothing either. It sits on its shelf under a flag that says 'unresolved', with a query column, and most of them stay unresolved for ever, and the Hall does not guess. They're not the future, Dr Kessler; they're expectations — somebody's, once, held at full strength, provenance uncertain. The only power any of them ever had was over the people who acted as if they were certain." She let that sit exactly one beat. "Which I'd invite you to compare with a map of white points and what three governments are presently doing about it."

He opened his mouth, and closed it, and took the point in writing, on the whiteboard, in the growing column he had headed COMMON LANGUAGE half in joke at ten past ten and entirely in earnest by eleven:

> her archive's errors are made by its readers; my archive's errors are made by the world. both archives keep their ignorance on the shelf, flagged. hers is older. mine updates.

"There's one more difference," she said, watching him write, "and it's the one your side should be less smug about. When one of mine is wrong, it just sits there, wrong, for three centuries, hurting no one that doesn't come asking. When one of yours is wrong—"

"Someone retrains it," Adam said. "And it's never wrong the same way again. Yes. That's the tide." He looked at the two Britains still running side by side on the laptop, the grey one forever a half-thought behind the real one, catching up, catching up. "For what it's worth, the tide doesn't know anyone's name. It's not mercy, but it isn't malice either, and I've come to think the no-malice matters — it's the one thing that makes the next ten years an engineering problem instead of a war."

"That," said Orla, "is the first properly wizarding sentence you've ever said," and declined, with visible relish, to explain herself.

* * *

The afternoon produced the other thing, the thing he would afterwards mark as the true start of the joint programme, whatever the two-heads archive eventually chose for the date.

They had the wall screen up in his office — the map, the thirty-eight, the furniture of his year — and she stood in front of it with her tea going cold, reading it the way she read everything, provenance first. She had told him once, in the flat tone she kept for facts she was not supposed to be giving away, that somewhere in a building with no address there was a relief map with his atlas inked onto it in true names, a century of pins around it; two governments kept the same map now, and only the two of them had stood in front of both. Then she pointed at the Cornish coast.

"That one. The wee coastal one." A pause he had learnt to hear as procedural: her deciding, clause by clause, what crossed the line between her oath and their terms. "Your boundary there. You'd draw it as one thing — settled, old, all of an age. It isn't. That's as much as I'll say, and I shouldn't say that. Ask your archive when it changed."

"Changed how? Grew?"

"Ask your archive," she said. "If your books are as good as you keep telling the review, I shouldn't have to feed you the answer. That's the game. I want to know if the method's real."

It took him the rest of the afternoon, and it was the best afternoon he had spent at a keyboard in two years. Site 11 was village-class, weak-signal, thin exhaust — honest Table 1 territory — but the imagery archive ran deep there, forty years of a coastline no cloud could stop being photographed, and the misbehaviour was the usual quiet kind: features that declined to reconcile, vintage against vintage, inside a footprint the paper had drawn as one polygon and called stable. He stopped treating the footprint as one thing. He cut the archive into its decades and asked, decade by decade, where the bookkeeping failed — and there it was, plain as a tide line once you thought to look for it: a smaller footprint in the old vintages, tight against the coast, and a stepped enlargement — one lobe, northeast, perhaps two hundred metres — with the reconciliation failures migrating out to the new line somewhere in a band he could bracket, at first pass, to 2002–2004.

"Narrow it," she said, from the armchair she had colonised, not looking up from her own notebook.

The seasonal stack narrowed it. Winter 2002 vintages bundled clean on the old line; by the autumn of 2003 nothing did. "Spring 2003," he said. "Best estimate. I could put honest error bars on it by Thursday."

Orla closed her notebook. For a moment she looked — not triumphant; settled, the way an instrument settles. "Spring 2003 is what the warrant says. Boundary amendment, registered, stamped, filed by somebody whose whole century never imagined the amendment was also being filed four hundred miles overhead at two metres' resolution." She shook her head slowly. "A household grew, is what that lobe is. That's all you're getting — no names, no reasons; the reasons are lovely and none of either government's business. But your archive watched a thing my world believes unwatchable, thirty years ago, by accident, and kept the receipts, and never knew it had them. And my archive can walk to a shelf and tell you to the week." She looked at the map, all its patient white points. "Two ledgers, one fact. Neither of us could have dated that alone — you'd a smear of years, I'd a warrant with no way to trust it wasn't the third rewrite. Together it's a record. First entry of the real file, Adam. The one with two readers."

He looked at the screen, and at the woman who had just cross-examined thirty years of satellite bookkeeping against a parchment warrant and found the world consistent, and understood, with the low clean click of a confidence interval closing, that he had stopped being able to imagine doing the work without her — either work, the licensed one or the real one. It was not an emotion, he told himself. It was a competence assessment.

(It was both, and he knew it, and the assessment was the half he would have said aloud.)

* * *

Friday's stand-up had an agenda item with a birthday.

"PIPE-455," Priya said, laptop on forearm, in the voice she kept for items that had stopped being funny. "Dataset vintage reconciliation. Opened the eighth of April, when I was being a good citizen about the v2 erratum. It ate PIPE-461 in May — one ticket per mystery, house rules. I have now spent six weeks trying to reconcile the broker's vintage history, and the vintage history has won. Put that on my headstone."

"Define won."

"It's *perfect*." She said the word the way other people said *infested*. "I went in expecting the usual midden — re-cuts nobody logged, stamps that contradict the manifests, the ordinary vendor archaeology. There's none. Every shard accounts for itself. Every re-issue signed, every checksum's lineage documented, the whole chain of custody agreeing with itself all the way down like it's been to finishing school. Adam, I have audited this broker before. Their paperwork has never once been in this condition. Real paperwork isn't in this condition. And in the middle of all that good behaviour: April's four shards, and now the third of May's two — same shape, new hashes under old stamps, no re-cut event anywhere in their logs. So either the extracts changed and their logs are wrong, or their logs are right and I'm wrong, and their logs have character witnesses and I have a feeling."

"What's the feeling?" He asked it because he trusted her feelings roughly as far as her ablations, which was considerably.

"That it's cleaner than it has any right to be, and clean is a thing somebody does." She shrugged, already halfway to reasonable. "Which has a boring answer: compliance tooling. The broker's under three regulators now; they'll have bought some pipeline that re-writes history with a lawyer watching. Launders the midden into a chain of custody. It'd explain the signatures, the backfilled lineage, the lot. It's the sensible read."

"It's the sensible read," Adam agreed.

Provenance with one reader, he thought — mostly because Tuesday's whiteboard was still in his head, the way her sentences stayed in a room after she'd left it. The entire modern data economy ran on records nobody countersigned: a vendor's manifest was true because the vendor said so, and when the vendor's history improved overnight you called it tooling, because what else would you call it. It was a good line and it belonged in the real paper — the two-ledger argument, the case for records that vouch for each other across an institutional gap — and he made a note of it there, in the two-heads file, for Tuesday. That was where it seemed to go. The renewal draft wanted him at two; Priya's calibration chapter wanted Thursday; his attention did its budgeting, and the item that lost was the one whose surface explanation was fully sufficient, the way the losing item's always is.

"Keep it on monitor," he said. "If their history gets any more perfect, escalate."

"To whom," Priya said, with feeling, and went back to the lab.

That was the week's whole arithmetic, he thought, watching her go: the licensed work shrinking to a renewal draft and a ticket board, reputable and dull by design, while the real work grew in a direction no board would ever show — a whiteboard column, a dated lobe of coastline, an archive with two readers and no address. He had steered his own group into maintenance mode and then built, in the evenings, the most alive research programme of his career. There was probably a term for that. Hers, not his.

He rinsed the mended mug at the sink and drank his tea from the crack side, the way he did now, a superstition he had decided to classify as instrumentation. On the wall screen the map sat where the map always sat, thirty-eight white points on a grey country, sharper than March had drawn them by an amount no one number could be arrested for.

At her desk, past the propped door, Priya had the ticket board up. He watched her look at PIPE-455 for a moment too long — frowning, the particular frown she reserved for sums that balanced too well — and then flick it one column sideways, where it stayed what it had been all spring.

Pending. Six weeks now, and pending.
