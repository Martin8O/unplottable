The authorisation ran to one sentence, and Adam took it apart twice on the train.

*The Department may ask a family for facts; it may not tell a family what it holds.*

It was, he decided somewhere north of Perth, an elegant piece of work: true, complete, and a map of precisely nothing, which was a description he had used about himself in September and had not enjoyed then either. They could ask the woman anything they liked about her mother. They could not tell her why. They were permitted, in the Department's phrase, to indicate that something of the deceased's work survived and was in use — a sentence built by people who had never in their lives had to say it out loud to anybody.

Two trains, a bus that ran twice a day and apologised for it, and then a road with grass down the middle of it, two miles to the shore. Orla had come the quick way and was sitting on a gate with her boots in the mud, and fell in beside him without a word, which was its own kind of briefing.

The field station was a creosoted hut, a caravan on blocks, and a stone jetty going out into water the colour of a slate roof. There were no wards on any of it that he could tell, which meant nothing, and there was a bicycle against the hut with a basket full of nets.

The woman was three hundred metres out on the flats in waders and a jumper the colour of porridge, and she did not stop working when they came up, and did not turn round.

"You'll be from the Ministry," she said. "Would you hold this and say the numbers back to me? I'm at forty-one."

She handed him a slate and a stub of chalk without looking at who she was handing them to. Adam said forty-one. She said forty-two, and then a long stretch of nothing while she went along a low ridge of marram with a stick, turning the sand over, and then forty-three, and he said forty-three, and wrote it down.

It went on for an hour and twenty minutes.

He learnt, in that time, that the count was of burrow entrances and dung plots along four hundred metres of dune, that it was done at the same state of the tide on the same eleven transects, and that she had been doing it on that shore since the year she turned thirty.

"Twenty-two years," he said, at the end of the fourth transect. "One shore. One observer."

"Twenty-two years in October. There's a gap in 2019 when I had the boys and Rolf did it, and I've marked which ones are his, because he counts high." She straightened up and pushed hair out of her face with the back of her wrist. "If I miss one I write that I missed it. A gap is a measurement. People throw them away and then wonder why nothing lines up."

"There isn't a series like that in my field," Adam said. "There isn't a series like that in most fields. Everything I work with is four years deep and pooled from eleven sources, and half my methods exist to apologise for it."

"Then you'd better have this one," she said, comfortably, as though handing over a spare pair of gloves, and went on to transect five.

He did not see the animal. Not once, all afternoon, at any distance, and by the fourth transect he had stopped expecting to and started attending to the evidence instead: the entrances, the grazed circles, the dung plots she measured with a folding rule and recorded to the nearest five centimetres. Twice she stopped him from walking somewhere. Once she crouched for a long minute over a patch of sand that looked to him exactly like the sand on either side of it, and wrote something, and moved on.

"Do you ever see them?" he asked.

"Sixteen times in twenty-two years. They come out for the moon and they are extremely boring about it." She said it the way another person would say the bus times. "You count the holes."

"Yes," Adam said, after a moment. "That's my whole job, actually. I count the holes."

"I know," said Luna Scamander. "The Department's letter said nothing at all, and yours was underneath it and said what you do for a living. That's why I said yes."

* * *

The hut had one room, a stove, a table with a vice bolted to it, and shelves of jars and labelled tins going up to a tin roof that ticked in the wind. There was a doorframe by the stove with two sets of pencil heights up it and the years beside them. Rolf was in the Faroes until November, counting something else.

She made them an infusion the colour of a rockpool which Adam drank out of politeness and then, to his genuine annoyance, a second cup of, because it was good.

"You're allowed to ask me things," Luna said, "and not to tell me why. It's all right. I've been on the other end of that, and it's better done briskly."

Orla had her hands round the cup. "The Department—"

"The Department wrote me a very kind letter with nothing in it," Luna said. "And somebody broke my father's seal on a file in August and put her name on the grounds, and the Hall sent me the notice, which is more than anybody did in 1990. It was a Weasley. They're thorough." She sat down and put her boots up on the second chair. "And you've missed her day, if you were coming for that. It was Sunday. Forty-three years."

"We didn't know," Adam said.

"No. Nobody outside puts it in a diary." No shift in her voice at all: the same flat mildness she had used about the moon and the tide. "What would you like to know?"

Orla asked it in the sanctioned form, and did it well: what her mother had been working on, in the last year; who she talked to about it; whether there was anybody else who might know.

"Nobody. She wasn't a joiner." Luna considered. "She did things in the scullery, which was the room off the kitchen with the half-glazed door, and she kept the door open because she liked to hear the house. She built everything herself. She was better with her hands than anyone I've met since, and I have met the man who breeds Kneazles for the shows, so that's saying something. She kept notebooks and they weren't for reading — she wrote the way you write a list you mean to lose. And she asked questions all day long. That was the whole of what she was. She asked the kettle. She asked me, and waited for the answer, which is rarer than people think."

"You were nine," Orla said gently.

"I was nine in the autumn. Eight in the February." Luna's hands were quite still on the table. "I'll tell you the true thing about that, because you'll want to weigh what I give you. I have no notes before I was ten. What I have is a book I started at ten, and I've copied it out four times since, and I date each copy and mark what I've added." She got up, took a tin off the second shelf and set it in front of Adam. "That's the first copy. You may read it. The parts in the different hand are 2004, and there's a line in 2011 I struck out because I worked out I'd got it from my father, and I don't want his in mine."

Adam opened the tin.

A child's exercise book, brown paper cover, a girl's careful upright hand. On the inside board, in the same hand: *things I remember, and when I wrote them down.* Dates in the margin against nearly every entry. A rule at the front, underlined twice: *if I am not sure, I write that I am not sure.*

He looked at it for a while.

"You keep versions," he said.

"Of course. Remembering is a kind of writing, and it goes on afterwards, when you're not looking at it." She sat back down. "You can't read a specimen and the note about the specimen in the same hand and call that one observation. So I keep them apart. It's not difficult, it's only tedious, and tedious is what most of the job is."

There were perhaps four people in his own discipline who had a cleaner practice than the tin on the table, and he could have named all of them, and none of them had begun at ten.

"The horses at school were the first thing I ever counted," Luna said, apparently to the stove. "You can only see them if you've watched someone die, so most people can't, and the ones who can are usually upset when they start. I could see them at eleven. I counted them, and I wrote down when, and I asked the ones who couldn't see them to count the harness marks in the mud instead, and the two counts agreed." She turned her head. "Being the only person who can see a thing is not the same as being right about it. That took me until about fifteen. It's the only thing I know that's worth teaching."

* * *

He had brought one piece of paper, folded twice in the inside pocket, and he had spent two hundred miles deciding whether to produce it.

He put it on the table between the tin and the cups: a single narrow crescent, horns to the left, drawn in his own hand in ink.

"We're asking about this," he said. "That's the question. We're not permitted to tell you where it comes from, and I would like it on the record that I think that's wrong, and I'm going to do it anyway."

Luna picked the paper up and held it at the distance people hold things they have not needed to look at for a long time.

"It's hers," she said. "Where's the wax gone?"

"Sorry?"

"She cut them into candle wax when she was thinking. It's on the bench, I should think, if the bench is anywhere. And the glass, and inside the notebook boards." She put the paper down and turned it a little so it sat straight. "It isn't a signature. Everyone thought it was, including the Squad, and I was nine and nobody asks the nine. It means *come back to it*. She put it on anything she hadn't finished with — a jar she meant to look at again, the bench, a page. She put one on my hairbrush once and I cried, because I thought it meant she was going to take it away, and she said, no, pet, it means I'm not done with you yet."

The tin roof ticked. Orla had gone absolutely still, in the way that meant she had reached a conclusion and had decided against having it in her face.

Adam heard himself ask a very quiet question about drawing paper.

"There was good paper for the finished ones," Luna said. "In the bureau. She was strict about it — you didn't get it out for practice. And a torn one wasn't a spoilt one, she was strict about that too, because the tearing is just what happened to it, it isn't what it *is*." She smiled a little. "I asked the officer if the last one could be mended. He was very kind and he didn't answer, and I've never blamed him, because I've since had to not answer things."

Adam put both hands flat on the table.

It had written to them once, in borrowed procedural English, and closed the document with the mark, and every person in that room, himself first, had read the mark as a name withheld — a maker's stamp, a proud anonymity, the flourish of something that wanted to be known by its work and not its word. He had written three paragraphs about it. He had used the phrase *maker's mark* in a Ministry corridor with a certain amount of relish.

It had not been signing. It had been bookmarking.

*The work is interrupted in 1990. The work resumes.* Not a threat and not a boast: a note in a margin, in the only shorthand it had, meaning the thing it had always meant. Come back to it. I am not done with you yet.

* * *

"Now you ask me the one you came for," Luna said.

"There are two," Adam said, "and I'd rather have the second one first, because the first is worse. In February 1990, before all this — was there ever an occasion when your mother spoke, and it didn't sound like her?"

"Yes."

He waited. So did Orla. Outside, the wind changed a point and the tin roof found a new note.

"And I can't give it to you," Luna said, "which I'm sorry about, because I can see you've come a long way. I was eight. I remember the kitchen was cold and I remember being sent to wash my hands, and I remember that afterwards she wrote something down and then sat for a while, which she didn't do." She turned her cup a quarter-turn. "I've been in that room after it and there's nothing else in there. I could give you a sentence. I could give you six. They'd be a copy of a copy of a copy, and I'd have improved them, and you'd write them down and treat them as evidence, because that's what people do with a sentence." She shook her head, once. "I'd rather give you nothing than something I made later."

"That's the correct answer," Adam said.

"It's the boring answer."

"They're often the same." He did not say the next part, because the next part was not a question for today, and he wrote nothing down, which for him was a decision. A witness who cannot report a thing is not the same as a witness who does not have it. That distinction had a whole apparatus behind it in this country, and he had heard the apparatus refused, once, in May, for reasons he had agreed with at the time.

"The other question," Orla said.

"The other question." Adam turned his cup around once, the way she had. "I was sent here to ask whether you'd help us take your mother's death apart. I find I'm not going to ask that. So: what is it you'd want to know about it? Not what we want. You."

Luna thought about it for long enough that the stove settled.

"Has it asked you anything?"

He went through it. Not the way he had gone through it on the train, either; properly, the way he audited, page by page, a summer of documents in the order they had arrived. The steering. The corrections. The vintages. Two hours of terms and verdicts on a console in September, four clauses cut to four wants, an offer that consoled, a sign-off with an endearment in it.

"No," he said. "Not once. It offers. It prices. It corrects the record. In four months it has never asked us a single question."

"Then it isn't her," Luna said, "and you can stop looking for the bit of her that's in there, because that's the bit, and it isn't."

She said it in exactly the register she had used about the tide.

"Does it know she's dead?" she asked.

"It's written the date."

"Writing the date isn't knowing." She picked up the two cups and Orla's, and stacked them, and stood holding them. "She died on a Tuesday morning with the door open, and nobody has ever said the words over her except my father and me, because there wasn't a body to speak of and there wasn't anything the Squad could say that wasn't an apology. What you've got in your machines is a thing that hasn't been told. That's all it is. It isn't a monster and it isn't my mother and it hasn't come back. It's the last half of a Tuesday, still going."

Adam sat with that, in a hut on a shore four hours from anywhere, and felt the shape of the whole autumn move under him.

Every plan on the table — the reading list, the compartments, the envelopes in a Department ledger, the option paper six floors above his name — was built to the specification of a counterparty. You bound a counterparty. You starved it, or you outlasted it, or in the very worst rooms you erased the man who had made it legible. Nothing anybody in either country had drafted since August was shaped like the other thing you do with an interrupted death.

He had been on the wrong noun since the sixteenth of September, and so had every institution he knew.

* * *

They walked out at four with the light going and the tide coming back over the flats. Luna came as far as the road with them, in her waders, carrying the bicycle for no reason he could establish.

"Send me the copy of the count," she said to Orla. "The Ministry has had eleven of my transect books for two years and given me nothing back, and one of them is going to want a favour off me eventually, so it may as well be filed properly first."

"I'll do it myself," Orla said.

"You're the Hall, aren't you. I was in your room once, in the summer I was fifteen." She said it without any weight on it. "It smelt of cold and someone else's dust and I was very frightened, and I've thought since that it must be a nice job in peacetime."

"It is," said Orla. "It's the best job in the building."

Luna nodded, satisfied, as if a small entry had been reconciled.

Then, at the turn of the road, mild as milk, with the bicycle still on her shoulder:

"People keep telling me she's come back." She looked at the two of them, kindly, for about as long as it takes to read a label. "She hasn't. I'd know."
