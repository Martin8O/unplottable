Number ninety-three announced itself from fifty yards, the way it had since before she was born: a shopfront conducting itself like a firework that had decided, against type, to hold a steady job. The window this June was a pyramid of Shield Hats under a banner reading WORRIED? WEAR SOMETHING FUNNIER, and the Saturday crowd around the door was three parts children and one part adults pretending to be brought there by children, and Orla stood across the lane a moment in the sun and let the shop do her heart good, which was what it was for.

Inside was weather of its own. Somewhere at the back a Potter forehead was visible above the testing-pen barrier, on fire in a supervised way; a witch at the Pygmy Puff terraces was losing a negotiation with a six-year-old; and down the centre aisle, sleeves rolled, came the operations half of the firm, carrying a crate of something that ticked.

"Orla Quinn," Ron Weasley said, with the delight of a man whose whole trade was recognising people at volume. "Rosie's one. The dust-and-secrets one. Don't tell me the Ministry's finally buying something useful."

"I'm buying for Rose. It's her birthday on Sunday, and I've had a grand catalogue of bad ideas and no good ones."

"Birthday. Right." He set the ticking crate down with the tenderness other men reserved for infants. "Well, you've come to the man. Rule one for our Rose: nothing that does the thing for you. She'd have a Daydream Charm dismantled by teatime to see if it flattered her, and it would, and she'd want a refund on principle." He was moving as he talked, the patter running ahead of him down the aisle. "Rule two: it has to be something she can win. Same as when she was six. Her mum lectures, her brother sulks, our Rosie prices. You know she kept a book on whether I'd cry at her graduation? Paid out against herself, and all. Proudest I've ever been, losing that bet."

He took her the long way to the counter, on the grounds that there was no short way and the long way passed the new stock: a shelf of Decoy Detonators in this year's livery ("they apologise now, before they go off — George's idea, he says it's funnier, and Merlin help me he's right"), the Daydream range in its dignified plum boxes, a bin of something marked REDUCED: SENTIENT-ISH. George did the ideas floor upstairs, he explained, and he did gravity: stock, staff, the till, the seventeen daily miracles of a shop that had never once behaved like a business and never once missed rent. "Thirty-five years I've been the boring half," Ron said, with the satisfaction of a man who had spent those years being nothing of the kind. "Somebody has to hold the ladder."

He stopped at a shelf below the counter and came up with a squat volume bound in racing green, and there was a new note under the patter, pride of a different vintage.

"The Punter's Friend. Self-totting book of wagers. Remembers every bet you enter, keeps the running score, red ink for welchers — polite about it, mind, we're not animals. New line this spring." He turned it over in his hands once. "Fred's idea, originally. Never built it — said the margin was in never paying out." He said it the way the shop said everything, which was at full volume with the door open; the shop had had thirty-five years of practice at carrying its founder.

"It's perfect," Orla said, and meant it flat and full, so that Ron looked for a moment like a man who had been paid a compliment by a building inspector.

At the till he rang up two Shield Hats for a customer ahead of her — a witch in travelling clothes who wanted three, then changed it to four, then said, "Family size," to nobody — and when the door had shut he leaned on the counter and let the patter drop a floor.

"You know what's moving this quarter? That." A nod at the window pyramid. "Shield line. Best quarter since the war, and I hate every Galleon of it. There's a fella comes in Tuesdays now — Wizengamot robes, buys nothing, leaves pamphlets. *Finish the Work*, they say, with a drawbridge on. I bin them. He knows I bin them. We've an understanding." He straightened a display that did not need straightening. "Percy says Macmillan's lot had forty owls a day in April and it's four hundred now. Week before last I'd two warlocks nearly at wands by the Puffs — over a motion, in my shop. Sold them both something and put them out different doors." He shrugged, and the sincerity arrived the way Rose had once told her it always did in her father, clumsy, sideways, unmistakable: "Rosie says your lot'll sort it. Well — she says nobody will, but that you'll be right about how, which from her is a marriage proposal. Family price on the book."

"Ron—"

"Family price, or I call James over to demonstrate the new Detonators at you. He's been wanting a volunteer with reflexes."

She paid the family price, which appeared to be the cost of the wrapping, and left with the parcel under her arm and the shop's noise following her out into the lane like a hand waved from a door.

* * *

The Ministry canteen believed in gravy. It was Wednesday, and Teddy Lupin had queued for both of them, paid before she could reach her purse, and steered them to a corner table with the ease of a man who de-escalated for a living and had long ago stopped noticing he was doing it to furniture as well.

"Vic's on nights all month," he said, unloading the tray. "I'm learning to cook for one and the one keeps refusing to eat it. How's your da's side of the world? Still wiring houses for people who think the lights work by magic?"

"He'd say the same of this building, and he'd be righter than he knows."

"He would." Teddy attacked the pie. Three months of the working party had put a tan on one side of his face — "we've walked more of Britain since March than I'd managed in a career; I'm brown to the west and Ministry-coloured to the east, Vic says I'm a half-timbered house" — and he told the one about the rambler in the Fens, three weeks back: a birdwatcher with a thermos, camped on the wrong side of a line he could not see, deflected twice by the charm and twice come back, "which happens — some people's pressing engagements just don't press." So Teddy had gone out himself and sat down next to the man and admired the reed buntings for half an hour until the fellow decided, all on his own, that the light was better further east. "Not a wand drawn. Best work I did all month, and it goes in no file." He said it like a joke; it was, she understood, the whole doctrine of his office in one anecdote, and the gentlest version of it she would ever be shown. The talk ran on through survey gossip: the reinforcement rota, a crew stood down in the Fens for a fortnight's leave, the Committee's excuse queue at its shortest since winter, everyone's paperwork immaculate the way it only ever was when nobody knew what to do. It was good company. It was always good company; that was the thing about the far side of the floor she had chosen against, that it was staffed by the best people she knew.

Then, over the pudding, without any change she could have pointed to except that the ease went out of his shoulders and into storage: "Someone with a caucus behind them wrote to Headquarters last week. Wanted us to cost a piece of work I'm not going to name over sponge." He stirred his tea. His hair, which had been its steady strong-tea brown all lunch, went one shade darker, like a kettle deciding. "I sent back a number with enough noughts in it to end the conversation. It didn't end the conversation."

"Teddy—"

"Assessor." And there it was, the lamp covered, the manner gone off like a light switched at the wall; the whole floor knew the rule, and she watched it happen across a canteen table with a spoon in his hand. "One working question, and then we'll have the rest of lunch back. On the day your line fills in, the docket comes to me. Fifty-two names, kept warm and idle three months on the strength of your blank — and I'll tell you what I've never told an assessor, which is that the machine doesn't idle once it's fed. It doesn't reconsider. It's built not to; I helped build it not to. So whatever the true speed of your judgement is—" he set the spoon down "—be slower. I'm good at what I'd be sent to do. I'd rather not find out how good."

"That's not pressure, then," she said, when she could.

"It's the opposite of pressure. It's the only favour my office has in stock." And the lamp came back on, all at once, and he was Teddy again, godson of the Head Auror, husband of a Healer who worked too many nights, thief of her last roast potato. "Right. Tell me your brother's still doing the thing with the insurance numbers, because I dined out on that for a month."

She walked back to the lifts afterwards loving him, and cataloguing on the way, because her hands wanted a list and there was no parchment in reach. Item: what Teddy knew — nothing. Item: what Teddy feared — the same thing she feared, from the opposite bank of it. Item: what Teddy had asked for — slowness, which she was already committing in quantities that would hang her. Item: the axe had bought lunch, and asked after her da, and hoped aloud never to be needed, and the dread of it was not that the machine was cruel. The dread was that it was kind, and thorough, and hers.

* * *

By Friday the week had earned an hour off, and she took it in the wrong world.

Adam had come south for Meridian — summoned, he said, to help Baird be friendly at a speed Baird chose, which had turned out to mean a morning of drafting one paragraph that promised a calibration appendix and committed to nothing, "the most expensive paragraph of my career; Baird read it four times and took a comma out the way you'd defuse something" — and they walked it off in St James's Park, along the lake, where the pelicans conducted themselves like a committee that had already eaten.

"He's good, your Baird," she said. "That's registry drafting, that is. The comma too."

"He's spent thirty years being funded by people he doesn't trust. It's given him a prose style." He had his sleeves rolled; June was performing summer over the water. "The Institute wants to visit. Baird's answer establishes that visits happen in the autumn. I have therefore been given a summer, and I find I don't know what the unit of a summer is any more. It used to be a conference deadline."

"It's this," Orla said. "The unit of a summer is an hour like this one, and you bank as many as you're let."

"And Wednesday?" he asked. "The lunch. The man whose job is me."

"He paid, and he asked after my da, and he hoped out loud that he never has cause to meet you, and he meant it as a blessing." The pelicans reorganised themselves around a point of order. "He asked me to be slower than my judgement. I told him nothing at all, which took the whole of my training, and he bought me pudding for it."

"I find I like him," Adam said. "That's the persistent methodological problem with your Ministry. I keep liking it."

He looked at her then instead of the pelicans, which she noticed, because noticing was the trade, and he did not look away when noticed, which was new, and data, and none of the registry's business. They had stopped walking somewhere under a plane tree without either of them proposing it. The week was full of knives and the two of them were the one appointment in it that had never needed a cover story — no, that was wrong, they had been a cover story from the first Tuesday; the thing that had no cover was this hour, off the terms, off both ledgers, banked in no archive but hers. He was close enough that she could see the exact place where his calibration ran out, the confidence interval narrowing with every second neither of them spent, and for once in her adult life she was not counting anything, not one thing, and she leaned—

An owl took the plane tree like a landing accusation.

It came down through the leaves with the entitled clatter of the Ministry's own post, and dropped, and what it dropped unfolded itself mid-air into a violet paper plane that circled once and snapped into her hand, quivering with self-importance. Nobody along the whole bright length of the lake looked up. Nobody in London ever looked up; it was the city's one great courtesy.

> DEPARTMENT OF MYSTERIES — OFFICE OF THE HEAD
> Miss Quinn: the Wizengamot brief. The Department's contribution is wanted Monday, nine o'clock, my office. Level Two requests it be stated "with every pause we can afford."
> Bring the pauses. — S.C.

"Your Ministry," Adam said, deadpan, from a distance that had restored itself to regulation, "has excellent timing. Somebody should audit it."

"Somebody is. You've no idea the paperwork." She folded the plane into her pocket with the rest of the week. Neither of them pretended the tree had not happened; that was the terms, she supposed — no records, and no lying — and the second clause was turning out much the dearer. "Monday, nine o'clock, I explain to the state how to be frightened of the past. Bank the hour, Dr Kessler. I'm told it's the unit."

* * *

The Hall was doing its winter as usual, and Rose was sitting on the clear end of the registry desk with her heels not quite swinging, which on Rose was a flag at half-mast, and a single page lay squared on the ledger, waiting, the way evidence waits.

"I've a confession," Rose said, by way of hello. "I reopened a closed book."

Orla set the wrapped parcel down next to the page and did not look at either. Six years of the registry held her hands still for her. "Officially closed, you told me. Tuesday before last but five."

"In ink, closed. I reopened it in pencil, which my section would tell you is a different epistemic act." She turned the page around with one finger, so that the columns faced its subject, and her voice stayed exactly at the register of the four-Sickle bet, which was the terrifying thing about it. "You're the provenance witch. Read the provenance."

> O.Q. — private book. Closed in ink, 10 May.
> assessment concludes by Easter — withdrawn, 22 Apr
> assessment concludes at all — withdrawn, 3 May
> the assessor is compromised — never offered
> the assessor is loyal to both — evens, 26 Apr; 1–2 on, 10 May
> — reopened in pencil, 27 May —
> the assessor has met the finders — 1–3 on, 27 May
> the finders know about us — evens, 10 June
> she'd tell me, if I asked her properly — this page

She read it twice, the way the week had taught her important documents deserved, and the last line sat there being the whole of it: not a trap, never a trap; a price, published, the way her friend had published every price since she was eleven years old. The room was very cold and very quiet and the shelves went away into the dark in both directions, three centuries of other people's held breath, and Orla put the craft down. It was heavier than it looked. She had been carrying it since March.

"Ask me properly."

"Are you running the assessment for the other side?"

"There isn't another side. That's the finding." The words came out plain, one at a time, nothing in them but themselves. "But yes."

"Is it treason?"

"By the book I serve? Yes."

"Is it right?"

"I think so. I check every Tuesday."

Rose nodded, once and then once more, the way she nodded at a sum that had come out the same twice. "Then it's kept," she said. "And I want to be clear what this is, because Mum's trade runs on people being unclear at exactly this point. I'm not selling you silence. Silence is free; you're my friend; I priced that when I was eleven and it's never moved." Something crossed her face that on anyone else would have been called emotion and on Rose was a controlled re-derivation. "This is a different transaction. Somewhere out there is the first genuinely new class of fact my Department has met since the Veil went up, and my world has filed it under somebody else's problem, and the only two people reading it properly are doing it at a kitchen table without me. I've spent five weeks pricing that, and the price kept coming out the same: the most interesting work in the building is happening off the books, by my best friend. I don't forgive that." A beat, precise as her mother's. "I invoice it."

"Rose—"

"Open your gift first. It's rude to negotiate past a parcel."

So she gave it over — treason suspended for wrapping paper, which was the most Ministry sentence her life had yet produced — and watched Rose open the racing-green book and go still, and then laugh: the real one, the whole Weasley half of her at once, a sound the Hall had possibly never held before and stood up well under.

"You bought me a book of bets from my own father to soften a conspiracy. You're learning the family grammar." She had it open on her knee already, and the book, sensing a professional, had ruled itself a fresh page. She wrote one line in it, unhurried, and turned it round: *Six Sickles says she'd have told me by Lammas anyway.* "I'm taking evens. The book will remember, it's the whole charm of it."

Then she slid off the desk, and squared the pencil page off the ledger, and folded the evidence back into her sleeve, and at the head of the row she paused, one hand on the shelving, exactly where she had left a question standing five weeks before.

"If we're doing treason, we're doing it properly," Rose said. "I want the data."
