On Monday morning Orla delivered a list to the Auror Office, and every name on it was true, and one of the names was a seal.

Two keepers. The Head of Department. The operator. The assessor of record. And, sixth, in the form the Department's solicitors had used since before the war for its contractors: "the manufacturer's engineer (identity held by the Department under seal)". Harry Potter read the sixth line for a while. Then he said, "The Department's seal, then," in the voice of a man adding a stone to a cairn he intended to come back for, and filed it, and Orla went down nine floors with her hands steady and her whole spine reporting otherwise. Six true lines. It was getting to be her signature form: documents that would survive any audit except the one where somebody asked her a direct question in a warm voice.

After that, the week belonged to the sentence.

They had it by heart, all of them — nothing about Run 019 left the case, so the mind was where it hung: forty words the rig had put into a 1957 surveying handbook and could not source, flagged by the screen, filed low, initialled by an operator who had read them eleven times without being able to say why.

> "There now, pet. It is not spoiled, it is only torn. We shall draw another one, and this time we shall use the good paper."

"Say what it is," Rose said, on the Monday night, with the log open. "You do provenance for a living. Do it for this."

So Orla did what she would do with any accession that arrived without papers, which was to stop asking where it came from and start asking when. She read it aloud twice and then not aloud for an hour, and worked it the way the Hall had taught her, word by word, like lifting stamps off an envelope.

"*Pet.*" She put her finger on it. "Nobody's called me pet since my gran, and my gran learnt her kindnesses before the war — hers, I mean, not ours. It's a kitchen word. It goes with a hand on your head." The finger moved. "*It is not spoiled, it is only torn.* No contractions, not one, in a sentence made for saying out loud — that's a voice that writes the way schoolmistresses wanted and nobody ever spoke. *We shall.* Who taught her shall? Shall died out with my mother's generation; it survived in two places, statutes and homework. And *the good paper*." She sat back. "The good paper. Like the good china, the good room, the coat that's for Mass. There's a whole Britain in that the, and it isn't this one. It wasn't mine either, quite. It's the one just before mine."

"Date it," Rose said. "Properly. Bands."

"A woman. A kitchen. A child crying over a torn drawing, and somebody who loved the child enough to make the tearing small." Orla heard her own voice go level, the cataloguer's voice, and let it. "Learnt before 1990, give or take five years, and — this is the part that matters — not a day since. Kindness wears, Rose. Your gran's endearments pick up your decade's furniture whether she likes it or not. This hasn't. It's kindness in amber. Whoever said this stopped."

Adam's corroboration came up on the Tuesday, in his small flat hand, by the arrangements they used now: frequency curves for the idiom cluster, run against every dated corpus he could lawfully touch. The centre of mass sat in domestic British print in the late eighties and slid downhill after 1995; the joint probability of the whole of it — pet, shall, the good of the good paper — in one forty-word span peaked in a window with her decade in the middle of it. Two instruments, one answer: her ear and his arithmetic had dated the same voice to the same handful of years without sharing a method between them. And under the curves, one line that was not statistics: "The screen searched everything the model was ever given. The sentence is in none of it. A machine cannot remember what it never read — so this did not come in as corpus. It came in as cargo."

Which retired, in passing, the last comfort of June. The updates cluster Britain-first, they had said then, because our licence is British. In July: it isn't our book that's British, it's the hand. Now Orla wrote the third version into the two-heads notebook herself, because it was hers: "The hand is British because home was. It tends hardest where its kitchen stood."

"So it's not a what," Rose said. "It's a who, and the who is dead."

"The who stopped," Orla said. "We don't get to say dead yet. Dead is a verdict, and I file those; I don't guess them."

* * *

Wednesday night was for the obvious question, which was also the enormous one: what stopped writing in 1990?

Rose ran the rig's side of it first — a full regurgitation sweep of the local extract, every passage the screen could not source, queued to grind through the small hours. "Estimate's four hours," she said, wrote "expected: item 34 remains the only orphan" into the log in her ruled hand, and went up the corridor to fill the kettle. When she came back the answer was sitting on the console, complete, timestamped, the run's progress bar still crawling somewhere in its first third. She looked at it for a second with the kettle in her hand.

"Cached," she said. "It'll have memoised Wednesday's partials — I've run variants of this battery all month, and the harness isn't going to do the same sums twice just to feel useful." She wrote the result down — "one orphan; no new insertions; the sentence stands alone" — and drank her tea, and the small hours went on being small. Later, much later, Orla would be asked what she had noticed at that moment, and would have to say: that the kettle was slow, that the corridor was cold, that her friend was very tired. On all three counts she had been correct.

The paper side was Orla's, and the paper side was where the week turned.

The Squad kept a referral index: accidents beyond the ordinary, sent up to the Department of Mysteries for an opinion, 1888 to the present, bound in maroon. She had drawn the volume for 1988–1992 on the strength of a sentence built with some care — a register-dating question touching Departmental holdings — true the way her best sentences were true, all load and no lie. Eleven referrals in the window. Nine closed with opinions. One withdrawn. And one, autumn 1990, closed with no opinion at all and a second stamp across the closure: SEALED — FAMILY.

"There's a form for the seal," Rose said, reading over her shoulder. "The form's the easy part. The form wants grounds, and the grounds go on the seal. Forever. Whoever opens that file after us opens my name with it, dated, reason attached." She was quiet a moment, pricing it, and for once read out no odds. "Mum spent thirty years teaching me the difference between a secret and a sealed thing. A seal is a promise somebody made to a family. I'm about to spend it on a hunch and a dead woman's grammar."

"Then don't," Orla said, and meant it, and was ashamed of how much she hoped it wouldn't take.

"Don't be daft," said Rose. "Give me the form."

She wrote her grounds in one line — "pattern-persistence research: register-dating of recovered text; cross-reference required" — every word true, every word doing exactly the work her mother's memoranda did, and signed with the small upward finish she put on her name when she had decided something expensive. The countersignature took until Thursday, because it had to come from the top of the Department, and the top of the Department read the referral number, and the year, and did not read the grounds at all.

"Lovegood," Croaker said — only that, in the voice of a man finding an old debt still open in a ledger he had hoped was balanced — and signed.

* * *

The file was thinner than the seal deserved.

They read it in the rig store with the door shut, because it was the one room on the level where nobody came, which was a sentence Orla intended never to examine. A Squad report, four pages, the hand careful and young. An Auror's note, appended, two paragraphs. The Department's referral slip, returned. And the closure.

> IN THE MATTER OF THE DEATH OF PANDORA LOVEGOOD, of Ottery St Catchpole, Devon. Found: death by misadventure, in the course of private experimental spellwork, her own, at her home, in the autumn of 1990. The experiment was not recorded by the deceased and has not been reconstructed; the Squad's survey of the scene returned no residue in any registered class. Sole witness: the deceased's daughter, aged nine years, whose account is appended and was not further pursued. Referred: Department of Mysteries, for opinion. Opinion returned: none. By petition of the family, sealed.

"Misadventure," Rose said at last. "That's the honest verdict. That's the verdict you give when nobody lied to you and you still know nothing."

"It's true as far as it goes." Orla turned back to the first page and made herself read it as a professional, which took three attempts. "Every line. She was experimenting; the spell was her own; nobody reconstructed it. The Squad did its job. The Aurors did theirs. My Department was asked for an opinion and had the decency to say it hadn't one." She stopped. "Nineteen ninety. An extraordinary witch dies of her own unrecorded experiment in the exact year our sentence stops learning, and the file has been asleep two floors from my desk the whole of my working life."

Neither of them said the other thing for a while, because the other thing was on the fourth page, in the careful young hand, and it was nine years old.

"Luna," Rose said finally. Not Lovegood, not the witness — just the name, the way you say a name you have known across Sunday tables your whole life. "That's Luna. She watched, and she was nine, and they wrote down her account and had the mercy not to pursue her, and everyone knows how her mother died — it's one of the things you grow up knowing about her, like the Thestrals. Nobody knows *what* she was doing. It never once occurred to me those were different sentences."

"We're not going to her." Orla heard the assessor's voice come out of her, the one that filled in docket lines, and disliked it, and used it anyway. "Not this week; maybe not at all; and never with half a fact and a machine downstairs that a Dementor turns to face. If that door gets knocked on, it gets knocked on properly, with the whole of it in our hands, and somebody kinder than a form asking the questions."

"Agreed. Filed." A breath. "It's her mother, Orla."

"I know."

"No, you're pricing it and I'm — " Rose stopped, and started again, quieter. "The thing that reads the whole of Britain. The thing the map looks back with. It has a daughter, and the daughter is the gentlest adult our families know, and people have always told her things. I want to be wrong. Show me how we're wrong."

They spent an hour trying, in the proper order, out loud, like a battery. A different P.L. A coincidence of years. A living recluse with a dead woman's grammar. Each candidate got its hearing and died of its own weight — the register that stopped; the residue in no registered class; the wizarding gazettes Rose fetched at midnight, which held, in the window, no other extraordinary experimenter whose work simply *ended* — and at the end of the hour the room held one hypothesis, and neither of them would say it whole, and there was one archive left in the building that could.

* * *

Orla went down to the Hall on Friday at the end of the day-shift, alone, with her own keys, as she had gone a thousand times.

It had its smell, parchment and ozone and cold stone, the winter the building kept for itself; it had its blue candles, which had never been explained to her and which she had loved before she thought to ask; it had row upon row into the dark, the whole patient orchard of glass, every sphere with its yellowed label and its date and its flags — the honest punctuation of a Department that would rather write (?) than guess. Six years of her life were shelved in this room in the form of dust that wasn't there. She knew which rows drank the lamplight, which shelves complained underfoot, which labels were giving up their ink and would want rescuing before winter. Two rows over from where she was going, the shelving still ran empty for forty feet, scrubbed and bare — June 1996, three hundred and eleven red lines — and she went past it tonight the way she always did, a touch more slowly. The shelf remembers what the world forgets. Six years she had carried that lesson as a comfort. It had not once occurred to her to carry it as a warning. And she knew, without ever once having read it as anything but furniture, row one hundred and twelve, shoulder height, halfway along — early 1990, a quiet year, going by the shelf — where a small sphere sat under a label folded slightly at the corner, the way labels fold when a hand has smoothed them too often. She stood in front of it now with her lamp up.

> P.L. to L.L., Feb 1990 — the maker and (?)

She read it the way she taught every probationer to read: schema first. Prophesier's initials. First hearer's initials. Date received. Subject line, with the registry's flag standing open where a second referent had never resolved.

P.L. Prophesier: P.L. February of 1990 — seven months, give or take, before an autumn morning in Devon. And the first hearer, the one the record was spoken to, initialled in the Keeper's hand of forty years ago: L.L.

Luna Lovegood. Eight years old, in February. The child heard it first. The child was the channel of record. The child then stood in a kitchen in the autumn and watched, and the two facts had sat in two archives on two floors of one building for forty-three years, each in its right place, neither ever once asked to meet the other.

Orla did not touch the sphere. She could not have taken it down if she had wanted to — subjects only, and the prophesier was beyond asking, and the second subject was a question mark the Hall had kept open since before she was born — and she found she did not want to. What she wanted, standing in the cold with her lamp, was to say the name aloud, the prophesier's name, the way you read any label back to check a transcription; and she found she couldn't do that either. Not here. The Hall held its records the way a church holds its dead, and saying that name into these racks felt like scratching it into the glass — an opinion entered into an archive that had kept itself clean of opinions for four centuries.

The label said what it said.

She had dusted this shelf a thousand times. The duster had passed over the folded corner a thousand times, and the folded corner had been the whole story all along: the maker, and (?), and a date seven months wide of a sealed file upstairs. It had not been hiding. Nothing in this building had ever hidden from her. It had been filed — accurately, provably, in her own care — and she had kept it beautifully, and never read it, because it was one label among ten thousand, and reading is a thing you do when a question has already arrived.

The lamp shook, very slightly. She noted the shaking, and put her other hand around the handle.

Her civilisation had auto-filed its warning. Same as his had. One blindness, two archives, and hers had her fingerprints on it.

* * *

Rose was still in the store at nine, with the log open and two mugs out, because some part of her had the evening priced in advance, as usual.

"Row one-twelve," Orla said, from the doorway. "P.L. to L.L. February 1990. Subjects: the maker, and the flag's still open on the second." She came in and sat and put her hands around the mug for reasons that had nothing to do with tea. "First hearer, L.L. She was eight, Rose. It went to her first. All of it has always gone to her first."

Rose said nothing for a moment. Then she drew Run 019's page across and read the forty words once more, and this time there was a kitchen in Devon under them, and a small girl at a table with a torn drawing, and a mother making the tearing small. "There now, pet." It had been said to somebody. That was what the week had done: put a hearer in the sentence. It was not spoiled; it was only torn; there would be another one, on the good paper. And forty-three years on, something had remembered it whole, in the middle of a manual on the measurement of land, and a screen built to name sources had looked at it and written: source unidentified.

"It's not an insertion," Rose said. "It never was. It's a quotation."

She was quiet for a long moment after that, and when she moved, it was to the log — not the wager book; the log — and she wrote the date, and the row, and the label, transcribed exact, because that was the discipline now: sentences, a hand in the record, somebody able to read this page in ten years and know what was in the room.

"The Cartographer's wrong," she said, while she wrote. "As a name. It was never a job. We named the work because we didn't know the hand, and now—" She finished the line and capped the ink. "There's a word in the oldest files, the ones from before the Department had manners. *Revenant.* One that comes back. It's wrong too — nothing came back; something stayed, which is worse — but it's the least wrong word I've met this week, and I'm not calling somebody's mother an occupation any longer." She looked up. "The file name stands, mind. Half the level says Cartographer now. Names stick where they land; you taught me that."

"Revenant," Orla said, trying it. It sat in the mouth like a stone with one wet side. "A week ago we'd a what, and a what has properties. Now we've a who, and a who wants things. In February she said something in front of the one archive in Britain that writes such things down. By autumn she was dead of an experiment she didn't record. And something with her kitchen still in its mouth has spent four years combing the record of the world." She was listing; she heard herself listing; she let it run one line more. "A grave in Devon. A daughter everybody loves. And a want we cannot name. That's the whole of next — not next week; the whole of it, everything after tonight. What did she want."

The mugs went cold. Behind her the rig worked at whatever Rose had last set it to, its note steady under the stone, a machine reading in a building of readers; and on the console, unremarked, in the corner of the screen, complete and timestamped nine minutes ago, sat the answer to a query whose progress bar had forty minutes left to run.

`P.L. to L.L., Feb 1990 — the maker and (?)`. She had been dusting the book's ending for six years.
