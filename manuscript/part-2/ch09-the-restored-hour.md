The nine o'clock knew her name by now, and she had stopped minding, which was its own small item for the file.

She rode north with no bag worth the word. That was the strangeness of the day, and she kept returning to it the way you return to a tooth: six years of her life had run on the principle that a record was a thing you could hold — vellum, wax, a label in a settled hand — and the most important record she had ever carried was currently in no luggage anywhere in Britain. It was behind her eyes. It weighed nothing. It had survived being weightless for four days now, and every morning of the four she had woken and walked through it start to finish before breakfast, the whole hour, the way her mother walked the school registers in September: not because the names would change, but because a record unvisited begins to think about becoming a story.

Testimony. The oldest storage medium either world had, and the leakiest, and the one they had chosen on purpose, and she had spent the journey up doing what the Hall had trained her to do with anything precious and unwitnessed — checking it against itself. Item: the times. Item: the pegs. Item: the things he had said aloud on a hillside to nobody, which were hers now to give back, and which she had promised to give back properly, and *properly* had been doing more work in that sentence than she had let him see.

Past Berwick the sea came up, grey and diligent, still writing. She let the list run once more and found it whole.

* * *

His kitchen at eight was becoming a place her week bent towards, which she had decided not to examine until at least June.

He had the notebook out on the table before her coat was off. She had rather expected the laptop too, the cameras' folder, the track files — the instruments' version, cued for cross-examination — and the table was bare of all of it, and that stopped her a half-step into the room.

"No exhibits," Adam said. He had seen her see it. "I've had the instruments' account for four days. I know what I did. What I don't have is being there." He set out two chairs, not opposite — at the corner, the short diagonal, close enough that the record would not have to be raised aloud like a toast. "You said you'd do it right. So would I. Sit down, and tell me the hour, and I'll write what you tell me."

"You'll want to check me against the left page," she said. "The entries to nine past ten. I'd want checking, in your position."

"I did want that," he said. "On Sunday I wanted it very much. I decided to want something else more." He unscrewed the pencil's little brass cap of a sharpener, set it aside, and looked at her — the whole of his attention, arriving the way it always arrived, like weather off the sea. "Begin where I stop being able to contradict you."

There were ways her world could have done this with silver and glass — a thread drawn shining from the temple, a basin, the hour replayed entire for anyone leaning in. She had considered them for the length of one train journey and closed the file. Every one of them made an object, and an object could be asked for, seized, entered into evidence by either of two governments; a record is a hostage, and they kept none. And every one of them would have been *her* hour — her vantage, her edges — poured out for him to visit like a tourist. Testimony was the only medium that handed it back in his own units, witness to reader, checkable sentence by sentence, and if it was slower, well. So was everything worth archiving.

She had prepared for the reading the way she prepared a fetch from the far racks: eaten first, breathed, hands warm. She had not prepared for that sentence, so she sat down a shade too fast, and opened the hour, and began.

"Sixteen minutes past nine. You'd just planted the fourteenth peg, and the rover had flagged twice, and you were pleased about the flags — you said thank you to it. Out loud. Then you crouched by the peg and told it the survey stood on it now, that it was load-bearing, and it should act like it." She kept her eyes on the middle distance, where the racks would be. "You wrote for a minute and a half. Wind was west-south-west and rising. You blew on your fingers between entries — right hand, twice."

He wrote. He had opened the notebook past the ruined page without letting his eyes settle on it, to the first clean spread, and headed it in his field hand, and the heading took him longer than headings take; she watched him rule a line under it and lay the hour out in columns, time and act and word, her testimony formatted like a survey, because that was the country he kept his courage in.

> RESTORED RECORD — 6 May, 09:12–10:27. From the testimony of O.Q., present the whole hour, read back 10 May at my request. Verified against surviving entries to 10:09; thereafter hers alone. Two readers.

"Nineteen past nine," she said. "You counted fifty and made it to about forty, and stopped, and said the arithmetic up here had a lean on it. Twenty-two past: compass, aloud, full bearing. Twenty-six past: you congratulated the burn on being the only thing on the hill still telling the truth—"

"That seems unfair to the loggers."

"You apologised to the loggers at half past," she said gravely. "There's a schedule; we'll come to it."

Twice in the first ten minutes he glanced from her face to the left page, where her account and his surviving entries ran together, and she watched him find them agreeing — word against word, her memory against his best field hand — and watched what the agreeing did to him. The third time the entries ran out on him, past nine past ten, off the edge of everything he could audit; and he looked at the page a moment, and turned it, and did not look down again for the rest of the hour. He took the record from her mouth as fast as she could give it. It was the single most trusting thing she had ever watched an empiricist do, and he did it with ink on his fingers, frowning, entirely unaware it was remarkable.

The middle of the hour was work, and she gave him the work: stations, bearings, the wry note about peg fourteen he had made and the wryer one he had said and not made. At thirteen minutes to ten he had stood a while looking at the light on the far slope, and had said, conversationally, to the moor at large, that the wind was getting personal, and that Miss Quinn would have it filed under conditions before it reached the fence line. She read that entry the way she read all the others, evenly, at dictation pace, eyes on the middle distance, and he wrote it down the same way, and neither of them looked at the other while the pencil crossed the line, and the kitchen was very quiet for a man writing and a woman remembering.

Then the hour began to end, and she slowed, because she had promised herself she would not soften it.

"Twelve minutes past ten. You stood up out of the survey — no pause in it, no look round, the way a man stands when he's heard the phone. You walked downslope thirty metres and stopped. Went back up. I heard you say the light was going — it was ten in the morning, Adam, the light was climbing — and you said it the way you say things you've checked." The pencil kept moving; his face had gone to the setting he kept for bad data, still, attentive, spending nothing. "Second time down, you stopped at the same line as before — the same line, to the stride — and you said: 'That's enough, I think.' Mild as milk. The way you'd close a meeting that went well. Third time you barely went up at all. You stood at the top of it maybe half a minute. And then you said the other thing, and folded the book, and put it under your arm, and came down the hill like a man with a train to catch."

"Say it," Adam said. "The other thing."

"'Good,'" she said. "'Nothing here.'"

The pencil stopped.

"Again," he said. Which she had known he would; it was the whole of him in one word, and she had heard it before, over a mended mug, in this kitchen, when it had meant wonder. It did not mean wonder now.

"'Good. Nothing here.' You said it aloud, to the glen. Then you wrote it."

"Whose voice?"

"Yours." She had promised herself. She kept the promise. "Your own voice, your own weight on it. Pleased — the way you're pleased when a check comes back clean. Adam, it was the best impression of you I've ever heard, and nothing was doing an impression. That's the working. That's what I watched it do with your face and your hands and your good sense, for an hour, from fifty yards, out of shot of your own cameras, doing nothing, because watching was the term you signed and the terms were mine."

He set the pencil down with a click on the wood, and was quiet, and she let him be. The kettle ticked; somewhere under the window the street changed shift. Then he did the thing she would take back south and keep: he turned back to the ruined page — the four tidy lies in his own hand, *nothing here* at the bottom like a signature — and she saw his shoulders set to tear it out, and stop; and he took up the pencil and ruled a single bracket down the margin instead, neat, the way you flag and never strike, and wrote beneath, small:

> The hand is mine; the sentence is not. See restored record overleaf; witness O.Q.; two readers.

"You keep the wear," he said, not quite a question, not quite to her.

"You keep the wear," she said. "Mend the break; keep the wear. It's the whole of my religion and there are days it's a cold one, and this is the first time in six years I've watched it warm somebody's kitchen." And then, because her voice had started performing and she could hear it, she stopped, and said the true thing plain. "My office takes hours. It's what we're for. Three hundred years, and kindly, and to order, and I've initialled the counts. Nobody has ever once asked us for an hour back. There's no procedure for it. I looked."

"You looked."

"I looked. There isn't one. So I wrote one." She nodded at the notebook, the bracket, the clean spread with the hour standing on it in two hands. "You're holding it."

He looked at her for a long moment, and what was in his face had no error bars on it at all, and she was all at once very busy fetching the teapot.

* * *

The second pot was for the lesson, because the evening had a syllabus — they had agreed it walking back from the terms like two people grateful for an agenda. Ward lore for statistics; hers first.

"A ward," she said, "is an argument with attention. That's the entire subject; the rest is craft. Everything my world builds to stay hidden is aimed at a person — at noticing, at wanting to look, at remembering you meant to. When the working's good there's no argument at all; you saw. You lose politely, in your own voice, and thank the room on your way out."

"And the instruments walk through it."

"The instruments were never spoken to." She turned her cup a quarter-turn, caught herself doing his tell, left it turned. "Here's what your Tuesday's actually for, so you have it straight from the archive: nobody ever warded against arithmetic. Why would they? For three centuries the only thing that ever came up the glen was a pair of eyes with a person behind them. Attention was the whole threat. So we became the best in the world at turning attention away — and your lot went and took the attention out of the looking. A camera doesn't want to be somewhere else. A ledger doesn't remember it left. There's no one in the room to lose the argument." She heard her own sentence land its full weight and let it. "Your machines aren't sharper than we are, Adam. They're deafer. It's the deafness that walks through walls."

He had his own notebook out by then — the daily one, not the field book — writing nothing, pencil balanced, which she had learnt meant the ideas were too load-bearing to trust to a page yet. "Then the map was never seeing us," he said slowly. "It was doing the accounts. And the accounts don't balance, because your whole civilisation is a withdrawal nobody logged."

"There," she said. "One lesson in and you're fluent. And next Tuesday you teach, and I want the other half: I want to know what your map thinks it knows. What one of your models expects of a Tuesday. Where the expecting lives. You've all built something that holds a picture of Britain in its head and is disappointed by the real one, and my professional opinion, Dr Kessler, as keeper of an entire cold floor of other people's expectations, is that I should meet it."

"Bring your flags," he said. "It has more open questions than the Hall; it's just never once flagged them."

She went south on the last train, tired in the good way, the way you are when the work was real. The carriage was nearly empty; her reflection rode along in the black window with Britain writing itself down behind it. Somewhere around Alnmouth she caught herself thinking of the second reader the way her brother would put it — off-site backup, one copy north of the border at all times — and was appalled, and grinned at the window like a fool, and made a note to tell nobody on Level Nine ever.

* * *

Wednesday, the minute.

Seventh of its line, and she could write them in her sleep now, which was the thing she had decided to stop pretending not to notice. *Access secure; subject cooperative in full; legend holding without maintenance.* Every sentence true; every sentence facing the reader it would have to survive; the grammar arriving before she reached for it, fitted, the way a wand arrives in the hand. She sat in the Keeper's cold office with the fair copy squared in front of her and made herself hear it this time — the little click of pleasure, the craftsman's change from a note — and did not pocket it.

Eight weeks ago the pleasure would have frightened her because it was treason. It frightened her now because it was skill. She was getting *better* at this, week on week, the way you get better at anything you practise with your whole mind, and the assessment file sat on the blotter, fat with sessions, provenance immaculate, a work of art in the one genre her floor never hung: the true false record. On the far side of it, out of the file's sight, a man was keeping a restored hour in a field notebook because she had walked it home to him behind her eyes.

The minute went in the tray for Level Three. Her hands wanted a list and she declined to supply one.

* * *

Friday afternoon the Hall was doing its winter as usual, and she was at the registry desk with the day ledger and a rack of new labels when Rose came down the row — no trolley, hands in pockets, which on Rose meant a working visit dressed as a stroll.

"Settle a bet," Rose said, by way of hello. "The Time corridor says the muggle map's got forty-one sites. The front office says thirty-eight. There's four Sickles riding and you're the floor's designated authority, which is a sentence nobody's said aloud upstairs and everybody's learnt to act on."

"Depends where they set the floor," said Orla, initialling a label. "Both numbers are true. Tell them to split it and read the caption."

"Mm. That's what the paper says too." Rose hitched herself onto the clear end of the desk, one heel swinging, and watched her work for a while, comfortable as a cat in the cold. This was the ordinary shape of a Friday, and Orla let it be ordinary, and was two labels further on before Rose spoke again, in exactly the same tone the bet had come in. "I withdrew my book on you, by the way. Tuesday. Officially closed."

"Your book on me."

"I keep a book on the whole floor, it's not personal, it's a public service." The heel swung. "Odds on everyone. When does Croaker next lose a quill down the Veil, who cracks first in the renewal round, whether the new girl in Time lasts to Michaelmas — she won't — and one page for you, which used to be the dull page. Quinn: will fret Tuesdays, will list under pressure, will come back off the assessment wrung out and lie about it badly. Steady odds for years. Lovely reliable page." Rose picked up a blank label, turned it over, put it back precisely where it had been. "Only you've stopped. You go up the country Tuesdays and come back with your sentences in the wrong order and no lists in you at all. Eight weeks on the worst file this Department's held since the war, and you've stopped counting things, Orla. You look like a woman who's had a good night's sleep in a burning building. There's no odds in you any more. A bookmaker notices what stops moving. It's generally the fixed races."

The Hall did not guess, and its keeper had six years of not guessing in her hands, and she used all of it: she initialled the next label, in the registry hand, without one wasted stroke.

"And what did the book conclude," she said, "before it closed?"

"That's the thing about my book. It's not the concluding kind; it just prices." Rose slid off the desk, unhurried, and squared the corner of the day ledger as she passed it — the whole gesture friendly, the whole gesture *thought section* — and paused at the head of the row, and asked it the way you ask about weekend plans, casual as a knife across a desk of parchment:

"Who's the assessment for, these days?"

She was gone up the row before the answer could be owed, which was its own message: not a trap sprung — a marker put down, the way you leave a flag standing in a query column. Orla sat still, both hands on the ledger, and listened to her friend's footsteps take the turn by the Time corridor, and then, in the cold, heard herself begin — item: what Rose knew; item: what Rose could know; item: what Rose would do with either —

Counting things.
