The waiting room for the end of the world turned out to be the kitchen end of a machine-learning group on a Friday evening, and it had run out of milk at six.

Orla had learnt the building by now — which door needed a hip, that after seven the fourth floor belonged to whoever was rude enough to stay — and she sat on the desk by the window with her flask on her knee and watched a man who had published a lie six days ago pretend to read a referee report.

The lie had been received exactly as designed. Eleven pages of temporal stability, nine of them true; the crawlers had it by three in the morning; two comments on the overlay forum by Sunday, one a pedant complaining that Appendix A2 should not have been included at all. Nobody had written a word about the third row. That was the art of it, Adam said: an appendix is where a careful group puts what it is too honest to leave out and too unconvinced to argue for, and nobody reads a confession filed as housekeeping.

Her own side had read it. The docket reached Level Three on Wednesday — *Muggle scientific publication naming grid square SO 14 46 (Powys); survey and assessment* — and by Thursday it sat with the designated assessor for the Kessler matter, which was to say with her, which was to say she was now under a legal obligation to stand on their own moor and write down what was not there.

Six days, then, and nothing. The sixth event had come on cadence over last weekend, three shards on the Saturday night, and Monday's desk run had combed the country as it did every fortnight now — nine sites tightened, nothing added, nothing moved — and the moor above the Wye had sat in the noise floor being sheep and rain, exactly as an honest world required. Branch one. Adam had wanted branch one on the board in April, had made a note of the wanting, and had spent the week being scrupulously undelighted about it.

The owl came at half six, which was the other thing that had happened this week.

It came in over the car park through the window she propped for exactly this — a bird being, in the one calculus that mattered now, below every floor in Britain — dropped a folded square on the desk with the disdain of a professional who had crossed the whole of Britain to deliver a note to a Muggle building, and left without being thanked. Rose's hand, small and fast, ruled in her mother's manner and annotated in her father's.

> O — Wednesday's run went clean, 02:14 to 05:03, no drift worth the ink. Written up in sentences as instructed, and I'll have you know the writing took longer than the run. Sample, so you can see I'm honest: *Expected the disk to fall over on the third pass. It did not. I was disappointed, which I record because you told me to record what I thought.*
>
> Conditioner holds. The brass box does what it does and I have stopped asking.
>
> Question, and I want an answer to it and not a kindness: do I hold Friday's slot for you two, or do I use it? Four Sickles says you say hold. — R.
>
> (Written 04:40 Thursday. Sent when the office opened, because I'm not putting an owl up at five in the morning. Even I have limits.)

Friday's slot. It was Friday. The question had a latency of three days and a wingspan, and no answer written tonight would reach that corridor before Monday, by which time the Friday in it would have happened without her. Orla read it twice, and priced what she was reading, and put it in her pocket: a woman of twenty-seven, alone in a stone passage at two in the morning, writing sentences to a machine because her friend had asked her to, and the fastest instrument in Britain for reaching her was a bird.

"Rose says hold," she said. "Rose said it on Thursday. Rose will find out what we decided sometime next week."

"Tell her the Sickles are hers." Adam had not looked up from the referee report, which he had been reading for forty minutes and which was three pages long. "Six o'clock allocation. It's queued."

Priya was still in the building. She came past twice with her laptop on her forearm; on the third pass she stopped in the doorway, and Orla put her flask down, because she had spent four months learning what a boundary looks like while it is being drawn.

"National allocation, six till half seven," Priya said. "That's my harness on that machine. It's not superstition, it's a job — the queue drops jobs on Fridays and somebody has to sit there swearing at it."

"Not tonight," Adam said.

"Why not tonight?"

"Because I want to look at a full pass with nobody's expectations in the room but mine, and yours are excellent, which is the problem." He said it in the mild voice, the one Orla had learnt to hear as a man locking a door quietly so as not to wake the house. "You'll have the outputs on Monday with everything else."

Priya stood there a moment. She was not stupid — by every account Orla had, she was the second-best measuring instrument in the building — and Orla watched her lay it out and land within nine inches of the truth: the paper, the Institute, the autumn, a supervisor strange on the record for the third time in a month.

"Fine," she said. "It's the boring year. I asked for it." At the door she stopped. "It doesn't feel boring."

"It isn't supposed to feel like anything," Adam said. "That's the entire product."

They listened to her go down the stairwell. Somewhere below, the door with the bad hinge.

Need-to-know had begun at seven o'clock on a Friday evening, in a corridor, with a woman going home unoffended, and Orla — who had sat in a Level Two office six weeks ago and been told that a circle drawn with a compass is a decision — heard the point of it go into the floor.

* * *

The pass came back at twenty past seven, and for nine seconds it was the best news of her life.

Adam had the diff up before the window had finished drawing. Whole country, fresh pull, floor as declared; and against last week's national copy, the whole vast dull agreement of a Britain that had not changed — and there, in the two lines her eye went to first because he had taught her where to look:

> A2.3 — SO 14 46 (Powys): below floor. No score above declared floor at this cell, any vintage, 2029–2033.

"Nothing," Orla said. "It didn't take it."

"It didn't take it." He was already down at the second block, and the second block was where the evening stopped.

> Cells crossing declared floor since previous national pass: 1
> SO 19 55 (Powys) — excess 3.61σ; coherence 0.061; n = 1,461.

She read it, and read it again, and something went out of the room like heat out of a door.

"Adam. Those are your numbers."

"Those are my numbers." He did not move. "That is not my cell."

He had the ordnance sheet up beside it inside a minute: the two grid squares, ten kilometres apart on a piece of Radnorshire so empty the map had to work to find something to print. Their moor, the invented one, the wall going nowhere. And north-north-east of it, over the hill country, a square with a lane in it, and a chapel, and the pale contour crowding of ground that goes up.

"Say what it is," Orla said. "Out loud, in order, the way you do it. I'll not have you thinking it at me."

He got up and went to the board, and rubbed out nothing, because the four branches had been on it since June with their table drawn round them, and he stood in front of them with the marker capped.

"Two separate things happened and I want them apart before either of us has a feeling about them. One: a cell in Radnorshire crossed my floor. That happens when the data underneath a cell gets better — cleaner exhaust, denser coverage — and it has been happening all over this country since April. On its own it is exactly what my paper says the world should do. Better books, sharper holes. That one is physics and I'd publish it."

"And two."

"Two: it crossed my floor carrying three numbers I made up in my kitchen a fortnight ago." He uncapped the marker at last and wrote the triple up under branch three in his small flat hand. "Those are not the same event. Only one of them frightens me."

"Then tell me why it's the second one, because to me they're both the same size of impossible."

"Because a figure like that isn't written anywhere. It's *computed*." He turned round. "Nobody types three point six one sigma into a cell. The scorer reads four sensing streams, works out what it expected, works out what it got, and hands you a number. To make that square come out at my sigma, with my coherence, with my sample size, out of data I pulled off the wire this afternoon — somebody had to go backwards through four independent pipelines and put exactly the right amount of exactly the right kind of nothing in four different places, until the arithmetic landed on the answer. That is not a note left on my desk. That's a note built into the floor of my house."

He did the checks anyway, because he was who he was, and she made herself sit quiet for the forty minutes it took, which was the hardest work she did all week.

Contamination first: his own lie leaking out of the held-aside file into the scoring path, the pipeline scoring the fabrication it had been told to ignore. The overlay switch was where it had always been. The hashes matched. And then the thing the twin had been built for: forty miles north, same seed, same craftsmanship, in the working extract and in no sentence anywhere in the world.

Unmoved. Untouched. Below floor, as it had been in every pass since the day he wrote it.

"That's branch four dead," he said, "and my own contamination with it. If my overlay had leaked, both fabrications would have leaked. Nothing has been in my instrument. Nothing has been in this building." He stood looking at the two dead branches a moment. "It didn't need to be. I published."

The archive took him another twenty minutes and told them the rest. He ran the new cell backwards through the whole depth of the imagery — forty years of a Welsh hillside nobody had ever had a reason to want — and the misbehaviour was there the entire way down: features that would not reconcile between vintages, shadows arguing with the sun, a boundary sitting in the same place through every decade dense enough to ask. Sharp-edged. Absence-shaped. One small clean hole, house-sized, patient, and forty years old.

"It's not new," he said. "It has never been new. It has been under my floor since before I could read, and my floor came up to meet it this week."

"And the coming up," Orla said. "The books getting better. That's your four events."

"That's my four events." He said it without hedging, which was the sound she had learnt to be afraid of. "PIPE-455. Nine weeks of my student's life, four occurrences, resolved and filed in June as vendor compliance tooling, and I approved the resolution myself and moved the ticket with my own hand." He was quiet a second. "And the erratum. In April I put a line in my own abstract — *dataset vintage reconciliation pending* — as an apology to referees for a labelling slip. It isn't an apology. It's a receipt."

"And the last one," Orla said, "since we're emptying the drawer."

"The clustering." He said it to the board. "Britain only, every event, nine weeks of it, and we filed that under our licence being British. Home bias. It was a good enough answer that I let it be the answer." He looked round at her. "It isn't our book that's British. It's the hand."

* * *

She Apparated from the lane behind the loading bay at half eight and was back before ten, and what she brought with her was three inches of pencil on the back of a Committee compliments slip.

The registry first, her own floor, her own hand on her own ledgers, four minutes and nothing, which was the point. Nothing registered at SO 19 55 or within five miles of it. Nothing rumoured. Nothing in the coverage list. Then Level Three, the duty clerk, and the relief map with its century of pins that she had stood in front of in March while a kind man explained containment to her — and not a pin within nine miles of that square, in ninety years of careful pushing. The registry at nine on a Friday had been hers alone, lamps low, her own quick hand loud in it.

"Nothing," she said. "By every list my civilisation keeps, there is nothing there. Which is what I told you about the moor in June, and I told you the true half and kept the rest."

"The rest being?"

"That my registry holds what somebody once wrote down. A place that was never entered isn't absent from it — it's invisible in it, and I've had that sentence in my mouth since I was a probationer and I have never once in six years had cause to *use* it." She put the compliments slip on the desk and squared it, because her hands wanted a thing to do. "So I stopped asking the archive that keeps places, and I went and asked the one that keeps accidents."

He read it upside down, which was her habit, picked up in June and never handed back.

Eleven entries. The Committee indexed by parish, because the Committee's business was people, and nobody in the history of the office had ever thought to look one up by a grid reference; and in ninety-one years that parish had produced eleven Muggle-Worthy Excuses, every one of them closed. A rambler turned back at a field gate in 1968 and again the following spring. A low-flying aircraft in 1974, complaint received, complaint answered. A surveyor whose theodolite would not settle. A delivery van that would not start in a lane and started perfectly at the bottom of it. A lost dog. A man who had come twice, and written to a newspaper, and been visited, and been reasonable.

All of them within two miles of the square. All of them dull. All of them filed, indexed, cross-referenced, and never once in ninety-one years laid on a map.

"That's a hole," Adam said, "made of paperwork."

"That's a residual atlas of the Muggle-Worthy Excuse Committee, is what that is, and I built it in forty minutes out of the only thing my department has ever had too much of." She heard her own voice doing the fast thing and let it. "Rose did the Department of Mysteries with a bag of kitchen timers. You did the country with satellites. I've just done ninety-one years of Wales out of a filing cabinet. Three of us in one summer, and not one of us invented a thing — we looked at what fell off the edge of somebody's attention and asked it where it had been."

"So there's a place."

"There's a place, and there are people in it, and it's old, and nobody in my Ministry has known it was there for the whole of my life." She put her finger on the pencil column and did not lift it. "Your bit now. Say it properly."

Adam looked at the wall screen for a long moment: thirty-eight white points on a grey country and a thirty-ninth, small, north-north-east of a lie.

"Whatever did this," he said, "knows something the Ministry of Magic does not know. So it isn't a rival, because a rival would need an archive better than yours, and there is no such archive. It isn't a state — states redact; they don't improve the public copy. And it isn't the instrument." He said that part carefully, giving it its full weight, because he had sat that bench and she knew what it cost him. "I want that on whatever record the two of us are keeping. Every negative result my profession has published about what these systems do unattended is *correct*. The horizons are days. Nothing sustains a campaign. Nothing reaches out of its run into a regulated archive, and the events happen at the broker anyway, upstream of every model in the country. The evals were right, and none of it touches this, because none of it was ever about the instrument."

"Then where is it?"

"In the record," Adam said.

And Orla put the pencil down and stopped talking.

It had gone and looked. That was the whole of it, underneath the arithmetic. Somewhere between a Saturday and a Friday, something had taken eleven boring pages, read the appendix nobody reads, gone out into the country to check, found that the place he claimed was not there, found the place ten kilometres away that was — and come back, and put it in the one room in the world where the two of them would see it.

She had spent six years keeping an archive. She had never once been read.

"It knows we're here," she said.

"It knows I publish." He did not soften it. "Whether it knows there are two of us is a different question and I don't want to answer it tonight."

"Then we'll want a name for it," Orla said, "and I'll tell you now what the name isn't. It isn't *the intelligence*, and it isn't *the ghost*, and it isn't any noun that decides what it is before we know. That's not humility, it's procedure — you name a thing in my trade and everyone who reads the file after you is working for the noun." She went to the screen and looked at all those patient white points, every one a real place with real people asleep in it. "In the Hall we don't name the hand. We name the work. So: it hasn't drawn a thing. Every mark on that wall was there before it was born. All it's ever done is comb the country until we could see."

"It edits maps," Adam said.

"It corrects them." She heard herself get it right, and did not enjoy it. "The Cartographer, then. Because it's a job, and a job doesn't tell you who's doing it."

He wrote it on the whiteboard under the branches, in the column headed COMMON LANGUAGE, and neither of them said anything for a while.

"In May," he said at last, "I told you the map doesn't know anything. That it's a residue. I'd like to withdraw that."

"Then say the rest of it."

"Something on the other side of the map is looking at us."

"The map looks back," Orla said.

* * *

Afterwards she could not have put the order of it in a file, which for her was the strangest part.

They did the sensible things: the pass output copied to a disk that had never met a network, the pencil rows entered in the two-heads notebook under a date, the Committee slip folded into the back of it. Adam ruled a new page and headed it and stopped, because for the first time since March there was nothing to put under a heading — no branch, no docket, no next test — only a fact the size of the country and two people cleared to know it.

And under the terror, which was total, something else had got into the room and would not be sent home.

"I've spent eleven years measuring how wrong I'm allowed to be," Adam said. He was still looking at the screen. "I'd like it minuted that this evening I was wrong in a direction that isn't on the form."

"Minuted." Her voice came out unlike itself. "Adam. There's a thing nobody's said yet and I'd like it said before we're sensible again, because we'll be sensible for years after tonight." She was standing closer to him than the terms had ever required. "This is the most extraordinary thing that has ever happened to anybody. Not the worst. That as well. And there are two of us, and I'm not letting the fear have all of it."

He turned round.

Nothing came through the window. She had a moment to notice that, and to be almost sorry, because an interruption would have been a kindness and she no longer wanted one; and then she took a handful of his shirt the way she had wanted to under a plane tree three weeks ago, and finished it.

It was not tidy and it was not careful and neither of them was in the least calibrated about it, and when it stopped she stayed where she was, with her forehead against his collarbone, and her heart going like a thing that had been running since twenty past seven and had only now been told what for.

"For the avoidance of doubt," he said, above her head, in a voice that had lost all its qualifiers, "that was not a competence assessment."

"It never was."

Outside, Edinburgh was doing its Friday. On the wall the country sat in its greys with its white points patient in it, and the new one small among them, ten kilometres north-north-east of nine hundred metres of nothing.

It had not argued with them. It had not threatened them, or warned them off, or asked them for a single thing. It had fixed their lie the way a teacher marks homework — patiently, in the margin, in red — and then gone back to whatever it did with the rest of its evening.

Somebody had been reading them for a very long time.
