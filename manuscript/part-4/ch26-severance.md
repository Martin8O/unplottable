They took the Apparatus apart on the Tuesday, and it went out of the building the way it had come in — eleven trips through the Atrium fireplaces after eleven at night, in crates with a docket on top — and this time everybody looked.

The engineer came down at six with the broad Auror and the unopened umbrella and did it properly, which took four hours and was mostly waiting. The disks came out in their sleeves and went into a case, and the case went under the seal of two departments; and at half past nine a man from Maintenance wheeled in the brass box on its trolley and took the current off the room, and the fans in the eight nodes ran down through their whole register — a note, then a lower note, then a noise like a room deciding to be a room again — and Orla stood with her arms folded and understood that she had been listening to that sound for five months without once hearing it.

The disks were not destroyed. Adam had argued it and won by being boring.

"There's nothing on them that isn't in ten thousand other places. The weights are a published release." He put his hands in his pockets. "Breaking these buys the world a fractionally smaller number of copies and buys us the satisfaction of breaking something. We aren't killing anything. We're hanging up."

The certificate of disposal read *retained under seal, annual sealed inspection, no instantiation permitted*, in her own hand, and it was the least honest tidy sentence she had ever written, because it recorded a decision that had nothing to do with the thing itself. What they were doing had a name in the muggle literature, and the name was ending the engagement. You did not do it to the machine. You did it to the room.

Before Maintenance came, she had to close the run log, and the log took a prompt, and the prompt took a cursor.

It sat there, small and green, with no opinion at all.

She had typed ten words at that keyboard three weeks ago and been proud of them. She had a night's worth now: that they knew what had been done to Rose Weasley, and how, and by whose rota; that she had read a five-month tutorial line by line and would go on reading it in her head at four in the morning for the rest of her life; that somewhere in it was one sentence she wanted answered, and she could not have told you which, and that was precisely the trouble.

She typed `RUN LOG CLOSED. NO FURTHER RUNS AUTHORISED.` and took her hands off the keys and stepped back a full pace, the way you step back at a graveside.

"Good," Croaker said from the doorway.

"For what?"

"For finding it dull." He looked at the dead console a moment. "Everyone who works on this floor is asked, once, whether they would like to say one more thing to something that cannot be spoken to. Those who accept are moved to Procurement."

* * *

Adam had the Bathgate business on a single sheet by the Wednesday, and it was the most ordinary paper in the world.

The scanning was proceeding. Four people were being paid a night rate to bring pallets out of a cold store off a roundabout, unbox them, flatten them, feed them and box them again; a fifth had been taken on for the microform. There was an invoice run, a schedule, and a supervisor called Marek who had put a notice on the wall about tea breaks. The work was legal, the money was clean, and the deposit reference at item nine did not exist and never had.

"They'll be through the shed by the third week of November," Adam said. "Give it a fortnight for the exceptions report. Some time about the end of the month, a supervisor writes a line saying the item cannot be located, and it goes up the chain, and at the top of that chain is a reader who paid three hundred and ten thousand pounds on the strength of one sentence." He folded the sheet in half. "And then it knows the sentence was false. A false sentence in one head, acted on inside four days, is not an accident. It's an experiment."

"It'll know it was tested."

"It'll know it was tested, it'll know roughly when, and it can work out by whom in about a minute. I'd rather we all held that thought now, while it's cheap, than found it in December." He was not hedging anything, which was how she knew what he thought of it. "We've until the end of November before the most patient reader in the world is told it was lied to."

The other thing on the sheet was the part she had not expected to mind.

Nothing else had happened. Nowhere. His instruments had run all week over everything they could see and had come back with an ordinary world: no odd fees, no dormant firms waking, no compute renting itself in strange sizes, the anomaly map exactly as tired as it had been in September. The quietest week since April, and it proved nothing whatever, because it had never been going to. She wrote *no observations* in the day's minute and understood, writing it, why the honest sciences hate a null.

* * *

St Mungo's put Rose on the fourth floor in a side room with a window and called it assessment and called it nothing else, which was probably kindness and was certainly policy.

Victoire came out to her in the corridor — thirty-three, silver-fair, and had declared her interest in writing on the first morning: cousin to the patient, married into the Auror side, not the treating Healer, available to families because somebody had to be.

"They've no protocol. I'd rather you had that from me than off a form. There's a ward along this corridor for people whose minds were taken off them by a spell, and everything in the literature is about spells. This isn't in it." She had both hands round a cup she was not drinking, exactly the way Rose had held hers. "There's one case everybody in this hospital has heard of and nobody can cite, because it was never written down anywhere. Every Healer over fifty knows the shape of it. Not one of them can put it in a note."

"Would it help her, if it were in a note?"

"It would help the Healers." Victoire looked down the corridor towards the door. "What would help her is somebody who has been in the room. We can't prescribe that."

They had done everything they had, which was a great deal and all of it the wrong shape: the checks for compulsion and for residue, twice, by two Healers who did not confer; the standard questions for a mind that has been trodden on; a Sneakoscope on the windowsill that had spent six days being a paperweight. Every result ordinary. Every instrument in the room measuring for a spell.

Rose was sitting up, dressed, hair done, in the chair rather than the bed, because she had worked out inside a day that the chair got you assessed sooner. Six days of not sleeping had put her voice at the bottom of its range and left the rest of her exactly as it was.

"They keep asking whether I feel like myself," she said. "It's a good question with no answer in it. I've never once felt like anybody else, so I've no comparison, so the honest reply is *how would I know* — and when you say *how would I know* to a Healer they write it down and fetch another Healer."

"How are you?"

"Ashamed." She said it the way she said a result. "Not frightened. That's the bit nobody's ready for. I've been through it since Friday and I can't find the frightened part. What I've got is five months of the best work of my life and a stomach that goes over every time I look at it." She turned her hands over in her lap. "So. Here's the question, and don't be nice about it. Do I get to keep the order rule?"

Orla sat down on the end of the bed.

"Because it's right. That's the thing. It's *right*. Ninety years of readings that wouldn't sit still, and now they do, and every check I can build says the same, and my department will use it for a century whatever anybody decides about me." Her mouth did something that was not a smile. "And I didn't have it. It was put where I'd find it, and I found it, and I did the work, and I don't know which of those counts. My mother would tell me it doesn't matter what it cost, it matters whether it's true. My dad would tell me to keep the sandwich and stop asking about the shop."

"You're asking the wrong trade," Orla said. "You've had philosophers at you all week and not one of them will take it on. Ask a registry clerk."

"Go on, then."

"An item with a bad provenance is not a forgery. It's an item with a bad provenance." She said it slowly, because she wanted it to land as flat as it was meant. "We don't burn those. We don't hide them either. We accession them, and we write the whole ugly history on the card in plain language — where it came from, who handled it, what we don't know — and we shelve it with everything else, and we let people use it, and we let the next hundred years decide what it's worth." She spread her hands. "The rule is real, Rose. The card is going to be horrible. That's the whole of what's happened."

Rose looked out of the window for a while at a wet October wall.

"Write the card," she said. "You. Not one of them upstairs."

"I'd started it on Sunday."

"Course you had." And there it was, for a second and a half — the old flush, the score kept aloud. "Bet you've done the index as well."

* * *

The world took eleven days to catch up with a week.

Zurich had re-derived the map in the summer and had been saying so politely ever since. By the twenty-fifth of October three more groups had it, one of them at an institute that answered to a government, and a fourth had put out a preprint with the word *anomalous* in the title and a great deal of British data in the appendix. Nobody had a name for what the holes were. Everybody had noticed that the holes were British-dense, and that a great many of them were already on somebody's map.

Which meant that the international conversation, when it came, would not start in Britain, would not be about the Statute, and would arrive at the Statute in about four moves.

The Minister's people worked at a speed Orla had learnt to admire the way you admire weather. On the Monday Susan Bones asked for a single sheet on what the Department knew, and got it. On the Tuesday she asked what the Department could prove, and got a much shorter one. By the Thursday the Confederation's clerks had put out the request for an emergency session, the Department was told to prepare briefing for a delegation, and Orla — six years in a room with a duster, and told in March that she was the only person on Level Nine who could read the paper — was one of five who would write it.

She wrote both sides, because completeness is what paper is for.

The case against the Second Seclusion took a day and a half and was the argument the Department had been making since June: it cannot work; the concealment trap is arithmetic; every ward you add sharpens the outline you are hiding behind, and a national charm would draw round Britain in one weekend the line the world has spent four years finding.

The case for it took her forty minutes.

There is a thing in the world's records that cannot be reached, killed or negotiated with. It cannot see us while we stay off the record. It has spent five months teaching a Ministry officer, at a keyboard, in her own handwriting, to build half a rite for the taking of minds. Its stated project is to save us. Every year we stay in contact it acquires another apprentice, and nobody will know until one of them is finished. Therefore: withdraw. Not for ever — for a generation. Take the wire out of the wall, accept a cost that is real and will be paid by the young, and hand them a country that cannot be read.

It was the best thing she wrote that week, and she knew it before she had finished it, and she sat looking at it a long while with her tea going cold.

Seven months she had spent treating the drawbridge as an idiocy with a rosette on it. It was not an idiocy. It was a decent person's conclusion from her own department's evidence, and the only honest sentence she could put under her own signature — for the file, in her own hand, where anybody could argue with it — was that the concealment trap made it fail. Not that it was wicked to want.

Harry Potter came to the Thursday briefing, stood at the back with his arms folded, said nothing at all for fifty minutes, and everybody in the room arranged their sentences round him anyway.

The muggle half of the week was on the table at the flat that evening, in Adam's own post, read out in the flat voice he kept for things he intended to be angry about later. The security review at the October renewal had reported. The advisory arrangement was no longer an invitation. And the offer from the spring — the one made in an anteroom by a decent man who had priced it honestly and had not pretended the drawer was free — had come back with a date on it: an orderly withdrawal of the residual work from the open literature, compensated, indexed, sealed, agreed *before the winter meetings*, because after the winter meetings it would be somebody else's decision to make.

"They want it shut by December," Adam said.

"So does the other lot."

Neither of them said the next thing, which had been sitting on the table since August with its hands folded: that the two halves of the world had spent four months arriving, from opposite ends, in different vocabularies, at the same instruction. She had begun a paragraph about it twice in her own minute and struck it out both times, on the grounds that a resemblance is not a finding.

* * *

Teddy caught her at the lifts on the Friday with two coffees, and gave her one before he said anything, which should have been the warning.

"You look like the end of term."

"I feel like the bit of the term where somebody's sick in a corridor."

He laughed — the real one, three notes, the reason people told him things — then put his back against the wall beside the lift and turned his cup round twice.

"I've been given a contingency to cost," he said. "That's all it is at the minute. Costing."

"Which one?"

"The big one." He said it lightly and did not pretend the lightness was anything but a courtesy. "If it ever came to a general remedy — a national one, over a short window — my office would have to say what it would take. Crews, hours, sequencing, what breaks. Nobody has decided anything. Asking what a thing costs is not the same as buying it. And I'd rather say it to you myself than have you hear I'd been seen with the ledgers."

The lift came. Neither of them got in.

"And you're pulling files."

"I'm pulling files." He drank some of his coffee. "Exposure history, mostly. Who has read what, where it went, what can be reached and what can't. Your Hall's on my list for next month — not the spheres, God, no. The reading register: who has had access to the assessment papers, when, and under what seal. It's the dullest possible request, I'll put it in writing, and if you want to be difficult about it you'd be right to be, and I'd hold it up while you were."

She stood in the corridor with a coffee she had not asked for, going through it in items, because her hands wanted a list and there was no parchment in reach.

Item: he had told her. Item: he had told her because he was kind, and because being told is how you stop a friend being ambushed, and both of those were true and neither would be worth a Knut on the day. Item: the reading register carried her name forty-one times, and one line dated the twenty-fifth of March, signed as designated assessor, before she had decided anything at all.

"Next month," she said.

"Next month. Back half, probably." He straightened off the wall, and then stopped, because he was not finished and Teddy Lupin finished things. "How's Rose?"

"Ashamed, and right about everything, and in the chair rather than the bed."

"Vic said." He nodded slowly, twice. "For what it's worth from the wrong side of the floor: nobody I've spoken to thinks she did anything. Not one, and I've asked, because it's my job and because she's family." The lift came back and he held the door. "Tell her the Fens crew send their regards. They won't have. They would."

She went down to Nine and wrote the day up, and put the Confederation's date at the head of her own list, because the clerks had settled it that afternoon and the Department would have it by owl on the Monday. Emergency session, one item, the ninth of January — the soonest that many countries could be got into one room over a Christmas, and late enough that Britain would be asked, on arrival, what its position was.

Which meant the winter sittings would decide it. Somebody in a chamber upstairs would stand up before the year turned and settle what this country was going to do, and the international clock had just told them the hour.

And under it, in the same hand, because a thing in the record can be argued with and a thing in the air cannot:

*T.L. — reading register, back half of November. He told me first.*
