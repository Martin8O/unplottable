On Friday the digest came up before the tea did, which had never once happened, and the top cutting wore two pencil flags — the compiler's entire vocabulary of alarm.

Orla read it standing, coat still on, in the Keeper's office with the lamp not yet lit, because the first line had caught her on the way to the hook and the rest would not let her go and sit down.

> CAUSEWAY — TECHNICAL NOTE 2033/11 (public)
> Re: Kessler & Nair, "Structured Excess Residuals in Earth-System World Models" (March 2033)
> 1. Confirmation. All 38 reported sites replicate in Causeway holdings at high confidence, with four further candidate regions beneath the paper's published floor (not enumerated here).
> 2. Archive finding. Grid cells coincident with the reported sites have been excluded from our training aggregates since November 2029 by automated data-quality triage (class: DATA-QUALITY, sensor vintage / ingest fault; recommended action: exclude from aggregates). These exclusions were procedurally correct and factually wrong. The signal is not attributable to any fault class we can enumerate.
> 3. Egocentric holdings. In wearable-video corpora, pedestrian tracks and attention statistics deviate at the reported boundaries: scene geometry requires the sites to enter the frame; the frames record foreground, weather and pavement. The deficit is in the observers, not the instruments.
> 4. Causeway is suspending further public comment pending internal review.

The compiler had underlined "procedurally correct and factually wrong" and pencilled beside it, in the margin's careful nothing-hand: "their words".

She stood with it a long time.

Four years. The biggest reading engines on earth had been looking straight at the white sites for four years and filing them, every night, under broken glass — the same tidy verdict Adam had held open with one line and his own name — and now the largest of the labs had checked its cellars and said so, in public, in numbered paragraphs. On Level Three there was a blackboard where a round grey witch had spent a fortnight hunting a better class of broken instrument. The Muggles' machines had been the better class of broken instrument. The excuse had been theirs, automatic, and free, and this morning they had retracted it, on the record, in their own words.

And the third paragraph. She read it again, slower, and the room got colder the way it did when the lift doors opened upstairs.

The charm worked. That was the horror of it — not a failure, the *working*. A wizard's whole science of staying hidden was a science of the glance that slides off, and it still slid, dependable as rain; only now the glance was worn on twenty million faces and wrote itself down as it slid. There was a version of Britain, held in a stranger's training corpus, assembled from every look it had ever turned aside — a map of exactly where the country did not look — and it matched the wall on Level Three, pin for pin.

Absence had been the school's mistake. Bending had been Azkaban's. This was everyone's, and it was the oldest one: the belief that being unlooked-at was the same as being unseen.

The violet memo arrived while she was still standing there, wings squared, in a hand she now knew.

> Minister's briefing, eleven, Level One. Bring the map and your voice. They will not like either. — T.L.

* * *

"Level One," said the lift, "Minister for Magic and Support Staff," in the tone it kept for its best material, and Orla stepped out into carpet.

She had gone down for six years, then sideways for a fortnight, and now up; the lift was running out of Britain to show her. Level One was quieter than any floor she had worked and warmer than any floor deserved to be, and the briefing room, when an assistant showed her in, held one long table, no portraits she could see being seen by, and the Minister for Magic, pouring the tea herself. Light came from high windows that were somebody's idea of a fine morning — a shade too even, like weather in a painting — and the only furniture with any age on it was the easel: oak, paint-flecked at the feet, plainly borrowed from a schoolroom once and never returned.

Susan Bones was a broad, quiet woman who read every paper on the table before she let anyone speak, and while she read, the room — heads of this and that, robes at their soberest, the Squad's daisy on one shoulder by the door — arranged itself around her silence the way furniture arranges itself around a fire. Orla had expected glitter, or at least weather. What the Minister had instead was the manner of a senior matron on a difficult ward: no hurry, no performance, the attention going round the table like a lamp.

"Miss Quinn." The Minister put the last paper down. "The Department's reader. I'm told the week has been eventful in your part of the library." A small gesture at the easel where Figure 1 stood, and beside it the fair copy in registry hand, turned face-inward. "Nobody in this room will pretend to have followed the arithmetic. Tell us what happened yesterday, in ours."

So Orla told them. She had rehearsed it in the lift and abandoned the rehearsal two sentences in, because the room did not want a briefing, it wanted a translation, and translation was the one trade she had never needed notes for. The paper had been checked by strangers, and had held. The strangers' biggest machines had confessed to writing the Ministry's excuse for four years, and had now torn it up in public, which meant the excuse was not late, it was *spent* — there was no version of the broken-instrument story left that their own instruments would not contradict. And the third paragraph. She gave them the third paragraph in wizard's terms, and watched it land.

"They have put eyes at eye level," she said. "Millions of them. Worn, always on — and the charm holds against every single pair. Every wearer looks away. But the looking-away is written down now, Minister. Their machines have learnt what a street looks like when nobody will look at it. Our concealments work on every person who comes near them, and that working has become the signature. We are not hidden from the thing that watches. We are hidden *in* it."

The room made the sound she had last heard on Level Three, over an island — twenty professionals privately re-deriving the same result.

"Thank you," the Minister said, without hurry. "The Head of Magical Law Enforcement reaches the same wall by a different stair. Her memorandum has been circulated; I'll have the operative part in the minutes." She nodded to the assistant, who read it out in the flat voice reserved for other people's thunder:

> DEPARTMENT OF MAGICAL LAW ENFORCEMENT — MEMORANDUM TO THE MINISTER
> Re: Breach (Documentary) 2033/1 — enforcement position
> 1. The Statute's instruments act upon persons and places. This breach resides in neither. It is held in records without owners, in copies without number, in jurisdictions without wands.
> 2. As of yesterday the largest Muggle archives have confirmed the finding publicly and withdrawn, in their own words, the only excuse we could ever have issued. There is no cover story left to write, and the Committee should not be asked to write one.
> 3. Action against the authors would remove from the world the two people handling the material with the most care, and nothing else. I decline to recommend it, and will say so to the Wizengamot if asked.
> 4. Containment, as doctrine, ended on 21 March. What remains to us is the management of arrival. I am aware this memorandum will be read as pessimism. I ask that it be read again in May.
> — Granger, DMLE

Paragraph three said, under Law Enforcement's letterhead, in its unhurried ink, the sentence Orla had crossed out of her own first draft a week ago for being too true to file. She kept her face still while it was read, and it cost her.

"Law Enforcement takes the gravest available view," said the Head of Magical Accidents and Catastrophes, a spare precise wizard with fifty years of other people's emergencies behind his eyes, "as her office requires. Ours requires the opposite. The survey holds; the wards hold; the trade has weathered every arrival since 1689, and the procedure—" he did not quite say *has never lost*, the way a man does not quite touch a wall he has always leaned on "—the procedure is not yet exhausted, Minister. Doctrines do not die of a memorandum."

"No," the Minister agreed. "They die of the thing the memorandum describes." But she said it mildly, and the room chose to hear it as balance, and the memo went into the minutes the way a stone goes into deep water: in perfect order, and without visible effect.

"Minister." The Head of International Magical Cooperation had waited the way committee men wait, which is actively. Ernie Macmillan: big, silver at the temples, upholstered in the deep black of a man who has chaired things on three continents, with a watch chain you could have moored a boat to. "Before the room comforts itself back into procedure — my department's arithmetic. That map is not a British document. It was assembled from archives no nation owns, by methods now public, and our soundings say the finding is already repeatable from Lisbon to Kyoto: the sites of nine other delegations stand latent in the selfsame data, waiting only on the asking. So the Confederation's question is not whether magical Britain has been seen. It is who will be asked about it first, and by whom, and whether Britain means to have an answer ready or to borrow one in a corridor." He let that sit exactly as long as it needed. "I have one to propose."

"Propose it," the Minister said.

He stood, which nobody else had done, and for the first sentence it was pomposity; and then it stopped being pomposity, which was the most frightening thing Orla had watched all week.

"In 1689 the Confederation signed the Statute, and we withdrew — and we did it badly. Half a withdrawal. We kept the hems of our robes in their world: enchanted ways through their stations, a hospital behind their shopfronts, this Ministry under their street. For three centuries we have lived like a man hiding in a house he insists on walking through, and we have called the success of the patching a doctrine. The paper on that easel is the bill for the half we kept. I move that Britain lay before the Wizengamot, and thereafter before the Confederation, a second and final Seclusion: every dwelling, every settlement, every soul of magical Britain enrolled under the Fidelius and its sister concealments; every enchanted way between the worlds closed; every Muggle-facing office of this Ministry wound up — in order, with honour, and for good. The first Seclusion was 1689, and it was left half-done because finishing was hard. I move that we finish the work."

Silence — but a working silence, the kind the Hall's readers gave a difficult label.

"Miss Quinn." The Minister had not taken her eyes off the easel. "You've read their instruments. If we did as the Head of International Magical Cooperation proposes — to the map, what would Britain look like?"

"Like the largest thing anyone has ever hidden, Minister." There was no kind way to say it, so she said it plainly. "The sites don't shine because we are careless. They shine because we are *there*, and hiding. Every concealment we add is more of the thing the map is a map of. Their models improve every year — that's the paper's cruellest line: the better the machine, the brighter we burn — and a Britain sealed end to end doesn't leave the map. It becomes the map. One absence the size of a country, with three centuries of archive behind it, and every eye at eye level writing down the not-seeing of it."

"Then let them stare at the hole," Ernie said — without heat; reasonably, which was worse. "A mystery on a map is a mystery, Miss Quinn. People have lived beside mysteries before now and still got their harvests in. A door with a street address is an invitation. Between a bright absence with nothing reachable inside it, and a half-hidden country full of doors, I know which my department can defend for a century."

And the Minister did not laugh.

Orla watched her not do it — watched the one person in the room with the standing to call the motion absurd sit instead with her hands round a cooling cup, listening the way you listen to an old song you had hoped not to hear again — and understood, with the part of her that read rooms the way she read shelves, that she was watching a family history attend a meeting. Nobody over fifty at that table needed the arithmetic of the name Bones explained. It was a name from both wars' lists — a whole family of the first war's famous dead, and then, a generation on, an aunt at the head of Magical Law Enforcement, found at home by the enemy himself behind a door locked from the inside — a family that had been easy to find — and its last daughter sat at the head of the table while a decent man proposed that no one magical ever be findable again, and she did not laugh, and the not-laughing went through the room like a change of pressure.

"The Department of Mysteries," the Minister said at last. "Do you keep a view on any of this?"

"The Department notes that the question has changed, Minister — from 'how do we stop it' to 'what do we choose' — and observes that questions are its trade, so the times appear to be moving our way," Croaker said, from the foot of the table, folded and grey and comfortable, and went back to his hands.

"Then here is where the morning ends." The Minister set down the cup. No wand, no raised voice; the room simply came to order around her, the way the Hall came to order around the evening bell. "The survey continues. The Committee issues nothing — there is no excuse left to issue, and I won't spend a century of credibility rehearsing one; that much of the memorandum is adopted, whatever the minutes pretend. The assessment of the source continues under its assessor, and I will remind every office in this building that the procedure stands: nothing touches that list until the assessor's line is filled in. Mr Macmillan — the motion will be heard, and heard properly, before the Wizengamot in full session, not born in a briefing room out of one bad morning. Bring it in your own time, and bring it at your best." A pause; and then the practical question, arriving after everyone else's rhetoric, plain as a returned form. "One thing before we rise, since every road on this table is a kind of hiding. When they find a hidden thing — the finders — and learn it hid from them on purpose: what do they do next?"

Nobody answered.

"That's the paper I want," she said, "from anyone who has one," and rose, and the room rose with her.

* * *

Lupin was at the lifts before her, holding the grille the way he held doors for people he was about to inconvenience.

"Miss Quinn. Two weeks today," he said, pleasantly, as the gate closed on the two of them. "I've read every minute you've filed. They're the best-drafted documents on my desk — status, materials, propagation, everything a file could want except a direction. If I didn't know the drafting was yours, I'd say it was ours." The lift recited Level Two to itself. "Assessments have a shape by now, in my experience. Yours is a circle."

"Circles are a shape, sir."

"So they are," Lupin said, warm the whole time, which was the worst of it, and got out at Three, and turned in the bright corridor. "Wednesday, as usual. Do give it an arrow."

Alone from Level Three down, the lift recited the Ministry to her floor by floor, a country reading itself aloud to nobody, and Orla stood in it and did what she always did on the far side of fear. She took inventory.

Three doctrines had stood up in that room. The wipe — still walking, dead since the twenty-first of March, kept upright now mostly by the loyalty of good men to the meaning of their own careers; she had watched the Head of Accidents lean on a wall that was no longer there and had felt nothing but pity, and the pity frightened her more than his doctrine did. The seal — born this morning, argued best of the three, wrong in a way only the map could prove, and the map would prove it too late to matter. And a third, which had no faction, no motion and no name, and had entered the room exactly once, dressed as the Minister's last question — "what do the finders do next" — because the honest answer was: that depends on what they find. A fortress, or a door. Nobody had proposed the door. She had stood in the highest room she would ever stand in with the whole shape of the proposal held ready, indexed, ordered the way she ordered things when she was frightened — and had put none of it in the room, again, and had spent the ride down examining what she was becoming, like a label re-inked in a hand she did not entirely trust.

Because here was the morning's true finding, the one in nobody's minutes. Every doctrine at that table ran across the same small bridge. The survey could hold, the Wizengamot could debate, the Confederation could convene — and all of it, every option and every deferral, stood on one interim minute in one assessment file, renewed weekly in her registry hand: status ONGOING; recommendation none at this time; no action pending recommendation of assessor. The Minister had said it herself with the whole room minuting: nothing moves until the assessor's line is filled in. Her six quiet sentences had become infrastructure. The blank line in a locked drawer on Level Nine was load-bearing now, and the state stood on it, grateful — all factions at once — because a question still open was the one shelter everyone could agree to stand under.

She had built that shelter out of true sentences, for a man none of them had met.

That was the other inventory, and it was short. Three hours, the highest table in Britain, the map on its easel and the motion on its feet, the machinery of two worlds turning over one Tuesday's arithmetic — and in all of it, no one had asked a single question about the man. Not the source, not the author, not *subject, primary*. The man: tall and sorry about it, milk offered first, keeping one line open because closing it would have been tidy, and untrue.

Nobody else in that room had once looked at him.

"Level Nine," said the lift, with its usual satisfaction, "Department of Mysteries," and she went down into the winter, to keep the state's one open question open another week.
