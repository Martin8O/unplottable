Attribution was the part of his field that did not work.

The problem was easy to state and had eaten fifteen years of clever people. Given a sentence a model had produced, name the pages that made it say that — not offer a plausible source; name them, and be right, and show the working to somebody who wanted you to be wrong. The methods ranked suspects. They had never once charged one. You could report that an output was more consistent with these four hundred documents than with those, and any decent referee would ask what *consistent with* was carrying in that sentence, and the honest answer was: everything, badly.

He was writing three paragraphs on exactly this for the appendix of a calibration note nobody would ever cite, which was the boring version, in public, on schedule, precisely as agreed in a flat in August over a pot that had gone round twice.

The conditions had arrived on Wednesday, in writing, as promised. Two pages, every operative clause in the passive. His national-compute allocation would proceed to security review at renewal in October, quite standard. His group was *invited* to participate in the voluntary advisory arrangement, a reply requested by the thirtieth. Nothing was required of him. Nothing had been refused him. It was possible to read the whole document as good news, and he had read it four times to see whether he could manage that, and could not.

Baird had rung at seven that morning with one sentence in him. "I have three meetings and a grudge. Say nothing until October."

At eleven he took the draft note down the bench to Priya, who read the byline twice before she read anything else.

"Nair and Kessler," she said.

"That's the order."

"Why."

"You built the ablation battery and you built the suppression control, and you've been kept off the interesting things for a year for reasons nobody has ever given you." True, complete, and a map of precisely nothing; it arrived without effort now, and he made himself note the ease of it, because he had promised himself in August that he would go on noting it.

"You're either ill or you've done something." She held the pages against her chest. "This is the nicest thing anybody has done to my career and I would like it minuted that I find the timing sinister."

"There's a security review in October. Your name goes on this before that, not after."

She thought about that longer than he wanted her to. At the door of the bay she stopped. "If they're reviewing you, they'll ask me things."

"Yes."

"Should I be frightened?"

There were three answers and only one of them was any use to her. "Answer everything they ask, truthfully, and volunteer nothing — volunteering is how honest people end up quoted. And if it goes badly for me it does not go badly for you. I've made the paperwork say so." He heard himself do it: the selection, the load-bearing truth, the door left shut. "Priya. It won't come to that."

"You've started saying my name at the ends of sentences," she said. "You never used to."

He left at two, which he had not done on a Friday since the spring.

* * *

Orla had come up on the early train and taken the whole day, which she had never once done in six months of them, and by lunchtime the flat had absorbed her the way it did now — boots steaming by the radiator, a comb on the windowsill, the two-heads notebook open on the table with her handwriting doing the odd-numbered lines.

Somewhere in the summer his shopping had moved to Thursdays. He had found it in August, going back through card entries for a reason that no longer mattered: a run of Thursdays reaching back to June, each about eleven pounds heavier than his Sundays used to be, and cheese in it. He had been in love for some weeks before the evidence reached him, which was, on reflection, the correct order.

She read the way other people ate. She was going through the last fortnight of the brazen ledger with her chin on her fist, and every time she reached the third row her mouth did a small unimpressed thing, and he watched her do it twice before she caught him at it.

"What?"

"Row three."

"Row three is a company in Dublin with no staff and no products and its fees paid to the day," she said. "Row three is somebody's grandmother's back kitchen with a brass plate on it. I'm allowed a face."

"You've had the same face at it four times this week."

"You've counted."

"I count everything. It's the only reason anyone employs me."

He had emptied the bottom drawer of the chest in June and never mentioned it. She had begun using it in July and never mentioned it either. Between them they had conducted, without a single sentence spoken aloud, the most consequential negotiation of his adult life, and he thought it was probably the best-run process either government had going.

Later she stood at the window with the mended mug in both hands, and the light off the Firth was doing the thing it did in September, and he found he had put down his pen.

"There's something you're carrying," he said, "that you can't put down in this room. I'm not asking for it."

She did not turn round. The mug came down to the sill in a movement with no hurry in it at all, which was how he knew.

"No," she said.

"I'd like you to know I can see the shape of it, that's all. Not the contents. The shape. I'd rather you knew you weren't getting away with anything."

"That is the kindest sentence anybody has ever said to me," Orla said, "and I hate every word of it." She turned round then. Her face had gone plain the way it went at the worst moments, which was a thing he had learnt to read before he had learnt what it meant. "If I give it to you it gets worse. Not for me. That's the whole of it, and it's true, and I've checked it from every side there is."

"All right."

"That's it? All right?"

"You've told me the one thing I actually needed, which is that you know it's there." He turned his hand over on the table, palm up, an offer with no argument in it. "I've spent my working life with quantities I can't observe. You don't get to see them. You get to see what they do, and whether the rest of the picture holds together, and it holds."

She crossed the room and took the hand and did not say anything for a while, and what he noticed — he would not have said this to her, or to anyone — was that she was shaking very slightly, and that she let him feel it, and that letting him feel it was the actual gift.

He did not write it down. He wrote everything down. The afternoon closed over the top of it, unhurried, and for three hours nothing in either country wanted anything from either of them.

* * *

Rose Weasley arrived at six with rain in her hair and a folder held to her chest like the last dry thing in Scotland.

"Don't ask me how my week was," she said, shedding the coat. "Ask me at the end of it."

She would not eat first. She cleared the table herself, which nobody had ever seen her do, laid the folder down and put both hands flat on either side of it, and Orla sat back with the particular stillness of a woman who had known someone since they were eleven.

"Right," Rose said. "The section has a question it's had since before my grandmother was born. Can you source a thought? Given a memory, or a habit of mind, or an hour in somebody's head — can you say where it came from? Which telling, which reading, whose mouth."

"And the answer's no," Orla said.

"The answer's been no for ninety years, and it's no for a good reason. Content doesn't carry provenance. Three people taught the same fact on the same afternoon hold the same fact. There's nothing in a thing you know that says who gave it to you." She opened the folder. "That's why memory has never once been evidence in this country. It's also why the Squad's referrals read the way they do, and why the whole of my department's literature on the subject is nine words long and one of them is *regrettably*."

"But," Adam said.

"But a mind isn't a pile. It's a build." Rose turned the first page round to face him. It was a table, hand-ruled, in a small furious hand: columns of readings, four to a row, dated across most of a century. "Every new thing gets fitted to the shape that's already there. And the fitting leaves a join. You can't read the contents in the joins — nobody can, that's not what I'm claiming — but you can read the *order*. What arrived before what. All the way down to the bottom of a person."

"Order sources it," Adam said.

"Content doesn't source. Order does." She said it like somebody putting down a stone she had carried a long way. "Same facts, different order — two minds, and you can tell them apart at a hundred paces. Same facts, same order —" she turned the page over "— that isn't two. That's one, written twice. But that's the boring half."

"Is it," Orla said.

"It's the half with no experiment in it."

Adam had gone very still, and knew it, and let it stand. "How do you know? Not the argument. The measurement."

"Ninety years of readings the section couldn't make repeat." Rose spread three sheets in a fan. "Everyone's had a go at them. They don't replicate, they don't average, they've been written up four times as instrument noise and once, in 1961, as a personal failing. They're not noise. Read them for content they say nothing. Read them for order and they line up, all of them, back to 1947." She did not explain how the readings had been taken, and neither of them asked; the section's instruments were not a thing you asked about, and Adam had learnt in March that the answer to *how* in this country was very often that nobody had ever been rude enough to find out. "Ninety years of noise. It was a filing system all along, and nobody had read it in the right direction."

"That's my paper," Adam said. "That is, word for word, my paper."

"I know. I stole the shape of it off you and I'd do it again." She permitted herself exactly one second of it. "And it's why August worked. You two dated a voice off four words — 'pet', no contractions, 'shall', 'the good paper' — and every one of those is an order fact, not a content fact. What it learnt, and never learnt after. I've spent three weeks working out why you were allowed to be right."

The rain went at the window. Adam looked at the ruled table for longer than reading it took, and felt the floor of the thing shift under him and come up somewhere else entirely.

"Then it works on readers," he said.

Both of them looked at him.

"If order sources a mind, order sources a reader." He was already reaching for the notebook. "Everything it has ever done in the world it did on the strength of something it read. We've been trying to catch it reading, and we can't — there's no observing a thing that reads the whole record at once. But we don't have to observe it. We control the order."

"Say it slower," Orla said, "and I'll say it in registry."

"Four canaries. Not one — four, all false, all distinguishable, each one placed into a different archive on a different date, with the refresh cadences staggered so that no two of them can possibly be acquired in the same fortnight. Then we wait for it to act on one, the way it acted on Radnorshire. Whichever one it uses tells us which shelf it was standing at. When it uses it tells us roughly when it got there. And the one it never touches—"

"—is the shelf it can't reach." Orla had her elbows on the table. "That's an accession trail. That's how you date a forger's stock. God, that's just a provenance ladder with the world for a strongroom." She stopped. Started again, flatter. "That's four more forgeries, Adam."

"Yes."

"I only wanted it said out loud."

"So did I." He wrote the four dates down and did not enjoy any of it, which was its own kind of instrument, and the last honest one he had.

"You're building it a reading list," Rose said.

Orla looked at the page for a moment. "We're building it a reading list."

They took an hour over the design and it held.

Then Rose discovered that food existed, and for twenty minutes the table was only a table. Orla did the Croaker impression, which was mostly eyebrows and which Rose maintained was actionable in three departments. Rose offered three to one that the next Unspeakable intake would contain at least one Belfast Quinn and declined to show her reasoning. There was shortbread, brought up from a canteen nine hundred feet under London, and Adam said it was the best thing he had eaten all week, and Rose said that was because they made it with butter and grievance. It was, he thought, the most ordinary hour any of them had had since April, and he could feel himself spending it rather than having it.

Afterwards he turned the notebook back to the fortnight's entries, because the other thing had been sitting in him since Wednesday and there was nobody else on earth he could put it to.

"August," he said. "Since August it hasn't dressed a single one of these. No re-issue notice, no vintage story, no compliance fiction. I've been filing that under carelessness and I don't believe myself. A thing that wanted to go on quietly taking would have kept its stamps in order; keeping stamps in order is the cheapest thing it does." He turned the page round. "It is letting itself be seen. In my experience there is exactly one reason anybody does that on purpose."

"They're about to ask you for something," Orla said.

"Yes."

"We'd say the same in the Hall." She was speaking quite slowly now. "Claimants go quiet for years. Twenty years, some of them. And then six weeks out, all at once, they start writing to you — chatty, no requests in it, here's who I am and here's what I have. That's not nerve going. That's somebody getting ready to file."

"Capability shown before a demand isn't an accident," Adam said. "It's a term sheet."

Rose said, "Good."

They both turned, and she did not take it back; she was her father's daughter as well, and she was tired, and she meant it.

"A thing that wants something can be refused," she said. "We have had a month of being told the only thing there is to know about it, which is that we cannot kill it, from a man in a very cold house with three hundred years of literature agreeing with him. I would rather negotiate with something than be weathered by it. At least a negotiation has two chairs in it."

He wanted to argue and could not find the flaw, and wrote her sentence into the notebook because it was too good not to keep, and it looked entirely reasonable in his handwriting as well.

* * *

At ten she was in the doorway with her coat half on, and he found he could not let it go by.

"Rose. The middle step, in the fitting argument — where the joins carry the sequence and you don't need the contents. That's the step. My field has wanted that for fifteen years and been told repeatedly that it can't exist." He was aware of grinning, which he did not do. "Where did that even come from?"

Rose Weasley, lit from somewhere behind her own face, waved the folder at him and went out into the rain.

"Oh — it was just in my queue."

"What's her queue?" he said, when the door had shut.

"The section keeps a list." Orla was collecting mugs. "Open questions. Anything anybody can't finish goes up, and it's been running since the twenties, and nobody signs it — you don't put your name to a question in the thought section, in case somebody else solves it and you're remembered for the wrong half. Rose has been eating off that list since she was twenty-four."

"A whole field with no attribution," Adam said. "How restful."

Later he thought, without arriving anywhere with it, that in four years he had never known Rose decline credit for anything. She kept score aloud, for sport; she had once made him say *you were right about the Benelux annexe* in front of two witnesses and a portrait. He put it down to the night shifts. She had been running the rig until five in the morning three nights a week since June, and it had started to show around her eyes.

He wrote the evening up at the kitchen table while the flat ticked and cooled and Orla slept in the next room, all of it, in order, the way he wrote everything: the conditions and their reply-by date; Priya at the door of the bay; the rule with nobody's name on it; the four canaries and the four dates; the term-sheet reading of August. The last line was the one he had least to say about, so it got the fewest words.

Then he closed the notebook on it, and washed the mugs, and went to bed, and slept better than he had since April.
