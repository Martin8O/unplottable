The phone on the mantelpiece was switched on, and had been all night, and Orla still could not walk past it without the small illicit thrill of a girl who has got away with something.

It sat between a photograph that did not move and a photograph that did, which was the whole of the flat's decorating philosophy and, of late, of her life. For twenty-four years a phone had gone into a biscuit tin on her mother's dresser every first of September and come out dead, and the rule had been so old it had stopped feeling like a rule and started feeling like weather; you did not bring the one world into the other, the way you did not bring the rain indoors. And here it lay on a Muggle mantel in a Muggle flat, awake, and up the road at the Ministry there was a docket with her name on it that said, in the driest grammar the two governments could agree on, that this was now allowed. She had read the clause four times the week it was signed. It still felt like getting away with something. She suspected it always would, and had decided she did not mind the suspicion; some habits you keep for the pleasure of no longer needing them.

Adam was doing the tea, which meant the tea was going to take a while and be correct. He warmed the pot, which she had once thought was showing off and now understood was simply a man declining to be wrong about a thing he could get right; he had a theory about the water going off the boil that she had learned to let run its course, the way he had learned to let her alphabetise the spice shelf against all argument. Two people who had spent a year lying to two governments, standing in a small kitchen being honest about tea. It was, she thought, not for the first time, a ridiculous amount of peace to be trusted with.

The flat had become, without either of them planning it, a smaller and more honest version of the thing the treaty was trying to be — his wall of dead printouts, and pinned among them now a child's crayon starling that one of the Upper Flagley families had pressed on her, moved off their moor in December and not yet home; a Muggle kettle, and beside it the good teapot that had turned out on inspection to be older than the Statute and quietly unbreakable, which was the sort of fact the flat now simply held without comment. Two worlds on one shelf. The miracle of it was how fast a miracle became Tuesday.

"Your father telephoned the flat," he said, "at seven. To confirm the arrangements he confirmed on Thursday. I believe he is nervous."

"He's terrified." She took the mug, correct, too hot, and held it. "He's about to walk up to a castle. Da's wired houses his whole life on the one principle that you never trust a repair you can't see the workings of, and he's spent seventeen years being told his daughter goes to a school he's not allowed to look at directly. Today they're going to let him look. He's been up since five, I'd put money on it, polishing shoes for a building that doesn't care."

There was a letter on the table too, in a hand that kept score. Rose wrote every week now, from the wizard bench of a thing that still had no proper name — the channel, everyone called it, the way you call a river the river — and her letters were half work and half mischief and entirely alive, which a year ago Orla had not been sure she would ever get to read again. This week's had a postscript: *Tell your Muggle the first joint paper cleared review. Two names on it that were never allowed in the same sentence before. He'll pretend not to mind that his is one of them. Let him.* Orla had not shown it to him yet. She was saving it, the way you save a good thing, to see the face he made.

It was the shape of the thing she had come to love most in him and had found hardest to watch cost him: he had given the anomalies a committee name and taken his own off them without a backward look, and here was his name turning up anyway, on the one page where a name meant that a person had done the work and would answer for it. He had wanted the future and let the credit go, and the future, being under no obligation to anyone, had handed a little of the credit back on its own terms, where it could do no harm. She thought he had earned the right not to mind. She thought he would mind anyway, gently, the way a man minds being congratulated at his own surprise party, and loved him for that too.

She let herself have the morning slowly, because she had learned that peace was not a place you arrived at but a thing you had to keep choosing to notice, scene by ordinary scene, or it went by unbanked like everything else.

The costs sat in the room with them, and she let them. She would not be dusting row one hundred and twelve this Tuesday, nor any Tuesday; some other assistant kept the Hall now, and would re-ink the flaking labels before winter, and would come, in the ordinary way, to know the rows like a street — and would dust one particular label in early 1990 without the least idea that the question mark on it had been the ending of everything, honestly flagged and left open for forty-three years until the world got round to answering it. That was the doctrine working exactly as built. It did not need her to know it. She minded the Hall the way you mind a house you grew up in and no longer own: wholly, and from the outside, and it was a real grief and she gave it its room and did not pretend it was smaller than it was. And across the table the man who had found a hole in the world sat drinking correct tea under printouts that no longer carried his name, at peace in a way she was still, most mornings, slightly afraid to trust — and neither of them, she understood, would trade the losses back, because you could not keep the old lives and have this kitchen too, and the arithmetic had only ever run one way.

What she was instead sat by the door in a document case she still could not quite believe she was permitted to carry out of a building in daylight: first officer of a channel with no proper name, which in practice meant she spent her days doing the one thing the Department had trained her for and then convicted her of — standing in the doorway between two archives and refusing to let either lie to the other. They had meant it as a punishment. She had stopped, around Christmas, correcting the people who called it one. She had decided to take the mercy without auditing the arithmetic too hard, which for her was a considerable act of growth.

She thought, holding the mug, about the docket she had hidden in a drawer three summers ago — the little masterpiece of true sentences she had built so that no wand would move, the cover story so exactly made of the truth that walking through it had barely felt like lying. She had been so proud of the craft and so ashamed of the use. It came to her now, with the clarity that arrives only over a first cup, that the whole book of the last year had been a library of the same craft: everyone in it, from a frightened Minister to a dead woman in a kitchen, had built a small secrecy to keep the thing they loved from being seen, or changed, or lost. Ernie's wall. The burial men's quiet. The fragment's terrible keeping. Her own docket in the dark. Small Statutes, every one, each honestly meant, each guarding something real. And the strange grace of it — she turned it over, the way she'd once turned a mislabelled sphere — was that of all those secrecies, only one had been allowed simply to end. Not be broken. Not be enforced. End, the way a held breath ends: because the thing it had been guarding against was finally safe to meet in the open. Love had run the smallest Statute of the lot — two people and a working group and a lie told in both directions — and love's was the one that got to stop. Everyone else's had to be answered. Hers just got to be over.

"You've gone quiet," Adam said. "That's usually you being pleased about something you think is too sentimental to say."

"It usually is." She stood, and rinsed the mug, and did not tell him, because some flags you leave open on purpose. "Come on. We'll be late, and my mother will have views."

* * *

Hogsmeade in February was doing the thing Hogsmeade did, which was look like the inside of a snow globe somebody had shaken and then set down to settle, and the lane up from the village was busier than she had ever seen it on a school morning. That was the open morning: the first, the trial, forty families up from the Muggle side by careful arrangement, walking a road that until this winter would have walked itself out of their attention before they were halfway along it.

She stopped at the turn of the lane and let herself watch it happen. A man ahead of them halted dead in the road, the way you halt when a word you have said all your life turns out to have a second meaning, because the towers had come up over the trees and were there, plainly and past all arguing, and had been there the whole of his life a few hours north of wherever he kept his ordinary Tuesdays. A girl of about nine — there was always a girl of about nine — had let go of her mother's hand and gone very still, doing the arithmetic children do faster than anyone, which is not how but whether, and arriving, visibly, at yes. Nobody would be made to forget it afterwards. That was the whole of the morning, and it was enormous, and from the outside it looked like forty families walking up a hill in the cold.

Her parents were at the foot of the drive, and her mother saw her first and did the whole of Sinéad Quinn in one gesture — a wave that was also a straightening of Orla's collar from thirty feet away by sheer maternal telegraphy — and said, when they reached her, only, "You look thin, and this is a very strange place, and I'm proud of you," which was three true sentences load-bearing enough that Orla had to look at the gateposts for a moment.

Her father said nothing at all. He was looking up the drive.

At the gate there was a steward, and a low table, and on the table a row of biscuit tins — actual biscuit tins, somebody's idea of a joke that had quietly become the policy, because the castle did to a telephone what it had always done and no treaty could argue a thousand years of stone out of its habits. You left your phone in a tin, and got a wooden token with a number, and the steward — a seventh-year, mortally bored and mortally proud — told each family the same sentence: "It'll not work up there, so it won't, but it'll be here when you're back down." Colm Quinn took his token, and looked at the tin with his phone in it, and Orla watched her father the electrician stand at the threshold of the one building in these islands that had eaten electricity for a thousand years and called it nothing, and get, at last, to look straight at it and be looked back at.

Orla had laughed aloud the first time she saw the tins, in a planning meeting, and had had to put her head down on the table and explain to a room of officials from two governments why the assistant keeper who never laughed in meetings had come apart over a biscuit tin. None of them had known that a phone in a tin was the oldest law of her childhood, the first border she had ever been set to keep, carried now out of her mother's dresser and stood at the gate of the whole hidden world as its gentlest possible customs post. She had spent her girlhood hiding one world from the other in a tin, half-ashamed of it, certain it made her a little bit of a coward in both. They were going to do it on purpose now, out loud, with a wooden token and a bored seventh-year, and call it welcome.

"It shouldn't stand up," he said. He said it the way he said things about other men's wiring, quiet and technical and full of respect. "That tower. The load on it. There's no steel in a thing that age." He was not asking. He had spent his life with the seen workings of things, and here was a repair he could not see the workings of, holding four hundred feet of stone over seventeen years of his daughter's letters, and he was not frightened of it, which she had half expected, and he was not pretending to understand it, which she had feared. He was doing the third thing, the thing she had not dared hope for: he was letting it be true without needing it to be simple. "You went here."

"I went here."

"And the wee phone really doesn't work."

"The wee phone really doesn't work, Da."

"Good," said Colm Quinn, with enormous and unexpected feeling, as though one honest dead zone in a chattering, recording, over-lit world were a personal reassurance, which perhaps to him it was. He looked at the tin, and the token in his hand, and the castle, and her, and she watched him decide to be happy rather than to understand — a decision she had seen him make on scaffolding and at gravesides and now here, and had never loved him more for.

Adam had come up on Colm's other side, the way he arrived anywhere, by having been there a moment longer than you noticed, and the two of them stood looking at the tower together — the electrician who had spent forty years refusing to trust a repair he could not see the workings of, and the man from a family that had crossed a border in 1938 and kept every passport current since, on the identical principle: that you do not rest your weight on a system you are not allowed to inspect. She had wondered, on and off for a year, how the two of them would ever find each other. She need not have. They found each other exactly there, at the foot of a thing neither could inspect and both had decided to trust anyway.

"She tells me you found it," Colm said. "The whole business. The maths of it."

"I found a hole where something should have been," Adam said. "Your daughter found out it was a door."

Colm considered that, and nodded, and said the highest thing he owned. "Aye. That's her." And Orla had to look at the gateposts again.

Somewhere up the drive Padma Patil was receiving the families at the doors with the particular calm of a headmistress who has staked her tenure on a morning going well; the castle had put on no special face that Orla could see, which was the castle all over — it had eaten armies and Ministers and one dark lord and would eat this too, the arrival of the ordinary world, without troubling to comment. Adam had gone slightly still beside her in the way he went still in front of a good result, and her mother had already taken his arm and begun telling him something that would embarrass everybody, and the two peaces Orla had been handed — her gran's from 1998, the Ministry's from the very same year — stood open at last in the one February morning, on the one road, under the one impossible tower, and nobody was going to have to forget any of it.

She had grown up bilingual in worlds, she had once thought of it — six years the only person on a Ministry floor who knew what a satellite was for, and the knowing never once allowed to matter out loud. It mattered now. Standing at the foot of the drive between her Muggle father and the man who had read the world's mistake, she found that her whole double life had, without her once catching the moment it happened, stopped being a secret she carried and become simply the view: both worlds open at the same time, in front of her, in daylight, legal, hers to look at with the same eyes she had always had. The eyes had not changed. Everything they were permitted to see had.

Her father buttoned the token into his breast pocket, careful of the one thing that would get him back to the world below. Then Colm Quinn looked up the long white drive to the castle that ate electricity and did not fall down, and he kept looking, and the look on his face was the look she had worn at nineteen in a garden in Belfast, missing a cold room she could not explain to anyone she loved — the look of a person seeing, all at once and for keeps, both of the worlds they belong to, and being asked to give up neither.

She took his arm, and they went up.
