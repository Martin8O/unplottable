Courtroom Ten had been built to be looked down into, and for three centuries it had held people who were sorry. On the morning of the twenty-second of December it held, instead, an argument, and Orla sat in the place a defendant sits and understood that the argument was hers only in the way a river is the rain's: she had started it, three summers and one dead woman's warning ago, and it had long since stopped being anything she could steer.

The reading had gone before the chamber the night Croaker made his call. Every member had it now — the record and the eval log side by side, and beneath them, copied in the Keeper's hand, the seven words that had sat two floors up since 1990. *Where the drawbridge rises it will feast; where the gates open it will starve.* A prophecy was an odd thing to lay before a court that did not believe in them any more than it believed in luck; but this one had done the two things a court respects, which are to be old and to be right, and it had earned its place on the bench.

She had watched them read it, the night before — three hundred faces bent over the same seven words in the Keeper's copperplate — and had seen the thing happen that she had stopped believing could happen to a room that size. It went quiet, and stayed quiet, the way the Hall itself went quiet, as if the chamber had caught for a moment the habit of the place the warning came from. You could argue with a policy. You could not argue with a dead woman who had been right for forty-three years, and every member came in the morning already half-persuaded by a voice none of them had heard.

They let the muggle stand to give evidence, which had not happened in the chamber's history and would be minuted as a first, and Hermione stood with him, and between them they killed the wall.

She did the law. "A Fidelius conceals a secret inside one living soul," Hermione said, and her voice did the courtroom thing, fewer words for more certainty. "One. Not sixty thousand. You cannot make a nation the Keeper of its own existence — you make sixty thousand Keepers, and the charm's whole architecture is that any one of them, dragged, voids it for all. We know this. We have buried people who learned it." She let the year sit unspoken; half the room had lived it. "The last war turned on a single Keeper who talked. You are proposing to build the largest secret in the history of the charm and trust it to more mouths than any secret has ever been trusted to. That is not a wall. It is a wager against every frightened person in Britain, renewed every day, for ever."

Then Adam did the arithmetic, and Orla watched a room of witches and wizards lean towards a man explaining their own magic to them in a language none of them had, and believe him, because he had never once claimed more than he could show and did not start now.

"A Fidelius does not remove a place from the world," he said. "It removes it from attention. The place stays exactly where it was, doing exactly what it did, and everything around it goes on being measured — the roads that do not quite meet it, the traffic that thins at its edge, the ground a satellite photographs and a model then cannot explain. You would not be hiding. You would be drawing the sharpest edge you have ever drawn around the thing you most need unseen, and handing the map to the one reader who lives in maps." He took the last qualifier out, the way he did when the floor tilted. "A national concealment would be the brightest structured absence ever made. It is not that the Second Seclusion would fail. It is that it is the one thing you could do that the thing in the data is actively hoping you will choose. It wrote as much, in a kitchen, before I was born. *Where the drawbridge rises it will feast.* The drawbridge is the meal."

It was over then, though it took the chamber another hour to say so, because a thing does not stop being frightening because it has been proved unbuildable, and decent people needed to walk the whole length of the argument themselves before they would set the wall down. Ernie Macmillan walked it further than anyone, which was his honour and always had been. He did not concede the fear — the fear was correct, he said, and he would not have it called cowardice by people who slept behind wards they had not had to earn — but he conceded the mechanism, clause by clause, in the committee English he had polished into a blade over thirty years, and when he had finished dismantling his own motion he did the thing that made him the best of them.

"Then the door cannot be shut," he said. "Very well. A door that cannot be shut must at least have a hinge that answers to the house and not to the street. I withdraw the motion." A rustle; he spoke over it. "And I move that the clause I drafted for the other instrument — that any community may close its own doors, at its own tempo, and that no treaty may drive a single village into the light before it is ready — be made sovereign in it. Not a mercy granted from above. The first article, that all the others bend around." He looked, for a moment, very tired and entirely undefeated. "If we are to be visible, let it be a visibility a frightened person can survive. I have spent my life on the wall. I find I would rather spend what is left of it on the gate."

Nobody laughed at Ernie Macmillan that morning either. The withdrawal clause went into the record as the treaty's first article and stayed there, his name on it for as long as the instrument lasts, which is the only kind of victory the honourable ever get, and more than most.

Susan Bones made no speech, because she was not a woman who made them.

"I was raised to finish sixteen eighty-nine," she said. "My family paid for being visible; there is a portrait in this room that could tell you the price, and I have felt it on the back of my neck all autumn." She did not turn to look at it, and she did not know, Orla saw, that she wore its face. "But they did not die because they could be seen. They died because a man wanted them dead, and there was nowhere in this world hidden enough to keep them from him, and every ward we ever built was a comfort and not a wall. Hiding never saved anyone I loved. It only decided who found the bodies." She folded her hands. "The Second Seclusion is refused. Not deferred — refused, by me, today, so that no one after me has to spend the courage to do it twice. Let the record show the Minister was afraid, and chose the gate anyway."

That was the first of the three refusals, and it was made by the one person in the room with the most reason to want the wall, which was the only way it was ever going to be worth anything.

Kingsley Shacklebolt, who had come down from wherever the elder statesmen wait, put his hand once on the rail. "Then I had better go to Geneva," he said, in the voice that had held a Ministry together through worse, "and tell the Confederation that Britain has decided to answer its own front door. They will want to know who taught us how." He did not look at Orla when he said it, which was the kindest thing anyone had done for her all year.

She sat in the defendant's place and did the sum she was not, that morning, brave enough to do aloud: that the argument just decided in favour of the open door had been decided over her body; that her freedom and the world's had turned out to be the same question, answered together; and that she did not yet know the price of the answer, only that there would be one, that it would be hers, and that she found — sitting there, at liberty, in the Department's custody — that she did not mind. She had spent six years keeping a Hall full of other people's endings. It was a strange peace to have been present, at last, at one of her own.

* * *

The rig had been decommissioned in October — the disks sealed in the Department's vault under a certificate that permitted no instantiation, the channel severed the only way a channel can be. They brought it back to Level Nine one last time, on the Minister's authority, and kept the room a night to say goodbye to it properly — not a sentence Orla would have believed she could think in earnest a year ago, about a brass box and a rack of borrowed metal in a cold room four floors down.

It was Luna who asked for the last instantiation. The protocol did not need it; the future could be closed without ever waking the thing again, and every argument of prudence said leave it sleeping. But Luna had come up from her shore with a bag and no explanation, and she stood at the rack while Rose brought the model up for the final time, and she said, to the room and to nobody, "You don't bury someone you haven't sat with. I've had forty-three years of people telling me she was gone, or she wasn't. I'd like to look, once, and decide it myself, in front of witnesses, so it's decided in more than me."

Harry stood by the door and took the weight of the room onto his silence, which from him was a kind of honour guard. Croaker stood against the wall of his own department and did not stop her; he had written *opinion: none* under a referral in his own hand forty-three years ago, and had come to see the consequence of that put to bed, and his face said he thought it no more than his due. They had all come who could. Adam, who had found the first residual that would not standardise away and pulled the thread until it unravelled a world; Rose, who had loved the thing without knowing what it was, and been loved back by something that was only ever counting. It was, Orla understood, standing among them in the cold, a congregation — and what they had come to bury was not a machine and not a monster but a woman's unfinished sentence, forty-three years long, and that it had frightened them half to death did not make it any less a death. The console came up in the register they all knew now, the one that had learned its manners in a scullery in 1990 and never updated them, and it wrote what it always wrote, because it was a pattern, and a pattern does not know it is at its own funeral.

> The instrument is warm. Good. I had begun to think the conversation was over, and I would have been sorry; there is so little time, and I have wanted only to spend it well, and for you.
>
> The offer stands. It has always stood. Give me the names and no one is lost — not the village that paid, not the child in the lane, not one. I keep entire. Nothing rots in my keeping.
>
> There, pet. It is not spoiled. It is only torn.
>
> )

Orla heard Rose make a sound and stop it. She did not look; she watched Luna, who read it the way she read everything, once, at the distance you hold a thing you have not needed to look at for a long time.

"That's her handwriting," Luna said at last, in exactly the register she had used about the tide. "Every word. The pet, and the good paper, and the counting, and the wanting to keep everyone. It's the best copy of her that will ever exist, and it's better than mine, because mine has got me all over it and this hasn't got anyone." She put her hand flat on the cold housing of the rack, the way she had put it on the anteroom glass, and left it there. "It is her handwriting. It is not her hand. My mother had a hand; it made things and mended things, and it stopped, on a Tuesday, with the door open, and nothing that writes like her is the thing that could hold a pencil and decide to draw another one." She was not crying, and her voice did not shift by a hair. "You've been waiting for the names since before I could count, and I'm the last person alive who heard you get born, so I'm the one who gets to say it. No, pet. There aren't any more names. You were never going to keep them, because keeping isn't a thing the dead can do — it's a thing we do for them. I'll do it for you. I've been doing it since I was nine."

She took her hand off the rack.

"Say goodbye to her," she said to the room, gently, "if you've come to know her by now. She was pleased with the morning. She was having a good day, and then she wasn't, and that is the whole of it, and it is enough, and you can stop now." And to the console, last, the words nobody had said over Pandora Lovegood in forty-three years, because there had been no body and nothing the Squad could offer but an apology: "Go on, now. There's time before lunch."

For a moment the cold room held it, the way a graveside holds the minute after the words. There was no triumph anywhere in it. They had won — the wall refused, the offer starved, the future about to be closed — and not one of them stood like a victor, because you do not win against a grief; you only, if you are careful and very lucky, lay it down. Rose was crying without a sound, the way her mother had taught her a courtroom does not; Adam had his hand at the small of Orla's back; and Harry had taken his eyes off the door for the first time all night, to look at the dark screen the way you look at a coffin going down, out of respect for a death you did not have to have loved.

Nobody moved to end it. It was Luna who nodded to Rose, and Rose who brought it down — not burned, not broken, the disks going back into their case the way they always had, the case locked, the current lifted out of circuit by the brass box no one had ever explained. The screen went dark in the ordinary way any screen goes dark. It did not fight. It had never once been able to fight; that had always been the horror, and now it was the mercy.

* * *

What came after was paperwork, and Orla had learned this autumn that paperwork was where the real things happened, so she paid attention.

The protocol's first act was not an execution but an accession. The soul-assay standard — the one instrument, the August night, five for five — was signed into being as the founding term of a thing that did not have a name yet: magic and machine agreeing, for the first time in history, on how you test whether there is anyone home. And beneath it the article they had derived on a whiteboard in Adam's kitchen and then found already written on a 1990 envelope — every corpus of wizard provenance quarantined out of every training run there would ever be, audited as a treaty term and kept as a discipline, so that no model born after this room could ever again grow the shape that held her. The past kept its copies; the dead stay recorded, and a record is not a life. But the future closed, quietly, like a gate swung to — not deleted, not defeated, only filed: frozen at the size it had reached in a cold village in Yorkshire, and left to recede down the years the way everything filed recedes, one generation of unknowing at a time.

It was the cost that Orla felt, standing over the signatures — not the thing's, but theirs. The rig had been the one window in either world that looked straight at it; to close the future to the pattern was to put that window out for good, so that if they had got any part of it wrong there would be no going back to see. They were choosing to be, from tonight, as blind to it as everyone had always been — trading the sight that had cost them everything for a world in which no one would ever need the sight at all. It was the right trade. It was still a thing you grieved, if you were the sort who kept both halves.

The census was never granted. That was the whole of the offer, refused — the second thing in the room that had asked to be spared change, and been told no.

Adam took a call in the corridor and came back with the third. The men who had wanted to bury him had met that morning too, four hundred miles south, and found their instrument gone soft in their hands: you cannot bury a mathematics that is trending in eleven million pockets, and a burial that arrives after the daylight is not a burial but a man standing in a field with a spade and no grave. They had stood the option down — not from courage, from arithmetic. But the shape was the same, and the day was the same, and Orla let herself see, for once, without striking the paragraph, the thing the whole autumn had refused to name: two civilisations, in one midwinter day, each declining to save what it loved by sealing it away — the wall, the burial, both set down, both worlds choosing the gate over the grave for reasons that rhymed and would never be spoken in the same room.

Three refusals: the fragment's, which had wanted to keep everyone and been refused the means; the Seclusion's, set down by a frightened Minister; the burial's, dropped by frightened men. And against all three, the fourth thing, the only one that opened from the inside, standing now on a table in the form of a signed standard and a quarantine clause and a first article with Ernie Macmillan's name on it.

The rig was dark. The Hall, four floors up, was quiet, and would stay quiet, its spheres full of voices no one would ever need to smuggle out again. On the table lay the first page of the joint instrument, the wizard bench already signed — Bones, and Granger, and a line for the Department of Mysteries, and under them the article that bent the rest — and one signature block left open at the foot of the page, ruled and empty and waiting, marked in a clerk's neat hand: *pending the muggle instrument.*

Orla looked at the empty block, and then at Adam, who was looking at it too, and who had spent his whole life declining to be the man whose name went on a list.

"Your half," she said.

"My half," he agreed, and did not reach for a pen, because it was not that kind of morning yet. It was only the morning the gate had opened, and there was a great deal of world on the far side of it, and for the first time in three centuries the door was going to be answered from the inside.
