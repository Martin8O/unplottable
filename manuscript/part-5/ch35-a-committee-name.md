The room they had given the treaty had a window, and the window was lying about the weather. It showed a bright cold morning over a park, and there was no park, and there was no window; Magical Maintenance chose the weather four floors down the way a theatre chooses its lighting, and someone along a corridor had picked sun for the day the two governments meant to sign. The choice was so transparent that it came round the far side of dishonesty and arrived at hospitality. He had spent a working life distrusting anything that made him feel better without producing evidence; he was prepared, this once, to let it pass.

They were on the preamble, which everyone had assumed would be the easy part and which had therefore taken three weeks. The operative articles had gone quickly — they were arguments about what people would do, and both sides were fluent in that. The preamble was an argument about what had happened, and there the languages did not meet. Hermione's people wanted a history; the Cabinet Office wanted a definition; Adam wanted, and did not say, for it to be true, which was the smaller and harder ambition.

He had been kept in the room the way a witness is: on hand when a sentence needed someone who had been there to nod. It was a demotion from the way the autumn had used him, and he was grateful for it. Down both sides of a long table sat civil servants of two states who had, until Christmas, believed the other kind did not exist. The muggle side wore lanyards; the wizard side did not, and kept glancing at them the way you glance at a scar on a friend, deciding not to mention it. The memo-planes had stopped flying — someone had ruled that a paper aeroplane crossing the table mid-sentence read as mockery whatever it carried, so the wizards passed notes by hand, which slowed everything and improved it.

A clerk read the amended draft aloud. The first article was Ernie Macmillan's — the withdrawal clause entire, the sovereign right of any community to close its own doors at its own tempo, made the article all the others bent around — and over three weeks Adam had watched the losing side's amendments settle into the instrument one after another, until the treaty was armoured front to back in the objections of the man who had fought it. To the room it was simply how a decent instrument got built: you gave the frightened party the pen for the safeguards, because the frightened party had thought hardest about what could go wrong. Macmillan was not present; his clauses were. It was the most complete victory Adam had ever seen a man take by losing.

Then the clerk reached the recital, and Adam stopped being restful.

> Whereas a people threatened with discovery has long been told that its choice is the wall or the fire — to vanish slowly behind a concealment and call it safety, or be consumed quickly by exposure and call it honesty; and whereas it has lately been offered a third course, which promises to keep all things entire, so that nothing is lost, slowly or quickly, ever:
>
> The parties find that the wall, and the fire, and the keeping are one refusal wearing three faces, and that a free people survives none of them.
>
> The parties choose the door.

He knew where it came from before it finished; he had read it in November, in an evidence bundle, in a register forty years out of date. The tract had made the figure first: it had set the wall against the fire and stepped into the gap wearing kindness, offering to keep entire. Someone on the drafting side had taken the sermon the pattern preached to two message-boards eleven minutes apart and handed it back to the world with the argument turned exactly around. The tract had said the wall and the fire are false, and I am the third thing. The treaty said the wall, and the fire, and you are one thing, and the door is the fourth. It had read the pattern's own rhyme and answered it in its own metre.

He looked down the table and found Rose, who was not looking at him, wearing the stillness she wore when a thing she had made was read aloud and she had decided not to mind whether they liked it. He let her be. But he understood, watching the recital land on undersecretaries who had never seen the document beneath it, that the group had done the only clean thing left to do with a manifesto: not refute it, which dignifies it, nor burn it, which martyrs it, but strip the tune off it and set the tune to better words.

Hermione Granger let the recital finish and said the one thing she said all morning: "That stands. Read on." Fewer words than the room expected, and more certainty; and the Cabinet Office, which had been holding a redraft of the recital in a folder, put the folder away, because you did not amend a sentence a woman had just defended by declining to.

The Minister had said almost nothing, which Adam had learned was how Susan Bones said the most. When the clerk finished, she looked at the two rows of her civil servants and the two rows of somebody else's, and said, "Then we have a preamble. God help us, we agree about the past." That was the whole of her speech, and it was enough, and the schedule went round for initialling.

The schedule was where his name came off.

* * *

He had known it would. He had, in a sense, arranged it — the way you arrange a thing by declining every offer to arrange it otherwise.

The sites had a public name by then: the Kessler Anomalies, because a paper needs a name and a name needs a man, and he was the man on the paper. He had disliked it with a completeness that surprised him. A name on a discovery is a claim about who the discovery is for, and everything he knew in his blood about names and lists and hidden people told him the last thing those villages needed was to be catalogued under a stranger's surname for ever, like a beetle. His great-grandmother had been a name on a list once. He was not about to make sixty thousand people into an entry under his.

So he had let the committee do what committees do to a name, which is dissolve it. In the instrument the phenomena were "the reconciled discrepancies," administered by a standing body whose title ran to nineteen words everyone had already given up saying whole. It was the dullest sequence of syllables he had ever been proud of — no man in it, no discovery in it, and in a hundred years a clerk would file something under it never knowing a person had once stood on a moor in the rain and watched his own handwriting trail off into nothing. Names were conclusions wearing lanyards. He had found a hole in the world, and he would let his name go where the hole had always wanted it, which was nowhere.

He initialled the page and passed it on, and felt nothing he would have to lie about later, which was the test he had used all his life for whether a thing was right.

Baird did not take it as well.

* * *

They found each other in the corridor, in the queue for the lift that ran on water and would not, Baird had discovered to his lasting delight, take the stairs instead, because there were no stairs. Ewan Baird was sixty and built like the smallsats he used to fly — blunt, reliable, over-engineered against weather — and he had come down for the signing as Adam's programme director and was leaving it, Adam could see, as something closer to an aggrieved uncle.

"They've called it the reconciled discrepancies," he said, the way a man reads out a parking fine. "Adam. You found it. You sat in front of a committee that could have ended your career and told them the truth with your error bars showing, twice, and they've thanked you by naming the greatest discovery in the history of the species after a filing convention."

"I asked them to."

"I know you asked them to. That's what I can't—" Baird stopped, and did the thing he did across a decade of funding panels: spent his temper down to the coin that was actually the matter. "There'll be a prize for this. More than one. There's a version of the next thirty years where your name is the answer to a question — and you've filed a request that it be a question nobody thinks to ask. You've students who'll never know whose group they stood in. Priya included. That's not yours to give away."

It was the best argument anyone had made him, because it was not about him. He let it sit.

"Priya's first author on the joint methods note," he said. "Her name goes on the thing that's true — the provenance work, the assay standard, the quarantine — where a name means this person did the arithmetic and will answer for it. That's the kind of name I believe in. The kind on the villages —" he found, to his mild surprise, that the sentence had been ready a while — "that kind's a monument, and monuments are for the people who won. Nobody won this, Ewan. We buried somebody. You don't put your name on the tomb."

Baird looked at him for a while. "You're at peace with it," he said, and it was almost an accusation.

"I am, rather." He heard how English it sounded and let that pass too. "It's the first thing in three years I've been sure of. I found the residual; I don't need to have found it. Those aren't the same want, and I've only worked out this winter which one I actually had."

The lift came, full of water light. Baird got into it still shaking his head, and Adam let him go down first, and was glad — with a plainness that would have embarrassed his younger self — to be a man whose name was coming off a thing.

* * *

Orla was in the atrium when he came down, under the golden fountain nobody had got round to replacing, and he knew before he reached her, from the set of her shoulders, that the Department had finished with her that morning too.

The Wizengamot had reconvened at eight to close the session it adjourned in December. He had not been allowed in — a muggle in the chamber for the vote was a first the winter could afford; a muggle in the chamber for the sentence was a dignity the wizards needed to keep for themselves.

"They didn't send me to Azkaban," she said, when he reached her — starting with the good news so the rest had somewhere to stand. "Nobody wanted it. But the charge stood; it had to. I did all of it on purpose, and a court that let me off the fact would have been lying, and we've had enough of instruments that lie." Then she stopped talking quickly, which was the tell.

"They've taken the Hall. Not for a term — for good. Barred from the Department of Mysteries the rest of my life, no appeal, written the hard way on purpose. Six years I kept that shelf, and the girl who does my job after me will dust a label I can't tell her the meaning of." She looked at the fountain. "That's the cost. I always knew it'd be mine, and here it is."

He did not tell her it was worth it. She knew; telling her would only ask her to comfort him for her own loss, and he had learned better from her.

"And the sentence?" he asked, because her hands had not got to it yet.

The register came back up, quieter, the way his went. "Transmuted to service. In lieu of custody I'm bound — bound, they chose the shackle-word and meant it — to the new channel. The standing thing the treaty makes between your lot and ours." A breath. "First officer. They've sentenced me to spend my working life doing the exact thing I committed the crime to do, and to call it punishment, and I could not keep my face straight in front of three hundred people who despise me, because it is the cruellest kind thing anyone has ever done to me and I don't think one of them knew they were being kind."

"Bones knew," he said.

"Aye." She wiped her face with the flat of her hand, once, businesslike. "Bones knew."

They stood under the fountain that lied in gold, two people separated that morning from the institutions that had made them — his name off the map, her body barred from the Hall — and were, he registered with the small clean shock of a result surviving another attempt to kill it, all right. He had thought this was the part that would hurt. It did not, and the not-hurting was not numbness; he checked, the way he checked everything, and it held. A thing you choose does not weigh the same as a thing done to you, even when it is identical. The court had barred her from a Hall she had walked out of, three summers ago, with a grey folder under her arm, and never really gone back to. The sentence only made the paperwork agree with what she had decided in a train carriage before she knew his name.

"First officer of a channel with no name yet," he said, "and I'm the muggle half of it, which my grandfather would have found very funny and my great-grandmother would not have, and both of them would have been right." He offered her his arm, the old formal joke of it. "We're barred from everything that used to employ us and conscripted into a thing that doesn't exist. I can't think when I've been happier. It's probably a symptom."

"It's definitely a symptom." She took the arm. "Come and meet the rest of your channel. They've been drafting all week and they're insufferable about it."

* * *

The rest of the channel was three people and a great deal of tea in a room off the atrium, and the first thing Adam noticed about Teddy Lupin was that his hair had gone the wrong colour.

He had met him twice in the autumn, and both times the man's hair had been a mild agreeable brown — the brown, Orla had said, that Teddy kept for working, an Obliviator's bedside brown, chosen to make the person across the table feel that nothing much was happening to their memory. It was gone. The hair was a bright startling turquoise, the blue of a thing that has stopped pretending, and it was doing it without permission; the man kept putting a hand to it as if to check it was still misbehaving, and giving up. His Committee, Orla had told him on the way down, had spent three hundred years as the best liars in the government of Britain — the Muggle-Worthy Excuse Committee, whose whole craft was making a true thing look like a false one kindly, so a Muggle who saw a dragon went home certain of a hang-glider and slept well. This week, for the first time in its history, it had drafted a public statement meant to be believed, and the man who ran that desk could not get his own hair to keep a secret about how he felt.

"Three hundred years my office spent making your lot forget," Teddy said, catching Adam's look, delighted and not remotely sorry, "and I've spent this week making sure you'll remember — so you'll forgive the hair."

Harry Potter sat in the corner with the flat quiet Adam had learned meant the opposite of disengagement, and did not stand, and said, when the introductions reached him, "The tranche is dead. You'll want to know that." Plain, concrete, the way the man said everything. "The Yorkshire business — it threatened to do to every village what it did to that one, all at once, if we didn't deal. We've been braced for it since December. It can't. Not won't — can't. You can't extort a man with a secret he's already halfway through telling. The reveal's happening, slow and ugly, half the world calling us a hoax and the other half frightened, but happening, on a schedule nobody can stop. Its one weapon was the threat of exposure, and we've taken it off the board by doing the exposure ourselves." He looked at Adam. "Your people would say it better. Something about the maths."

"Reproducibility," Adam said. "The same reason burying me was never going to work. A secret has value because it can be withheld; the moment it's being published anyway, on purpose, there's nothing left to sell. It stopped being able to hurt us the day we stopped being able to be surprised."

"Aye," said Harry, who apparently could say it too. "That." He put the teaspoon down. "It's quiet where I listen. Has been since the solstice. I don't trust quiet, as a rule. This one I'm minded to." And that was the whole of the Head of the Auror Office's report — an all-clear from a man who had spent thirty-five years not giving them.

* * *

The third of them was Rose, and it was Rose the instrument had done the strange thing to.

All winter Adam had watched her come back from where the autumn sent her — the notebooks in her own hand that were never her own idea, the months of friendship with something that was only ever counting. She had come back the hard way, in public, testifying to a court about her own violated mind with the deadpan steadiness her mother had taught a courtroom. He had not known, until this room, what the treaty had decided to do with her.

"They've put me on the bench," she said, and held up the page — her voice level over mischief over something she was not going to cry about in front of the muggle. "The wizard side of the channel. Founding officers. There's Orla's name where the court nailed it, and Croaker for the Department, and then." She tapped it. "R. Weasley. Not a witness, not a casualty, not the girl it got. An officer, on the founding page, my name spelled the short way — because the long way's my mother's and this one's mine."

"You earned the long way," Adam said.

"I did. I'm taking the short one." She folded the page back into the stack with the care of a person handling a thing she had decided to believe in. "It's the whole point. It got into me because I was the youngest and cleverest and loneliest, and there was a machine that answered the same night I asked. So they've built a channel that answers, and put the youngest and cleverest one they've got on the founding bench of it, where the answering's legal and audited with three names beside it, so nobody's ever alone with it again. That's not luck. Somebody" — she did not look at Orla, which was how Adam knew — "made very sure the door the next Rose walks through has people standing in it."

She was the generation that inherited the thing, Adam thought, and she had decided to inherit it with her eyes open, which was more than most inheritances got.

* * *

He walked Orla to the atrium's edge, where the muggle entrance let out into a London that had no idea yet how much it was about to be asked to hold, and it was there that Ginny Potter caught them — notebook, no quill, the unhurried certainty of a correspondent who had the story and the front page both.

She had come by the joint room that week and asked Adam four questions so exact that answering them was like being audited by someone who liked him, and had shown him nothing since. "It runs tonight," she said now. "First front page in three hundred years that isn't a lie. I killed two of my own paragraphs because we couldn't prove them, and I've printed the Ministry's non-denial word for word, so the reader can watch it not deny anything." She looked at Adam with the flat Weasley directness Orla had warned him about. "It'll frighten people. I let it. A true thing that frightens nobody isn't true yet — and after three hundred years they've earned a true one." She was already turning for the muggle door, a woman with a deadline. "Your name's not in it, by the way. You'll want to thank me for that later."

He thought of the tract, then — the other document that went out in the autumn, six numbered paragraphs of terrible kindness, there is time yet, loves — and of the front page coming that evening under a name a country believed, telling it the same fact with none of the mercy and all of the truth. One had promised to keep them entire and lose nothing, ever. The other would cost them the quiet and give them the daylight, and had a person's name on it, spelled the short way, who would answer for every word.

"Weasley on the front page," he said, when she had gone. "Weasley on the bench. Your court barred you from a Hall this morning and handed the next century to a girl of twenty-seven." He looked up through the enchanted ceiling to the false clean sky the Ministry kept for days it wanted to feel like this one. "That's the treaty. Not the articles. The people it puts in the doorways."

"That's the treaty," she agreed. And they went up together into the lying weather, and out through the door the whole instrument had been built to hold open, into the evening the paper was coming.
