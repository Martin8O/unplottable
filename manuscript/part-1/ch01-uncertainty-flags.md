By half past nine the Hall had told Orla Quinn one small lie, which for a Tuesday was about the going rate.

The sphere on her trolley said one thing and the registry said another. Its label — yellowed to the colour of weak tea, the ink browned down to almost nothing — gave the teller's initials one way; the ledger, in a clerk's hand a century dead, had them the other way about. Somewhere in 1911, somebody had been tired. That was all it would be, near enough certain: a transposition, the sort of error that bred in copying the way damp bred in stone. She could have mended it in one stroke of the quill. She wanted to, the way you want to pick at a scab.

She dipped her quill and drew, in the query column, a small patient (?) instead.

The Hall did not guess. That was the whole of the doctrine, and she had it now the way she had her own name. A record could be wrong for a hundred years so long as it was honestly wrong; the moment somebody tidied it by feel, it was wrong forever and looked right, which was worse. Flag it, date it, leave the question open until the world got round to answering it. Her da wired houses for a living and kept a version of the same commandment about other men's fuse boards: never trust a repair you can't see the workings of.

She wheeled the trolley on down the aisle, her breath going ahead of her. The Hall of Prophecy was the coldest room she knew and the largest, church-high and church-dark, its shelving running off into a distance the eye gave up on before the shelves did. Candles burned blue at the end of every row — had burned, as far as anyone could tell her, since before the Statute — and gave no heat at all, only a light the colour of deep water, so that the thousands of small glass spheres in their dust sat overhead like something drowned and patient. Some kept a faint light of their own, for reasons no Keeper had ever written down. Parchment, ozone, cold stone: the Hall's smell, which lived in her cloak now, and which she had once — nineteen, home in July, halfway through a burnt sausage in her parents' garden — caught herself missing like a person.

Tuesdays were the nineties through the one-twenties: dust, cross-check against the registry, re-ink where the ink was giving up. Six years of Tuesdays, and she knew the rows the way you knew a street you'd grown up on — which shelves complained underfoot, where the damp got in each February, which labels were going grey and would want rescuing before winter. Row one hundred and twelve, shoulder height, halfway along, ran early 1990: a quiet year, going by the shelf.

She took the feather brush along it, reading as she went, because dusting was reading — nobody wipes a label without the eye taking it in — and after six years the row all but recited itself:

> C.V. to J.O., Jan 1990 — a crown under water and (?)
> P.L. to L.L., Feb 1990 — the maker and (?)
> F.E. to Y.K., Apr 1990 — the widow's clock and the widow

The teller's initials, then the first hearer's; the date; then the subjects, worded as the telling worded them — and where the telling hadn't said enough to be sure, the flag. The third label was flaking. She added it to the re-ink list and went on down the row.

She liked the flags best of anything down here. Whoever had built the registry, centuries back, had been honest enough to record its own ignorance and patient enough to let it stand. Half the Hall was flagged. The Hall was in no hurry.

Two rows over, the shelving ran empty for forty feet, scrubbed and bare, and the registry said why: 311 entries struck through in red, each in the same exhausted hand — "destroyed, June 1996." The Hall knew exactly what it had lost and not one word of what the lost had said. Her first month, she'd thought of the war the way her whole year did — their parents' story, finished before any of them were born — and then she had stood in the forty empty feet and counted red lines until the count stopped being a number. The Department had rebuilt everything the battle broke except the one thing that mattered, because the one thing that mattered could not be said twice. It was the plainest lesson she knew in what an archive is: the shelf remembers what the world forgets, and when the shelf breaks, nothing anywhere remembers for it.

Which was the sort of thing she could never say at home, and could never stop thinking in the grammar of home. Our Ciarán counted things for an insurance firm in Manchester — risk, he liked to say, was just weather you could bill — and he'd have glanced down the registry and called it a dataset, and he'd have been right, and it would have been like calling the sea a water feature. She'd grown up bilingual that way: the phone into the biscuit tin on her mother's dresser every first of September, going obediently dead for the term; her gran's peace counted from 1998, and the Ministry's counted, as it happened, from the very same year — neither of them her doing, both handed down to her like the good china, mind that now. Twenty-seven years old, and the only person on Level Nine who knew what a satellite was for. In six years it had not once mattered to the work. She dusted, and flagged, and took the lift home like anybody.

At ten to eleven the door at the registry end opened, which on a Tuesday it never did, and the duty officer put his head round it wearing the face of a man delivering a parcel he suspects of ticking.

"Quinn," he said. "There's a Muggle thing."

* * *

The corridor outside the Hall ran black and bare towards the entrance chamber, and at the midpoint of it stood more Unspeakables than she had ever seen outside a leaving do. Six of them: the pair from the Time Room, the tall witch from the thought section, two seniors she knew mostly from their commendation portraits, the duty officer making seven — arranged in a loose horseshoe around the open door of the Keeper's office, at a respectful distance, as though the office had done something.

On her desk, dead centre on the blotter, sat a grey folder.

The folder came on Thursdays. That was the first wrong thing. The Department of Mysteries did not read the Muggle press — that was policy, stated where necessary with real conviction — and so there was, officially, no weekly digest of cuttings, no arrangement for compiling one, no grey folder; and the no-folder arrived each Thursday, was initialled unread by three seniors in strict order of their not having seen it, and reached her tray on Mondays smelling of everyone else's offices. It was the one record on Level Nine with no provenance at all, which offended her professionally, and it was the best thing she read all week, which she had never told a soul.

Today was Tuesday. The folder was early, it had been hand-carried, and nobody in the corridor wanted to be standing nearest it.

"It came up an hour ago," the duty officer said, to the wall beside her. "There's been an item. You read that sort of thing." Not a question; not quite an order; the whole Department present in the pause. Nobody said for pleasure. "Tell us whether it's anything."

She went in, sat, and drew the folder towards her, and the horseshoe, with the courtesy of the profoundly relieved, let the door swing to.

One item, alone in the folder. Forty-odd pages of Muggle paper, that bleached bright white no parchment ever manages, still carrying the ghost-warmth of whatever machine had pressed it: printed this morning, then. Somebody's morning had gone wrong early.

The title ran to three lines. Muggle papers led with their modesty the way wizarding ones led with their claims, and she read it twice, once at speed and once properly, feeling the day give its first quiet creak underfoot:

> Structured Excess Residuals in Earth-System World Models: Evidence for Persistent Localised Anomalies in the Terrestrial Sensing Fabric
> A. Kessler, P. Nair — World Model Reliability Group, University of Lothian

Residuals, Ciarán had explained one Boxing Day, pleased as a conjuror, are what's left after you've accounted for everything you know how to account for. In a well-behaved world the leftovers look like static. When they start looking like something, you've found either a mistake or a discovery — and it's a mistake, he'd said, roughly always.

The abstract was nine sentences, and she had read enough Muggle science by now to know the register: the big ones whisper. Somewhere in the world's oceans of measurement, an error that refused to average away — patterned, localised, stable across years of archive; places where the record ran thin and wrong in the same shapes, in every kind of data at once, and nothing in any inventory of the explainable would own them. The authors claimed nothing. They proposed nothing. They had found holes in the world's paperwork, and flagged them, in prose so careful you could feel what it was costing to stay calm. Three words near the end had been underlined, faintly, in pencil — the compiler, God love them, doing their job unto the last:

> persistent, structured, inhabited-looking

Somebody over there had drawn a (?). Somebody had drawn the world's first (?) the size of Britain, and then spent nine careful sentences trying not to say so.

Then she turned the page to Figure 1.

A map of Great Britain, printed in greys. The grey was scoring, the caption said: every square of the country rated for how far the world's behaviour there sat from what their models had learnt to expect of it. Most of the island was smoke. Burning up through the smoke, white as scratches on a photographic plate, were the places where the error would not close.

A hard, compact blot in the western Highlands. A stitch of pinpricks in the middle of London. Village-sized points scattered over the West Country, over Yorkshire, out into the Fens; marks standing off the coast in open water, where there was nothing to mark.

There was the school. She had lived seven years inside the blot in the Highlands. The stitch of pinpricks was over her head. The map had no names on it, and needed none. It was the shape of every place she had ever been safe.

Her breath had gone somewhere. The office was no colder than it always was.

Belfast sat off the map's left edge — Great Britain only, the caption said, coverage pooled 2029 to 2033 — and for half a second, before anything else in her could catch up, she was glad, the way you're glad of a locked door in a bad dream.

She read the rest standing up, without ever deciding to stand.

Three-quarters of an hour, and she understood — flag on her own ledger — maybe one sentence in three. It didn't matter. The grammar mattered, and the grammar was the Hall's: provenance, cross-check, the refusal to guess. They had controlled for everything a Muggle could think of to control for — weather, soldiers, mines, the fenced-off polygons of their own data-merchants — and after every control the white places stayed: sharp at the edges, old as the archive, the same in every independent stream of record; and records were the one thing the Muggle world had never stopped keeping. The authors never wrote hidden. They never wrote warded, nor Unplottable, because those were not words they had; the entire paper was the sound of careful people declining, at length, to make the one claim their own figure was screaming.

It was the finest piece of registry work she had ever read, and there was nobody on Level Nine she could say so to.

When she opened the door the horseshoe had not moved, which meant it had re-formed at the sound of the handle.

"Well," the older of the two seniors said. "Is it anything?"

There were kind answers and there were true ones, and she stood a beat too long looking for the overlap. "It's us," she said. "It's a map of us. The school. The — " her hand did a small helpless circle that took in the corridor, the black door, the whole warm secret mile of it " — this. All of it. They don't know what they've found. But it's us they've found."

And the corridor did the thing she would remember longest afterwards: it stood a little straighter. The tall witch from the thought section laughed — one pleased note, the laugh of a woman whose life's conviction has been confirmed by the worst news available. "Three centuries," she said, to nobody in particular, satisfied. "Three centuries, and they got there by sums."

"Is it divination, then?" one of the Time Room pair asked. "Their kind of it?"

"No." And then, because the Hall trains you not to guess even in your own head: "Not — quite. They've built themselves an expectation of the world. This is a ledger of everywhere the world lets the expectation down." The pair exchanged the look of men revising their retirement dates.

"Who else has seen it?" the older senior asked, and meant which Muggles, the way you'd ask which houses the fire had reached.

"Everyone who wants to." She heard her own voice go flat, the translator's flat, the fact coming through with no duty paid on it. "It went up yesterday on a public archive. It will have been copied by — " mirrored, scraped, forked; she folded the useless words away " — by more hands than we have Obliviators. There is no original for anyone to go and fetch. It isn't a document any more. It's a thing everyone knows."

Nobody looked satisfied now.

"Then one corrects the author," the tall witch said, not unkindly, in the tone of a woman naming the obvious. "One always corrects the author."

"The author worked from everyone's records. Any Muggle with the patience can run the same sums on the same archives and draw the same map — that's the point of the paper. It's most of why it's good." Wrong word. She watched wrong word land around the horseshoe like a dropped sphere. "Correcting him corrects nothing. You'd be adjusting the memory of a man whose memory isn't the problem."

The tall witch looked at her then — at her, not the folder — the way you'd look at an interpreter you'd just caught being fluent in the wrong direction. It lasted half a breath, and it was entirely fair.

"Then what," the duty officer said, "in your reading, is the problem?"

She thought of the query column. Of flags standing open a hundred years because closing them wrong was the only sin the Hall recognised. "The problem is that they've finally asked the right question," she said, "and we've had three hundred years of nobody to practise answering it on."

Down the corridor, soundless over their heads, came a memo: Ministry violet, folded sharp, flying with the joyless competence the paper aeroplanes only ever had down here. It braked at her shoulder and hung there. Five words, in the countersigning hand she had known for six years:

> My office. Bring the item. — S.C.

"There," the duty officer said, with rich relief, to the folder rather than to her. "That's you seen to."

* * *

The Head of Department kept his office off the entrance chamber, behind a door identical to all the others, which was either security or a joke that had outlived everyone who could confirm it. She went up the corridor with the folder held in both arms — hugged to her, she noticed at some point, like a schoolbag — and met Rose Weasley coming the other way behind a trolley of green solution, wearing the expression of a woman who has heard everything worth hearing and is prepared to auction it.

Of course she had. The Brain Room got gossip the way other rooms got draughts.

"Sickle says Croaker sells you to Level Three by lunchtime," Rose said, not slowing, in the tone other people used for lovely morning. "Two if you cry at the leaving do."

"There's no leaving do. Nobody's sold me anywhere—"

But Rose was past her, trolley and all, one hand raised in benediction or invoice, and gone round the curve of the corridor before Orla could decide which.

Croaker's office smelt of dust and pear drops. On the wall behind the desk hung a clock with no hands, which she had long ago decided was either profound or broken, the two conditions not being exclusive here. The Head of the Department of Mysteries was somewhere past seventy-five and looked, as ever, like a man interrupted in the middle of a more interesting century: white hair at all compass points, robes older than her career, eyes that did the arithmetic on you and carried the one.

"Miss Quinn." He nodded her at the chair. "You've read it."

"Twice, sir."

"Then you are the Department's leading authority." He let the sentence end where it wanted. "On what, we shall discover. Sit down."

She sat, the folder squared on her knees like homework.

"Tell me what it is," Croaker said. "Not what it says. I have had what it says from three people who read the pictures. What it is."

What it is, she had been assembling since the memo, the way she assembled things whenever the floor moved: it's a paper; it's a map; it's a mirror; it's the obituary nobody has dared file yet. What she said was: "It's the world noticing it's been lied to, sir. Not any single Muggle — the world's records, taken all together. We spent three centuries making sure no person ever saw us, and no person ever did. Their paperwork saw us. We never once thought to hide from the paperwork."

Croaker was quiet a moment. The handless clock did whatever it did instead of ticking.

"Three hundred years," he said at last, "of the finest concealment ever worked by wand, undone by double-entry bookkeeping." He sounded neither frightened nor angry. He sounded the way she felt in front of the flagged rows: reverent, roughly. "I have sat in this Department since long before you were born, Miss Quinn, waiting for something down here to do something new. I confess I had my money on the Veil."

"Sir—"

"Level Three is standing up a containment working party. Friday morning, nine o'clock. The request that reached me does not, of course, exist, and it asked — in the way of requests that don't — whether the Department had anyone who reads Muggle." A pause you could have shelved things in. "As though it were a dialect."

"It is a dialect, sir."

"Yes," Croaker said. "You're seconded. Effective now; duration indefinite; the Hall will be looked after."

Looked after. The re-inking. Row two hundred's damp coming with the spring. The '96 pages wanting their red lines checked against inventory, the query ledger, and who would keep the questions open — who would even know that open was the way you kept them—

"The dusting will survive you, Miss Quinn," Croaker said, with something that in a younger face might have been gentleness. "The questions will keep. Keeping is rather the speciality of the place."

"Sir, the Department has cleverer people than me—"

"Cleverer at what?" He tipped his head at the folder. "Every soul on this floor can tell you what that map is not. You are the only one who can read it in the original. Upstairs will spend the week deciding what is to be done about its author, and somebody in that room ought to know who the man is when he's at home." A beat. "One more thing. Upstairs you will be the Department's oddity on loan: the archivist who reads Muggle. You will be asked, sideways and over tea, how you come to know what you know." The old eyes did not blink. "Answer honestly. Nothing you could invent would unsettle them half so much."

"Grand," said Orla, meaning nothing of the sort.

"Take the item with you. Learn it. And if anyone at the lifts should ask — " and here was the first entirely visible smile of the interview, thin as a paper's edge " — it is not a record, it does not exist, and you can therefore hardly be removing it from the premises."

* * *

She came back down at the end of the day to hand over the trolley and sign the ledger out, because there was nobody yet to hand either to, and the forms did not know that.

Cross-checks: nineteen. Re-inks listed: four. Queries raised: one; resolved: none. None today, none most days; the Hall was in no hurry. She signed her name under the day's honest little harvest, and the ledger's countersign charm thought about it for a long moment — buffering, she was too tired not to think — before it took her name with the usual dry flick of gold.

The candles were doing their blue nothing the whole length of the room. Her breath went ahead of her up the aisle one last time.

Six years of Tuesdays. Rows she could have walked blind: one hundred and twelve and everything either side of it, the little lights and the dark ranks of the unlit, teller to hearer, question marks standing patient over the whole slow argument between what was said and what was meant. The shelves she had dusted since she was twenty-one held what they held the way the Hall kept everything — safe, and cold, and unread, waiting with no hurry at all for the one person entitled to come and take it down.

She put out the lamp in the Keeper's office, settled the file under her arm, and went up to learn the other archive.
