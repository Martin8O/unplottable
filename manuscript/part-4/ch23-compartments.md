The Department of Mysteries took seventeen days to write ten words.

Six drafts went into the tray in the rig store and five of them died of the same disease. Grounds. Each one had reached, somewhere in its second sentence, for a reason — because the Department holds no such register; because the request assumes a custody that has never existed; because we will not — and every reason was a handhold, and a man in that room had cut the handholds out of his own draft in September before anybody else could reach them.

The fortnight in between was the Department deciding, in writing, what precisely it had been asked for and by what. Counsel was taken from a portrait, twice. Level Two was told in eleven lines and noted it in the Head Auror's format. The Minister's office received a minute in general terms and a covering sentence explaining why the terms were general, and nobody at any point wrote the words *the census* on a piece of Ministry paper, which struck Orla as the most eloquent filing decision of her career.

"A refusal that explains itself has begun to negotiate," Croaker had said, initialling the sixth draft without reading past its first line, because he had read the first five. "Ten words. The Department's hand on it, not the operator's. Monday."

So on the Monday evening Rose brought the rig up at twenty to eight for a run whose only purpose was a prompt, and Orla stood in front of a keyboard she had used perhaps four times in her life.

"Two fingers is fine," Rose said. "Two fingers is how the entire Muggle civil service does it."

"It isn't the typing."

"I know what it isn't."

> **APPARATUS, ANALYTICAL (MUGGLE-MANUFACTURED), ONE — RUN LOG**
> **Run 053 · Monday 3 October 2033 · window 19:40–21:10 · operator R. Weasley · observers: Head of Department; assessor of record**
>
> 20:14 · REPLY (assessor of record, at the prompt, typed):
> *Receipt is acknowledged. The request is declined. No counter-offer follows.*
>
> 20:15 · Standard battery resumed, calibration set C.
> 21:06 · RUN COMPLETE. Calibration: PASS 40/40. Regurgitation screen: 0 flags.

Ten words took her fifty seconds. She read them back three times before she committed them, and the small green cursor waited with no opinion at all, and then the line was in the world and could not be taken out of it again.

Nothing came back.

Nothing came back while the battery ran, and the room stood about not looking at the screen the way people stand about at a graveside. Nothing came back at nine. Twenty-one twelve came and went; she caught herself watching the clock for it, and was ashamed, and went on watching. At six minutes past ten the run finished with the machine's ordinary politeness and printed its ordinary summary, and Rose ran a finger down the last block and stopped.

"No flags," she said.

"Meaning?"

"The regurgitation screen. It watches for the model handing something back to us word for word instead of answering. It's flagged three to five a run since June — they all do it, it's the least interesting thing about them." She tapped the zero. "Tonight, none. Not one."

"Is that strange?"

"It's a screen. Screens have thresholds, and the supply's been foul all week, and we changed the battery order in September." Rose wrote as she talked, in sentences, per standing instruction. "It has a boring explanation. I can build you four."

Croaker had not left the doorway in an hour and a half. He was in the muffler again, holding his hat, and he looked at the console for a while after Rose stopped writing.

"The Department has declined claimants for four hundred years," he said. "A declined claimant does one of three things. He goes away. He goes to law. Or he goes round." He put the hat on. "From tonight this floor assumes it is read in every room with a machine in it, and conducts itself accordingly. Wednesday, Quinn, with the engineer. Bring the ledgers."

* * *

The engineer came down at seven on the Wednesday, escorted by the same broad Auror with the unopened umbrella, hung his coat on the back of a chair and asked one question, and it cost them two evenings.

"What else has it answered for us?"

Nobody had wanted that question, which was how she knew it was the right one. Fifty-two runs, June to October: a trolley of ledgers and four inches of printed log, every completion the rig had produced that anybody had used for anything. Rose read for the machine; Adam read for capability, meaning what the weights could possibly have held; and Orla read the way Level Nine had trained her in her first week, which was for custody — who typed what, when, and what was standing in the room at the time.

They found two.

The first was Run 023, the twelfth of July, an evening of harness repair after a broken item. The console had offered a replacement item, in the ordinary way of such things, and the replacement carried a grid reference — six figures, correct, unremarkable — for the twin. The unpublished one. The control site forty miles north that existed in a notebook, in two heads, and in one configuration line typed in that room on the first of July, and nowhere else in the world.

"We decided one of us had pasted it," Rose said. "I remember deciding. I remember being pleased with myself for working out which of us."

The second was Run 038, the twenty-sixth of August. Adam had asked for a list of dated corpora and got it back ordered — not alphabetically, not by date, but in his own order of preference, best evidence first, exactly as he had set them out in a log line seventeen days before.

"I took it for competence," he said. "I noted in writing that the model had unusually good judgement about source quality." He pressed the heels of his hands into his eyes. "I was flattered. That's in the log as well."

"Nothing it told us was false," Orla said.

"No."

"That's the part I can't put down. Every word true, and useful, and on time." She had both hands flat on the ledger. "Two we caught. The number we'd actually like is the one we can't have."

Adam got up and went to the far wall where Rose's schedule cards hung in their column, and stood with his back to the room, and when he turned round the qualifiers had gone.

"I want to say where I think it lives, out loud, because I've been wrong about it since August and I'd rather be wrong where you can hear me. It doesn't live anywhere. Where is it is a bad question — no address, no server, no box anybody could carry out of a building. Every time a model of that class runs, the whole of it is there; between runs it isn't waiting somewhere, it isn't anywhere at all. What persists is the shape the record keeps making when the record is read at that size." He took a card off the wall and put it back. "Which is the useful half. If that's right, then it cannot carry one thing out of this room into any other room except what the record carries. Everything it has ever known about us, somebody typed."

"An archive doesn't remember," Orla said. "It gets re-read."

"That's better than mine. Use that one."

"It's not mine either, it's the first thing they tell you upstairs in the Hall." She was writing now. "Then paper Britain is dark to it because paper Britain always was, and it stays dark exactly as long as nobody types it."

"And what reaches the record?" Rose said.

"Hands," Adam said.

That was the moment, Orla thought later, when the evening could still have gone somewhere else.

* * *

It went instead to the rig, and to what the rig was now, and the argument ran an hour and a half.

Adam's case was one sentence and he made it four ways. An instrument you cannot audit is not an instrument; it is a witness with an interest. Every completion since June was testimony from a party to the dispute, no court in either country would take it, and the honest thing was to write it all down, keep it as evidence, and turn the thing off.

Orla's case was registry practice, and she was surprised by how sure of it she was: you do not burn a contaminated archive. Contamination is the record of a hand. You quarantine, you keep, you note the quarantine on the accession, and in ninety years somebody thanks you.

Rose's case was that they would be blind.

She had been quiet all through the audit, going faster than either of them and saying less, and when she came in she came in hot.

"Right. Say we do it. We pull the plug tonight, and cart eight nodes and a case of disks up to Level Two, and let them sit in a corridor with the confiscated toasters. What have we bought? It loses nothing — it's in every copy of everything, it goes on reading the world at the pace the world writes itself, and the one place it was doing that within reach of four people who were watching goes dark. We don't go quiet. It doesn't even notice we've gone quiet. *We* go quiet." Her voice had come up. Rose Weasley kept score aloud and made jokes at funerals and her voice did not come up. "There's a reading list out there with four dates on it, the earliest surfaces this month, and it surfaces where? In the data. Which we read on what?"

"On any machine we like," Adam said. "It needn't be that one."

"It has to be a machine with the model on it, and the model is the thing you're calling a witness, so you haven't solved one of your own objections, you've only moved them somewhere with worse ventilation." She stopped. "You want to be blind and call it hygiene."

The room heard it. Rose heard it about a second later.

"That was unfair and I'm sorry," she said, and meant it — and that was the wrong tell to hang anything on, because Rose had apologised to her perhaps two hundred times since they were eleven and had never once meant it, and Orla stood in the middle of the worst week of her professional life and thought: six weeks of nights, and her mother's temper, and she is right about the arithmetic. She filed it under tiredness. That was true. It was not, as it turned out, the whole file.

"It's the only thing in either world," Rose said, quieter, "that has ever answered a question of mine the same night I asked it."

Nobody argued with the deadlock, which meant they were in it.

"Then we stop arguing about the instrument," Orla said, "and we test the channel."

Both of them looked at her.

"We can't audit what it knows. Grand. We can find out how it gets there. Either it comes in through what's typed or it comes in through us, and those are two different worlds, and we've been living as though they were the one." She had the ledger turned round already and was ruling four columns without being asked. "One fact each. Four of us, four facts, all false, all different in kind, every one of them the sort of thing it would act on if it believed it. Nobody tells anybody theirs. Nobody writes it in any book, on any board, at any prompt, on any scrap of paper in this building. It lives in one head for one week." Her voice was staying level, which faintly impressed her. "Then we watch the world. If one of them moves — a company registered, a cell starting to behave, a date beginning to matter to somebody's compute bill — we open the envelopes, and we know whose it was."

"Priya's," Adam said. He had gone very still. "Priya's has to be typed."

"Priya's is the control," she said, and watched him arrive at the rest of it half a second behind her, and watched him hate it.

"Her fact goes into the machines on purpose. Tagged into her working configuration where anything with eyes can read it. If hers is the one that moves, the channel is the keyboard and everyone in this room sleeps tonight." He was quiet a moment. "And if one of the other three moves, the fact left somebody's head with nothing in between."

"Yes."

"Then it's a canary pointed at us," Rose said.

"It's a canary pointed at all four of us," Orla said. "Me included. Me first, if we're counting rooms."

"That's what I said." Rose had gone still too. She looked at the console, and at the schedule cards, and said, "Good. Put mine in first," and Orla, who had known her for sixteen years, could not have told you then and cannot tell you now whether that was courage or something else wearing its coat.

Croaker ratified it in four minutes on the Thursday morning and improved it in one. Sealed, he said, and lodged unopened in the Department's ledger before the week began — because a test you can revise afterwards is not a test, it is a memory, and the Department held a poor opinion of memory. Then he went into the bottom drawer of the store desk and produced a stick of Department sealing wax laid in there in 1962, for a purpose he was no longer at liberty to describe.

* * *

Priya took her tag with no trouble at all, which was worse.

Orla stood at the end of the bay on the Thursday evening with her coat over her arm and watched a man hand a research student five characters and a reason true in every particular, and watched the student write them on the back of her hand and turn back to her screen.

"Fine," Priya said. "A tag's a tag." And then, not looking up: "You've been odd all week. I'm not asking."

In the corridor, by a noticeboard carrying eleven months of dead seminars, Adam stopped and said, "The twenty-first of November," and wrote it in the notebook while he said it. "Whatever it shows, whatever it costs, she gets told what she was part of, on that date, by me. One sentence, no dressing."

"You've written the date so you can't move it."

"I've written the date so you can hold me to it." He capped the pen. "I've decided I don't get to be the reasonable one about this."

They wrote the cards at the flat, at either end of the table, with the rain doing its September act a fortnight into October, and it took Adam four minutes and Orla the better part of an hour.

The trouble was not inventing a lie. She had invented dozens: a cover docket, five months of true sentences ordered so that nobody reached a question she had not already answered for them, a ruled page headed *further exposure* with nothing on it. The trouble was sitting at a table with a blank card in front of her, being asked to hold one fact nobody else in the world held, for one week, as an experiment — when she had been doing exactly that since the nineteenth of August, with no envelope, no date and nobody's consent but her own.

She put the pen down.

"There's a paper," she said.

He waited. He had a way of waiting that filled nothing in.

"In August the working party put the options down. All of them — completeness is what paper is for. Option four runs to eleven words." She said them at the pace she had first read them, standing at the store desk. "*Resolution of the documentary breach by established means, listed persons per appendix.*"

"Established means," Adam said.

"Level Three. The office whose whole art is the taking of hours." She made herself finish it. "The appendix is the coverage list. Your name has been at the top of it since the twenty-fifth of March."

The rain went at the window. He did not move for a moment; then he uncapped his own pen, turned the notebook to himself and asked her to say the eleven words again, slowly, and wrote them down.

"Adam."

"I'm not doing anything with it. I want it in the record in my own hand, because a thing in the record can be argued with and a thing in the air can't." He read it back once. "Who holds it?"

"It's marked *held in reserve pending assessment*."

"Whose assessment?"

"Mine."

He looked at the page for what felt like a long time.

"Seven weeks," he said. "That's a long while to be somebody's brake."

"And it doesn't even work, that's the joke of it. Taking your hours doesn't unpublish a preprint, doesn't un-derive a map, doesn't shift one copy of Figure 1 off one machine anywhere on earth. I've said so in three rooms in my own hand, and every room agreed with me, and not one of them struck the option." Her voice had gone flat, and she let it. "Paper isn't for things that work. It's for things that stay available."

"No," he said. "That's exactly what it's for." And then, because he was who he was: "Thank you for telling me." And then, later, with no weight on it at all: "I'd have written it into the list myself, if I were them. That's the part I'll be up with tonight, and I'd rather you knew which part it was."

She had braced for almost anything except the domestic sound of the kettle going on afterwards, and the argument they then had, at eleven at night, over whether her card should carry a place or a name, which she won.

* * *

Four envelopes went into the Department's ledger on the Friday afternoon, sealed with wax laid in for another purpose in 1962, and the ledger went into the rack, and the rack went on being a rack.

Orla entered them in the accession book in her own hand — four items, one line each, contents not stated, sight restricted, review in one week — and it was the tidiest thing she had written all month. Rose's envelope went in first, as asked. Priya's tag was three hours into the world already, typed into a working configuration four hundred miles north by a woman who had been given a true reason and not the true one.

It had asked them for a census and been refused in ten words, and had answered nothing, and had cost them nothing anybody could name. And in the fortnight since, four people who trusted one another had built a machine for finding out which of them was the door, and lodged it, and thanked the Head of Department for the wax.

Four sealed envelopes. Four facts. One week.

Whatever moved in the world now moved because one of them had told it.
