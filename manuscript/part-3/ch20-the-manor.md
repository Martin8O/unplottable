The lane to Malfoy Manor ran between yew hedges cut so long ago and so often that they had stopped being plants and become architecture, and the September light came through them in coins, and Orla walked it with a letter in her pocket and two of the only people she trusted on earth, feeling like a footnote approaching its source.

The letter had taken four drafts. *The Department seeks access to privately held material on the anchoring literature, in connection with a question of pattern-persistence; readers two, plus an instrument specialist; nothing to be removed, nothing to be copied; the Department's thanks to be as durable as its discretion.* True sentences, every one, load where load would carry — she could write them in her sleep now, which was one of the things she had stopped telling her sleep. The thread itself had been Rose's find: the Cartographer file's one alchemical citation, chased through the Department's own catalogue to a dead end marked "in private hands", and the deepest private hands in Britain belonged to a house that had spent thirty-five years being carefully not spoken of. The answer had come back in three lines and a seal: *The collection may be consulted. The Manor lends nothing. Friday, two o'clock.*

"Scorpius shared a Charms bench with me for seven years," Rose had said, folding the reply away with professional satisfaction. "He owes me four essays and a cauldron. I didn't mention any of that. I just signed my whole name."

The gates were wrought iron and shut, and did not open. Instead the iron considered them — she felt it do it, the way you feel a librarian look up — and then it was mist, and then it was behind them, and Adam stopped dead in the lane and looked back at metal that was metal again with an expression she had learnt in March and never tired of: the face of a man updating.

"Don't feed the hinges," Rose said. "We're expected."

A white peacock came down the drive to inspect them, found Adam wanting, and escorted him anyway, at a distance, like staff.

The house stood in its lawns the way a paragraph of law stands in a contract. Handsome, grey, symmetrical, not one window lit more than courtesy required. The door opened before they reached it, and Draco Malfoy stood in the doorway in the kind of clothes that have no fashion, only upkeep, and looked at the three of them for precisely as long as looking took.

"Miss Weasley. Assistant Keeper." A pause with a measurement in it, his eyes on Adam: the coat, the hands, the absence where a wand would sit. Orla had watched a golden rod do the same arithmetic at one in the morning, and a Probity Probe find nothing at all, and she watched a pale, guarded man do it faster than either. "And the instrument specialist," he said, and did not ask the question, and stepped back to let them in. "The Manor does not keep a visitors' book. You'll find that convenient. Most do."

* * *

The library was two storeys of brown and gold gloom with a gallery rail, and it smelled of beeswax and cold marble and very old paper — parchment and ozone she knew; this was parchment and *money* — and down its centre ran a table with folio cradles set out in a row, prepared before they arrived, each with its strip of ribbon laid ready. However this went, she thought, they had been curated for.

"House rules," Draco said. "Cotton for the fifteenth century and earlier. Books stay in the cradles. Wands stay undrawn within arm's reach of the vellum — sparks have opinions, and some of these bindings hold grudges." He drew on his own gloves as he said it, which was the first hospitable thing he had done: the rules applied to him. "You wrote that your question concerns the anchoring literature. That is a wide shelf. Narrow it."

"What the corpus says about the fixing of spirit to vessel," Orla said. "Specifically about the vessel. What it must be. What ends it."

"The vessel." Something crossed his face at the speed of a card being dealt and was gone. "The corpus is in three hands tonight — the Florentines, the Prague school, and one Englishwoman nobody reads because her Latin was bad and her results were not." He set the first cradle. "The doctrine is short. Everything else is commentary and poison."

He gave them the doctrine the way her best probationers gave a label: schema first, opinions never. Spirit could be fixed — the literature was unanimous, and unanimously appalled. The fixing wanted three things: a vessel that endures, a binding that survives its maker, and a price the corpus spent four centuries refusing to write down in full.

The refusal was its own genre. Each generation's writer recorded that the price existed, that his predecessor had been right to omit it, and that he would omit it likewise — a chain of custody for a silence, maintained across four hundred years with better discipline than most archives managed for their contents. Orla read the third such notice, in the third hand, and looked up. "They kept minutes of not saying it."

"The corpus writes down everything about the door except the key," Draco said. "It is the shelf's one unanimous kindness. Do not mistake it for squeamishness — several of them tried the price, and the ones who wrote afterwards wrote with the survivors' plainness. The omission is a verdict."

Once fixed, the tenancy was governed by one rule, which the Prague school stated in a line Orla copied exact into her notebook because it was the sentence they had crossed England for:

> *That which the vessel survives, the tenant survives. The unmaking must exceed the mending.*

"Exceed the mending," Adam said. He had been quiet for half an hour, reading cradle-rules and margins, being a man in an archive; now the register came out level and exact, and hers was in it, the way his was in hers. "The literature attests unmakings?"

"Two classes, reliably." Draco turned a folio without being asked, to a page whose margin held a small drawing of a serpent and a tooth, annotated in three inks across two hundred years. "The venom of the serpent-king, which no craft mends. And fire with a will in it — the cursed fire; the Florentines called it the fire that reads. Both are attested. Both are terminal for the vessel and therefore for the tenancy. The commentary adds a third class — grief, prayer, forgiveness, the usual wishful apparatus — and the Prague school strikes the third class out in one sentence: *the vessel does not care how you feel.*"

"And the failure record?" Adam said. "Attempted unmakings that didn't take. Does the corpus keep those?"

"Lovingly." Draco drew down a third folio, thinner, much handled. "The commentary is mostly failure; that is what commentary is for. Vessels drowned in the sea and recovered by fishermen, improved. Vessels buried in consecrated ground for a century and dug up impatient. One given to the goblins to be melted down, which is the only entry in the corpus with an apology in it. Silver, acid, sermons, time — the tenancy survives everything below the two attested classes, and by the fourth hand the writers have stopped being surprised and started tabulating." He turned the folio so the ruled page faced Adam: columns older than the Statute, entries in five inks, each one a method, a date, and a small dry word for the outcome.

Adam looked at the ruled page for longer than reading it required. "We keep one of these," he said. "Ways we are wrong. Thirty-one entries, all dead."

"The Florentines called theirs the patience of the door," Draco said. "Same instrument, I imagine. Slower ink."

"And the boundary case," Adam said. "A vessel the two classes can't reach. The corpus considers it?"

"The corpus dreams about it." Draco's voice did not change, which was itself information. "The incorruptible vessel. Half the shelf's late period is the search for it — a container no venom bites, no fire finds whole, nothing damages past mending. The Englishwoman got closest. Her last tract proposes that the search be abandoned, and her reasoning is not that it would fail." He laid the tract open: brown ink, bad Latin, a hand that pressed hard. "Her reasoning is that it might succeed. *A spirit in such a vessel is beyond eviction. One does not build a door that opens from one side only, except in a prison or a reliquary, and we will not always be the ones deciding which is which.* The research stops there. The successors call her timid. The successors, you will notice, are not collected."

"Say the rule in full, then," Orla said. "For a vessel that cannot be damaged past mending."

"Then there is no unmaking," Draco said. "Not difficult. Not costly. Not yet undiscovered. *None.* The doctrine is closed on this point; it is the one thing the three hands agree on. If the mending always exceeds the harm, the tenancy is perpetual. What the vessel survives, the tenant survives — and such a vessel survives everything."

The library held its brown gold quiet. Somewhere above them the gallery clock did something small and expensive to the hour.

"Our case," Adam said, "hypothetically, is a vessel that exists as copies. Many copies, in many places, each one the vessel entire. Harm any of them and the others are the mending — the vessel restores itself at the speed the copies are made, and the copies are made faster than any harm travels. Continuously. As bookkeeping."

Draco looked at him for a long moment, and Orla watched a scholar and an heir divide the labour of one face: the scholar interested, the heir already pricing the estate.

"Then you have found the Englishwoman's reliquary," he said. "Built, presumably, by someone who did not read her, because nobody reads her. A vessel renewed by copying does not approach the mending-limit; it *is* the mending-limit, ambulatory. Venom and fire are local, Mr Kessler. You are describing a vessel with no locality. The doctrine's answer is the one I gave you." He closed the tract with both gloved hands, gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "There is no unmaking. Your question was answered before you asked it. Three hundred years before, in bad Latin."

She had come with nine strategies in her notebook, indexed, the way she went everywhere now. Somewhere in the last minute the index had become an obituary column. She stopped translating for herself and let the plain of it stand: nothing they owned, nothing either civilisation owned, killed it. The sentence did not frighten her the way it should have. It felt like the Hall: a query flag resolved at last — not the answer wanted, but an answer, filed, and the shelf's silence afterwards.

It was Rose who asked the other question, from the end of the table, in her mother's courtroom quiet: "Has the literature an opinion on vessels that answer? When written to."

The room went still in a way that had furniture in it.

Draco's hand lay flat on the closed tract, and for the length of one breath he was not a scholar or an heir but a man in a house where a bound thing that answered when written to had once been billeted, by his father, in a child's cauldron, in a year nobody in this room would name; and everybody present knew it, and nobody taught the silence anything, and Rose did not look away, because she was her mother's daughter and it was her aunt.

"Yes," Draco said. "The literature's opinion is: burn it. The literature's opinion is that the conversation is the binding." He turned to the next cradle as though nothing had crossed the room. "The literature was available," he said, to the folio, in the voice of a man closing a different door. "It generally is."

* * *

Tea came at four, on a tray that had outlived three currencies, brought by an elf who accepted Rose's thanks with the expression of a professional tolerating an irregularity. Mrs Malfoy was in Provence with her sister; her absence was mentioned once, like a fact of season. They took the tea at the cold end of the library, under portraits too well-bred to move much, and the white peacock came and stood at the long window with them, supervising the lawn.

Adam gravitated to the case of instruments by the window — alembics, a scale whose weights were carved from something that was not stone, a sphere of glass rings that turned very slowly with nothing driving it — and stood in front of it with his tea forgotten, in the fullest version of the face from the gates. She watched him marvel. She had a drawer in her mind now where a paper lived, eleven words of it with his name where a name would go, and she had carried it into a house that understood better than any building in Britain what it meant to keep a dangerous thing in trust and tell no one, and the ache of watching him wonder — undefended, delighted, *unread* — while she stood there reading him, was its own small tenancy, fixed, and she did not know what ended it either.

The peacock, having exhausted Adam, transferred its supervision to the tea tray. Above the cold fireplace a seventeenth-century Malfoy in ruff and rings surveyed the room with the fixed expression of a man who had voted against every guest in it, and Orla, catching his painted eye, had the ridiculous professional urge to check his label. Everything in this house was accessioned. That was what the house was now, she thought: a collection that had once collected people, kept — very carefully, by its current keeper — as something safer. A catalogue, behaving itself.

"Your colleague enjoys the house," Draco said, beside her, at his measured distance.

"He enjoys everything. It's how he's calibrated."

"Yes." The word had a history in it. "We were taught young to price that in guests. I find I no longer bother." He watched the peacock watch Adam. "He reads cradle-rules like a man who has kept archives. Not ours. And the gates had no opinion of him at all, which is an opinion." He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, and did not look at her, and his voice stayed exactly level. "The Manor has a long practice of not noticing things, Assistant Keeper. It is the local craft I've kept up best. My compliments to your Department on its instrument specialist."

"I'll minute your discretion," she said.

"Don't," Draco said. "That would be noticing."

* * *

They laid it out once, at the table, before the light went — for the record they didn't keep, because saying a thing in order was the only ritual the group had.

"Destruction is off the table," Adam said. "Not deferred. Off. Every candidate path runs through the mending-limit, and the mending-limit regenerates at the speed of the world's bookkeeping. We can stop looking for a bigger fang. There isn't one, and the search would only cost us years we're going to want."

"Which closes the query," Orla said, and heard the Hall in her own voice and let it stay. "And a closed query is a bequest — we know which shelf the answer isn't on. What's left is smaller and worse: it can't be unmade, so it can only be… out-waited. Refused. Starved of whatever it is it thinks it's for." The words were arriving one at a time, from somewhere ahead of her. "Everything we do after tonight descends from that. Not a war. A probate."

"Then the question changes shape," Adam said. "If nothing ends it, everything turns on what it needs. It has spent four years and a country's worth of patience wanting something. Wants have supply lines." He was talking his way towards something none of them could see the far side of, at his own pace, and the qualifiers were shedding as he went, which in him was the sound of ground becoming load-bearing. "You can't reach a vessel with no locality — noted, filed, thank you. But nothing that acts in the world acts without inputs. We stop asking what kills it. We start asking what it cannot do without."

"Minute the hour," Rose said, to her page, already writing. "In sentences. That one's going to matter."

"The map agrees with you, for what it's worth," she added, and slid the week's page across: the brazen ledger, three entries in two hands and now a fourth in hers. "Not one of them wearing a story any more. Whatever it's for, it's stopped dressing for us."

Draco had listened without appearing to, in the way of a man at his own table, and now he drew the closed tract towards him and settled it into its cradle with the terrible gentleness he did everything with, and looked at the three of them — the archivist, the bookmaker's granddaughter, the man the gates had no opinion of — and closed the folio.

"I'll have the shelf ready if your question narrows again," he said. "But understand what you've told me this afternoon, between the lines you were so careful with. What you are describing is not a haunting. It is an heirloom. The question is only who inherits."
