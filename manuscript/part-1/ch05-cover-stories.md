Ten days after submission, the map had been rebuilt from scratch by strangers on three continents, which was the entire point of publishing and still felt like watching other people let themselves into his house and put the kettle on.

The Zurich group had done it in four days, from European archives only and the weights as published, and recovered thirty-six of the paper's thirty-eight sites — the two misses were the two lightest, sitting exactly where Zurich's thinner exhaust coverage said the power wasn't. A consortium in Toronto had run a model family Adam had never touched, set the floor lower, and reported forty-one, the extra three arriving inside the paper's own sensitivity note like passengers with valid tickets. Nobody had found the fault. The hunt for the fault had taken on the flavour of a public works project:

> re: Structured Excess Residuals in Earth-System World Models — comment #614
> Independent re-derivation, EU-licensed archives only, weights as published. 36/38 sites above the paper's floor; both misses are the lowest-mass sites, consistent with our exhaust coverage. Method repo linked. Somebody please locate the sensor fault, because if there is no fault I would like a different explanation for why I have stopped sleeping.

The artefact camp had spent the week on its best remaining candidate — a common ingest-vintage fault, some shared rot in the sensing fabric's plumbing — and had walked into the appendix built to be walked into: the argument's spine never touched the shared plumbing at all. Aviation-grade integrity, radar coherence, imagery; no common hardware, no common owner, no common physics. A fault with no home was not a fault; it was a description of the world with a fault's name on the door. By Wednesday the camp had regrouped, with some dignity, around *unknown systematic cause*, which was — several people pointed out within the hour — the paper's own first alternative, cited back at it as dissent.

His inbox had grown a taxonomy. Replicators were answered the same day. Journalists were answered once, in writing, on Fridays, in sentences pre-shrunk for quotation. Cranks went to a folder he had named, after some thought, PARALLEL LITERATURES, and read at lunch — people had found the sites in ley lines, in the Domesday Book, in the flight paths of swans, and one gentleman in Shropshire had found them in himself. Two conference invitations and a December panel sat in a folder of their own, pending a decision about aeroplanes he was not ready to make. And, filed alone, a two-line acknowledgement from the reliability lead at Causeway — *we are looking at this; expect nothing soon* — followed by a week of silence, which from that quarter he had decided not to interpret.

And the funder's integrity strand, in the person of Miss Orla Quinn, had asked for a second session, on the grounds that somebody had to read the appendices.

She had arrived on the Friday with the file grown an inch, and the appendices session had run long. She had found, in Appendix D, the one loose thread the referees had missed and he had not: a vintage split in a footnote that disagreed by one quarter with the table above it — harmless, a labelling slip, the numbers themselves consistent — but she had put her pen on it and looked up and waited while he checked, and he had experienced the sensation, novel in an audit, of hoping an error was real so that her finding it would count.

Her questions, this visit, had stopped pretending to an order. Two of them he wrote down afterwards, verbatim, in the notebook he kept for referee objections, under a heading he left blank. On the suppression appendix: *You got eleven brokers' scrub-lists by asking. Who else do you suppose has asked?* And on Table 1, her pen resting on the evidence matrix like a surveyor's stake: *If a stranger wanted to confirm just one site — the cheapest one — what would it cost them, and how would anyone know it had been done?*

Priya had been in the doorway at that moment, a monitor cable in both hands, on her way from one side of the lab to the other, and had stopped. "Is that an integrity question?" she said, to the cable.

"It's the misuse register," Adam said, before he had decided to say anything. "Foreseeable harms are a renewal criterion. It's the question I'd want asked." Which was true, and had been supplied to the room the way the pipeline supplied confidence intervals: instantly, and with no record anywhere of a person deciding. He went looking, later, for the moment he had chosen to answer for her, and found the log empty. The sentence had simply been there — warm, pre-drafted, waiting on its cue.

Then he answered the question too, because it was the question he'd want asked: a few thousand pounds and a commercial tasking account for the imagery-rich sites; for the Highlands site, more patience than money; and nobody would know it had been done, which was the part worth minuting. She wrote it down in the small neat hand that never crossed anything out, thanked him, and moved on to scheduling as if the temperature of the question had been ordinary.

She left at twenty to five for the London train. Priya watched her the whole way down the stair — which was becoming the lab's own minor tradition — and then came and stood in the office, cable still in hand.

"Nobody reads the appendices," she said. "Your words. Week one, when I wanted appendix C in the main text."

"She does."

Priya looked at him the way she looked at a residual, and went back to the lab.

* * *

Monday began with Baird.

The call came at ten past nine, from a corridor by the sound of it, Baird's voice carrying the specific weariness of a man who had spent one career explaining orbits to accountants and was now spending a second explaining accountants to researchers. The board had met. The board had read things. "The board's discovered you're interesting," he said, "and interesting is what money calls a risk it hasn't costed yet — so be boring till autumn, aye, and publish nothing louder than a correction."

Adam said true things back: no follow-ups planned, the replications going well, the group dull by every metric that mattered. He did not mention the review. The question stood in the queue the whole call, costless, one clause long — *your integrity strand, Quinn, one of yours?* — and he did not ask it. You did not volunteer to a nervous funder that his own strand was turning you over ahead of renewal; nothing frightened money like the word *integrity* arriving unannounced. All true. He hung up and sat for a moment with the discovery that the truth had been load-bearing in both directions, and that he had built something out of it during the call, and that the something had a designed look.

Priya arrived at noon and did not sit down, which was the lab's convention for an agenda.

"Objection thirty-two," she said. "Live. I'd like it minuted."

"Minuted where?"

"Don't." She put her laptop on the desk, closed, which was somehow worse than open. "Funders send forms, Adam. When they send a person, the person costs grant money and behaves like it — half a day, a slide deck, lunch if you're lucky, a report you never see. She's been up twice in six days and she's booked in again this afternoon. She takes the five o'clock south like a shift worker. And she reads the appendices. We wrote them, and we barely read them."

"Her questions are good."

"Her questions are excellent. That's the objection. An integrity review checks whether you did what you said. She's checking things we never said anywhere — propagation, who else holds the scrub-lists, cost-to-confirm. On Friday she asked what it would cost a stranger to verify one site, and you answered her, and I stood in your doorway holding a cable while our funder's auditor priced our map like a catalogue."

"Say it's real," Adam said. "Say the strand's turned thorough because the paper's loud."

"Then it's a review with our renewal behind it and my fourth year inside the renewal, and I'd like to know, so I can spend the summer being immaculate." She had not raised her voice; this was the pitch she kept for seminar questions that were verdicts. "And if it isn't real, somebody is being funded to sit in your office learning where our map is weakest, and I'd like to know that more. My actual bet? She's not integrity at all — she's diligence. Some lab is shopping for a reliability team, Meridian's brokering the introduction, and she's pricing us for the buyer. It would at least explain the appendices. Either way it's one email. Baird owes you thirty seconds. Or the strand's page — it's public, twelve faces and a phone number. Either she's the thirteenth or we're having a different conversation."

The people page was one click deep, and he knew it. He put two fingers on the trackpad.

What he thought about, cursor resting, was a doorplate in Leipzig, and the family habit he had carried four generations downstream like an appendix organ: the passports current, the lists mistrusted. He had only ever read the habit one way — never be on the list. It had a second face, it turned out, and the second face was the operative one on a wet Monday in Edinburgh: never be the man who runs the check. That was the state's whole gesture, the interior of it — a person held against a register, and the difference acted on. He had inherited the negative of that gesture the way the sites were the negative of footfall, and he could no more send the email than he could have filed the residual under sensor vintage and gone home to sleep.

There was also — he filed it with the other confounds he had declined to model — the plain fact that he did not want the answer. He marked the bias as a bias. It changed nothing, which was the property of biases his field had never found a fix for.

He took his fingers off the trackpad and closed the lid.

Not the page. Not Baird. The question stayed open. His to hold.

He knew the shape of what he was doing. He had done it exactly once before — one line, no cause he could file, on his own recognisance, against every green dashboard in the building — and that hold stood at five hundred and thirteen days this morning, and it had been right. HOLD, said the queue upstairs, of forty-one clusters of structured absence. As of this morning there was a second hold in the building. It ran in no pipeline, appeared on no dashboard, and it was his.

Priya watched him do it. Whatever she saw, she filed.

"Minute it, then," she said, taking the laptop back under her arm. "Whatever you've just decided. WAYS WE ARE WRONG has a page two after all."

"It isn't wrong yet," he said. "It's unresolved."

"That's what you said about the map," Priya said, and went back to the lab, and left the sentence running.

* * *

The café by the station made tea in a pot, unasked, which in Adam's private ranking of Edinburgh institutions placed it above two colleges and most of the weather. He had proposed it for the afternoon on logistical grounds — the lab was hosting the quarterly seminar, folding chairs everywhere — and on a ground he did not propose aloud, which was that Priya now marked every visitor down the stair, and her doorway had acquired a customs post.

He brought the file, and also, in the file's back pocket, items of no audit value whatsoever: the Zurich repo's one grudging compliment, printed; the Shropshire gentleman's map of himself; her footnote correction, now typeset. He logged the behaviour without flinching from the log: he was collecting things to show her. In a pipeline this was called cherry-picking. Elsewhere, he suspected, it had an older name.

The renewal-year plan took forty minutes and survived. Somewhere in its dullest quarter he asked his own off-file question — where does somebody train, to read the way she read — and got back what he had come to think of as her weather: warm, quick, and precisely bounded. "Records," she said. "Old ones. Six years in an archive where the rule was: nobody guesses. You write what you can prove, you flag what you can't, and God help you if the flag comes off before the answer's in. Integrity work's the same trade with worse tea." He believed every sentence of it. He noticed — the noticing had a column of its own now — that believing her sentences and believing her file were two different operations, and that only one of them was converging.

She squared the file at last, and said the review had a bottom, and that he had been more cooperative than the strand was used to. "Most people guard the cupboard under the stairs. You kept opening cupboards I hadn't asked about."

"That's the file, so," she said then, and closed the notebook and set the pen on it, which he had learnt was her punctuation for a change of register. "Off the file. You'll allow me one question that serves no criterion at all."

"Those have been the good ones."

"The first day, you said—" and she quoted him to himself, exactly, no notes: "*I don't love lists of hidden places. Family habit.* Say the artefact camp's wrong, then. Say the holes are somebody's. Hiding — for any reason, like you said. What do you owe them?"

He took his glasses off, which she seemed to have been waiting for, the way you wait for an instrument to finish its warm-up.

"Accuracy," he said. "First and mostly. The map was arriving with or without me — it's latent in everyone's archives; my whole result is that it can no longer not be found. What I could choose was its manners: error bars, a named author who answers questions, no guessing about occupants — you'll have noticed the paper never once guesses who. And past accuracy—" He stopped, and went on anyway, because the question deserved the whole of the answer. "If they're real, they had reasons. People who hide have reasons, and the reasons are usually other people. So I'd owe them quiet. A finder who sits very still, publishes nothing further about their doorsteps, and gives them the chance to knock first."

"And if nobody ever knocked?"

"Then I'd have a map I never used and a clear conscience. There are duller careers." He put the glasses back on. "That isn't the answer a strategy panel would want. I'm aware."

"It's the answer you'd give," she said, "which was the question."

She opened the notebook again — *off the file* had apparently ended — and wrote one line in the hand that never crossed out. He did not read it upside down, and wanted to, and the wanting went in the column with the rest.

The column had entries enough by now to constitute an inventory of things he was not saying. That in six days he had never once seen her phone — not on the table, not face-down, not at all. That the pen was a fountain pen, and the hand it wrote seemed trained somewhere older than she was. That the strand's page held twelve faces he had specifically not consulted. That he had answered for her in his own doorway before deciding to. That her questions and the file's questions had different centres of gravity, and hers sat somewhere off every map he owned. And that he had started, in the privacy of his own head, calling her Orla, and had caught himself backdating the change, which was against his own data policy.

And the talk itself had a shape — he would think this on the walk home, in the exact register he had spent two years keeping out of the paper. There was a subject it did not touch, and did not touch smoothly, the way the delivery tracks did not touch Site 01: no swerve you could point to, no signpost, only the conversation resuming on the far side in perfect agreement with the conversation that had entered. Absence, behaving impossibly. He was fairly sure — confidence wide, mechanism unknown — that the subject had a name; that she knew it; and that it sat at the table with them at every session, drinking its tea.

At ten to five she stood, on the clock in her body that never needed checking, and did the coat and the bag and the goodbye with the economy of a woman who had never once missed a train. At the door she turned. "Thursday's still bad?"

"Thursdays are non-negotiable."

"Tuesday, then. I've the appendix reading list still to finish, so." And she was gone, into the station's five o'clock weather.

He stacked the two cups and the pot, and audited the afternoon, because the audit was the only instrument he owned and it ran whether he asked it to or not. Findings, held at high confidence and no mechanism: two professionals, both on the record. Two cover stories — hers unverified on a standing hold of his own; his assembled in doorways and phone calls out of true sentences, with no recorded decision anywhere in the build. One table, now cleared. And under the table, all afternoon — under every table since the first pot of tea, running like mains hum under a quiet street — the conversation that was actually happening. It kept no minutes and served no criterion. It was also, he would have sworn, the only exchange of the day in which neither party had said one word that was not true.
