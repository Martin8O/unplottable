The fifth event came in on the Saturday night, as advertised, and being expected turned out to be worse than being surprised. Three shards re-verified between one and three in the morning, stamps unchanged, manifests in agreement, lineage signed all the way down; the Monday desk run drew the same map a shade cleaner, nine sites tightened, nothing added, nothing moved. PIPE-455 sat in the RESOLVED column and did not stir, a resolved ticket having no opinions. The only record in Britain that the night had happened at all was a line of pencil in a notebook that had never met a network, ruled in a registry hand, five rows deep.

Orla wrote the date in herself, in his office, before her coat was off, and then sat looking at what she had written with the quill capped.

"I'd like it minuted that I was right," she said, "and that I'd rather have been wrong, and that those two facts are the whole of my emotional life at present."

"Noted. Fourteen days and eleven hours from the fourth." He had the interval on the board behind him and had not rubbed it out, because it was the most useful number they owned. "Which means the next window is the weekend after this one. And we can't watch it any harder than we've watched the last five."

"So we stop looking," Orla said, "and ask it a question. Go on, then. You've the face on you."

He had rehearsed the opening on the walk in and abandoned it, as usual, and went to the board instead. "In my other trade there's a thing called a canary. You plant a value that has no business existing — a fake credential, a record for a customer who was never born — and then you watch to see who turns up holding it. Coincidence can't reach it. You made it; you're the only source there is."

"A trap street," said Orla.

He stopped. "You know it."

"Every ruled book in the Hall has one. My third week the Keeper walked me down a shelf and showed me a marriage in Argyll in 1806 between two people who never drew breath, and said that if I ever found that marriage outside this building I was to come to her at a run, whatever the hour." She uncapped the quill at last. "Your trade is four hundred years behind mine and terribly pleased with itself. Right. What do we plant?"

"A site," Adam said. "One that isn't there."

He laid out what he had built over three evenings, in order, because the order was the argument. First: the fabrication would not touch the world's data. He was not going to write into a regulated archive, both because it was a felony with a facilities budget and because it was the exact thing they were hunting; the lie would live in two places only, their own local extract and his own prose. Second: the site had to be checkable in both directions — a cell where he could show from the archive that there was nothing, and she could say from hers that there was nothing either, so that anything disagreeing with them about it would be the whole of the finding.

Third, he wanted somewhere with nobody in it to hurt. He put the sheet on the desk: a stretch of moor above the Wye, between Painscastle and Glascwm, four cells of rough grazing, a stone wall going nowhere, sheep, rain, a track. Nine hundred metres of nothing, imaged four times a week since before he was born, and boring in every one of them.

Orla read the grid reference twice and went quiet in the way that meant she was reading a room she was not in.

"Nothing," she said at last. "Nothing registered, nothing rumoured, nothing in the coverage list, and I'd know, because Wales is the section I did my probation in and it's mostly a list of places where nothing is." She set her finger on the sheet and did not lift it. "And the other half, since we're being honest for a living now: my registry holds what got recorded. A thing never entered isn't absent from it, it's invisible in it, and I'll not have you leaning on me as though those were the same."

"Understood. It goes in as low confidence for that reason, among others." He turned the second sheet round. "And it doesn't go in alone."

Four cells, in a table: three of them honest. They had been sitting in the noise floor for two years, unglamorous, explicable one at a time — a Fenland cell with no baseline traffic to speak of, a decommissioned range in Northumberland, a village in the West Country with a speckle to it that had never quite resolved. Every atlas had a drawer of these. Publishing a short list of marginal candidates at reduced confidence, not claimed as sites, was the dullest thing a careful group could do, which was the point: the lie would be one row in a table of true ones.

Orla put her fingernail through the third row.

"Not that one."

"That one's the most honest of the four. It's been in the noise floor since—"

"Strike it," she said. "Please."

He looked at the row a moment, at the coordinates, at the small speckled cell in a county he had driven through, and did not ask. Then he ruled it out and copied a fourth marginal up from the reserve list to keep the table at four, and heard himself say, in the mild voice he used for results he did not like: "We've just weeded the map."

She did not answer that. She did not take it back either, and the sentence stayed on the desk between them for the rest of the morning like a thing neither of them was willing to file.

The mechanics took another hour. The synthetic residual had to be manufactured to a standard nobody would test and he would know: correct in its statistics, plausible in its per-modality split, generated from a seed nobody else on earth was carrying. He used sixty-one. It fell out of the arithmetic into three published figures — an excess of 3.61 sigma, a coherence term of 0.061, an effective sample of 1,461 — and any one of them surfacing anywhere he had not put it would mean the number had travelled, and there was exactly one road it could have travelled by.

Then the last piece, which she liked least and he liked best. A twin: a second fabrication, same method, different moor, forty miles north, built the same afternoon and never published — held in the working extract, on their own machines, and in no sentence anywhere.

"Why?"

"Because a test with one arm isn't a test." He drew the table on the board and filled it as he went, because writing it down was the only way he knew to find out whether he believed it. "Branch one: nothing happens. Then whatever tends the data doesn't read papers, and we've learnt something cheap. Branch two: the world starts to agree with my lie — the pings thin over that moor, the cell hardens, the fake comes true. Then it takes its truth from us, which is a mirror, or a rival, or an artefact, and it is frightening but it is not new physics. Branch three." He stopped hedging, which he noticed himself doing, and did it anyway. "It corrects the lie. Puts something true there that we never fed it. Then it can see the real moor, and it has read my paper, and it cared enough about the map being right to mark my homework."

"And the twin," Orla said.

"Branch four. If the unpublished one moves, it isn't reading my paper at all. It's reading my instrument. It's been in the building the whole time, and everything either of us has done since March has been done in front of it."

Nobody said anything for a while. Down in the quad somebody's fire alarm was being tested, on a schedule, by the book.

"That's a good test," Orla said. "That's the trouble with it. There's no answer it can give that leaves the world the way it was this morning."

"No. And it can't be unasked. Once it's posted it's posted." He put the marker down. "Which brings us to my terms, since we're itemising. This is a follow-up analysis. It's a sharpened map with a new cell in it and my name on the front, and I agreed in March that there wouldn't be one, in a kitchen, to you."

"And I'm the entire archive of that agreement, and so are you, and neither of us keeps a copy. Released." She put the quill down. "Now hear mine, because it's not smaller. That note lands on the Committee's desk inside a week — Muggle science naming a Welsh grid square is the precise thing my department was built to notice — and it will be entered, and assessed, and someone will be sent to walk it. The assessor it goes to is me, because I made sure of that in April for other reasons. So I'll write the survey myself, and it will say there is nothing on that moor, and it will be true, and I'll have used the state's own machinery to launder a lie I helped build."

"And if it doesn't come to you?"

"Then it goes to the working party," Orla said, "and Teddy Lupin drives four hours to stand in the rain on a moor for nothing, and asks me afterwards how my da is."

Adam looked at the four branches on the board and found he wanted, quite badly, for branch one to be the true one, and made a note of the wanting, because a preference that strong was a bias and biases went in the file.

"Last question," he said. "It's the one I'd ask if a student brought me this design. What do we do about the possibility that the subject notices it's being tested?"

Orla capped the quill.

"In my trade," she said, "you never test a witness and expect them not to remember it. You test them anyway. You just stop pretending afterwards that you were only observing."

* * *

The Department of Mysteries acquired its first computer on a Thursday, through the front door, entirely legally, and it was the legality that kept Orla awake.

The paperwork was beautiful. Her contribution to the Wizengamot brief had carried one sentence engineered for exactly this — *the Department cannot advise on an instrument it has never watched work* — and Croaker had read it, and made the small noise he made when handed something that would annoy Level Two, and initialled the corner. After that it was procurement, and procurement was a solved problem: the containment section had been manufacturing plumbers and surveyors since March, and one of its legends, a partnership of land surveyors in Sydenham which had never surveyed anything, turned out to be entirely capable of buying forty-six thousand pounds of computing from a business park in Reading. Gringotts changed the money without a flicker.

It came through the Atrium fireplaces after eleven at night, in eleven trips, in crates with a docket on top, and nobody looked twice, because in six years Orla had never once seen anyone in that building question two people carrying something heavy with a docket on it.

They put it at the far end of the corridor, in a store that had held confiscated Muggle artefacts until somebody's grandfather stopped bothering: eight nodes in one rack, a shelf of disks, and the whole of Britain's expectations of itself sitting on four of them in a locked case. On the paperwork it was Apparatus, Analytical (Muggle-Manufactured), One, which was how the file came to be called *the Apparatus* by everybody who had not seen it and *the rig* by the three people who had.

Power arrived on a trolley, pushed by a man from Magical Maintenance who would not give his name and did give, unasked, his opinion of Level Nine. The thing on the trolley was the size of a tea chest and appeared to be mostly brass, and it hummed, and Rose Weasley — who had been on the floor with a meter for two days and had cabled the whole run herself — asked him what was in it.

"That does the electricity," he said.

"Yes. How?"

"Maintenance," said the man, in the voice of somebody quoting a statute, and wheeled his empty trolley away up the corridor, and that was the end of the only conversation the Department of Mysteries was ever going to have on the subject.

"I have a first-class degree," Rose said, to the ceiling. "I have published on thought-persistence. There is a box in my corridor doing physics at me and the answer is *Maintenance*." She wrote the voltage down anyway, and the way it wandered, and the size of the conditioner she would need between the brass and anything that cost money, and put a small pencilled damn beside it.

The rest of the week she had spent doing something Orla had not asked for and could not stop thinking about. Rose had gone out into Muggle London with a purse of Galleons changed at the bank and come back with a hundred pounds of the cheapest tat in Britain — kitchen timers, torches, digital clocks, four disposable cameras, a radio — and put one of each in every room on the level she was cleared to enter, and left them six days, and written down what died.

The chart was on the store-room wall now, ruled in her mother's fashion and annotated in her father's. Near the deep chambers nothing survived an afternoon; the timers went first, the clocks lost minutes in clumps, the radio came back as weather. Along the outer corridor things lasted days. The gradient was smooth and steep and it had hours in it: worst at eleven in the morning and four in the afternoon, when the department was full of people doing what the department did; best between two and five, when it was not.

"I've made a residual atlas of my own department," Rose said. "It took a week and a hundred quid of rubbish, and it works, and I'd like that noted somewhere I can't be made to explain it."

Orla stood in front of the chart with a cold hand on the back of her neck. Six days, no instruments, no theory, no permission: a map of where machines misbehave, and the shape of the department in the shape of the holes. A man three hundred miles north had needed forty years of archives and a model of the whole country to draw the same class of object, and Britain had lost its mind over it, and a woman of twenty-seven had just done it to the Ministry with a bag of kitchen timers. She thought about saying so. She thought about the seventeen other things it meant, and said none of them, and put the thought in the drawer where the rest of the week lived.

"Which is why the runs go at night," she said instead.

"Which is why the runs go at night." Rose had the whole thing priced already; Orla could hear the ledger closing. "Two till five, clean. Six till nine, usable. Anything in the working day is a lottery and I'll not have our answer come out of a lottery. So: nights. Mine."

"Rose."

"Don't. I've done the sums twice and they come out the same twice, which you know is my only religion." She swung up onto the empty crate, heels not quite reaching the floor. "I've a section that thinks I'm cataloguing thought-residues, which I am, on Tuesdays. I've no children, no husband, and a flat with a leak in it. And what's on offer here is the only genuinely new fact my Department has met since the Veil, at two in the morning, with nobody in the building to tell me I'm holding it wrong." She produced the racing-green book from her robes, and the book, sensing a professional, ruled itself a fresh page. "Four Sickles says I get more out of this rig in a month than my whole section gets out of the tank in a year. I'll take that from either of you. Nobody? Grand. It's written down now, so it happened."

She was going to be there alone, in a stone corridor at the end of Level Nine, from two in the morning, for as long as this lasted. Orla made herself look at that, and then did the arithmetic she would be doing for the rest of her life: the alternative is telling her no, and the alternative to that is doing it myself, and I cannot, because at two in the morning I am the assessor of record and I sleep at an address the Ministry holds.

"You'll log everything," Orla said. "Every run. Times, versions, what you did, what you thought while you did it."

"The rig logs itself. It's very proud of it."

"That's a machine talking to a machine, and it's not a record. A log with no hand in it is a receipt." Six years of the registry came out of her before she could price it, and she heard her own Keeper in it, and was pleased with herself, which she would remember. "Write in it properly. Sentences. Say what you expected and whether you got it, and if you break off at four in the morning say why. Somebody has to be able to read that page in ten years and know what was in the room."

Rose considered this with her head on one side, the way she considered a sum that had come out interesting.

"Nobody's ever asked me to keep a diary before," she said. "All right. Sentences." She was already leaning over the console, one finger finding the comment field like a woman finding a light switch in a house she had decided to live in. "It'll be a dreadful diary. There's nobody in it but me."

* * *

By Friday evening the note was eleven pages long and, up to page nine, entirely true.

*Temporal stability of the anomalies reported in Kessler & Nair (2033): a re-analysis note.* Sole author, for Baird's reasons — a promise of a calibration appendix, Baird said, was a debt you serviced in small dull instalments for as long as you could. Pages one to nine reported that the twenty-three published sites were stable across the whole archive, which they were; that no site had moved, appeared or gone since 2029, which was true of the sites; and that the group had no extension of the atlas planned, in preparation or in contemplation, which was true of the group. It gave the national evaluation picture nothing, at length, with figures.

Priya read it over lunch and pronounced it the most boring document ever produced by a British university, "and I include the ones that are legally required to be boring", and signed off on the analysis, and asked why her name was not on it.

"Because the Institute asked for follow-up work," Adam said, "and I would rather your name were not attached to anything the Institute asked for."

She looked at him for a moment, and he watched her decide it was kindness, which it was, and file it under supervision, which it also was. "That's the second time this month you've been decent on the record," she said. "It's unsettling. Am I dying?"

"You're getting a boring year. I told you it was a commitment."

She went home at six. He added Appendix A at eleven.

> **A2 — Provisional candidate cells.** Listed for completeness. These cells sit at or near the noise floor, are unreplicated, and are **not** claimed as sites; they are recorded here so that the temporal-stability analysis above can be read against everything the re-analysis surfaced, including its marginal output.
>
> A2.1 — NY 74 96 (Northumberland): imagery + SAR coherence; excess 2.8σ; single vintage; consistent with a decommissioned range boundary.
> A2.2 — TL 41 88 (Cambridgeshire): exhaust only; excess 2.4σ; baseline traffic near zero — see §3 on power.
> A2.3 — SO 14 46 (Powys): exhaust + GNSS integrity; excess 3.61σ; coherence 0.061; n = 1,461. Provisional.
> A2.4 — SD 66 51 (Lancashire): imagery only; excess 2.6σ; consistent with land-use change.

It took nine minutes to write and read like every appendix he had ever read, which was the part he would think about later, at intervals, for years: that the thing he had spent twenty-two years being — a man whose numbers could be trusted because he would rather be dull than wrong — could be put down in nine minutes, in a table, in his own house style, and that nobody would ever be able to tell from the prose. He had ended a postdoc's career in 2029 for less. He had been right to.

He checked the twin one last time before he closed the machine: forty miles north, same seed, same craftsmanship, sitting in the working extract where only their own pipeline could see it, and in no sentence anywhere in the world.

The submission form wanted a time. He set it for midnight, which was a needless piece of theatre, and did it anyway, and then stood at the window with the mended mug going cold in his hand while Edinburgh got on with a Friday. The canary would go up at twelve; the crawlers and the mirrors would have it by three; and the summer allocation had moved the group's booked pass to Friday nights, so the next full re-score of Britain — the one that would tell them what, if anything, had answered — was a week away, on Friday.

Somewhere over Wales it was raining on nine hundred metres of nothing.
