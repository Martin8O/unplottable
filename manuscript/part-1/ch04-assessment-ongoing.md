The nine o'clock out of King's Cross knew her name.

It hung over seat forty-one in small amber letters — QUINN — and she stood in the aisle a beat too long looking at it, until a man with a coffee edged past and sat down under his own name without reading it. That was the manner here, then. The world wrote you down, and the courtesy was to pretend it hadn't.

She stowed her coat and kept the bag. In the bag: the paper, annotated now to the thickness of a hymnal; the morning's sheaf from the compiler, because the working party took its cuttings daily now, by the same arrangement that did not exist; a notebook; and the Committee's folder, eleven sheets of legend assembled by somebody who plainly enjoyed the work. Programme review, integrity strand, ahead of renewal. Routine, cooperative. Her own name throughout — the chairwoman had been firm on it, laying the principle down like a rule of hygiene: you build the lie out of truth wherever truth will carry the weight, because truth needs no rehearsing and cannot be contradicted by a photograph. Fewer parts to service. Orla had copied that into her notebook under the heading THE TRADE, and had not yet worked out whether it belonged to spying or to archiving, and was beginning to suspect the two fields had more correspondence than either would enjoy hearing about.

The train drew out on time, and Britain began to file past the window, writing itself down as it went.

She had known this, the way you know a thing you have only ever read. Now it kept happening in front of her. The carriage announced itself at every stop, to the metre and the minute, in a voice as pleased with its lists as the Ministry lifts were with theirs. Phones lay face-up on tables like small patient witnesses. Across the aisle a girl of maybe nine wore the glasses half the carriage wore, and whatever the glasses saw went somewhere, and nobody looked up to wonder where; a wizard could walk this aisle in full robes and be filmed from eye level six times before the buffet car. In the Hall, a record was an event. You fetched the ledger, you dipped the quill, you signed what you had seen. Here the recording had come loose from anybody's intending it. The country kept a diary the way a coastline keeps the weather — without meaning one word of it, and without the least ability to stop.

Six years she had been the only person on Level Nine who knew what a satellite was for, and it had mattered to the work not at all, and now it was the work. She took out the sheaf.

The compiler's hand was all through it, underlinings and small pencilled queries, and she read the chatter the way she read anything: provenance first. Four days since somebody on the archive's public forum had begun taking the paper apart line by line, sums re-run in public, figure by figure — hostile, she had thought at first pass, and then had understood, somewhere past Peterborough, that it was the opposite of hostile. It was countersigning. A stranger, owing the authors nothing, re-deriving the record to see if it held — the Hall's whole doctrine, run at a scale the Hall could not dream, by people who would never once call it doctrine. Two groups had the same map already. A third had the map wrong, and had been corrected within the day, in public, with citations, by the first two. And a camp was forming — she could watch it form, post by post — around the comfortable reading: artefact. Some common fault in the sensing fabric, unknown but surely findable. The Muggles were drafting their own Muggle-Worthy Excuse, unpaid, unprompted, in their thousands.

It would not hold, and the paper itself said why, in the property she had underlined three times on the first reading: the better the models got, the louder the sites would ring. This year's excuse was next year's evidence. An excuse with an expiry printed on it was not an excuse; it was a countdown.

She put the sheaf down. The country went by, all that recorded weather, and she let the thing she had been circling for a week finally sit still long enough to be read.

There was no author to correct any more. There were only readers. The paper had stopped being a thing he had written and become a thing the world knew, held in more hands than the Ministry had ever counted, re-derivable from records no wand could reach — and records, she had told the corridor, were the one thing Muggles had never once agreed to stop keeping. All true. All filed. And upstairs it had changed nothing, because the machine had its procedure, and the procedure said: assess the source. On the blotter in the Keeper's office lay a docket with one blank line, and every wand on Level Three was waiting on it, and the line was hers.

She got out the notebook and made the list, because her hands wanted the list. Questions an auditor would ask: data provenance, methods audit, error handling, the register of foreseeable harms, plans for the renewal year. Questions an assessor would ask, wearing the auditor's clothes: who else, how many, how far, how fast. Where the two lists agreed, which was nearly everywhere — that was the Committee's craft, and she could admire it now from inside, the way she admired the Hall: the door built so exactly out of the truth that walking through it barely felt like lying.

Under both lists, unclothed, unprocedural, the question she was actually carrying north:

What do you do with a man who keeps his flags open?

Past Berwick the sea came up on the right-hand side, grey as the figure, and did not once stop writing.

* * *

The World Model Reliability Group lived on the third floor of a university building that smelled of carpet and cold radiators, up a stairwell postered with colloquia three months dead. Her lanyard said VISITOR and, beneath it, her own name, being true.

The corridor door of the lab stood propped, and a young woman came out of it carrying a laptop like a writ — small, quick, a cardigan the colour of strong coffee — and Orla knew her before the introduction the way you know a label you have re-inked: NAIR, P., twenty-six, second author, assessment pending source. On a list in a locked room, a ruled line. In a corridor in Edinburgh, a person, with a plaster on one thumb and her whole life ahead of her subject to recommendation.

"You're the funder," Priya Nair said, in the tone of a woman confirming bad weather. "Third door. He's made actual tea, so you're either being honoured or managed — with Adam it's genuinely hard to tell." And she was gone down the stairwell, taking the stairs two at a time, before Orla could decide whether she had just been welcomed or warned.

The third door stood open. Kessler, A., author, source, primary, was taller than his photograph and sorrier about it, folded into the doorway with his hand already out; behind him the wall of printouts from the staff picture went on being unapologised for, and a teapot — a real one, warmed, she could smell the leaf from the threshold — sat on the one clear corner of the desk like a small defended position.

"Miss Quinn. Thanks for coming up — I know reviews usually summon us down instead." His handshake was dry and brief and his attention was not; it landed on her whole, the way Croaker's did, minus forty years of practice at making it frightening. "I should say at the start that I've been hoping somebody would come. That's not," a beat, "the standard opening, I'm told."

"It isn't," she said, and sat, and took out the notebook. "Most subjects of an integrity review greet it like an audit of their soul."

"It is an audit of my soul." He said it with no dramatics at all, pouring. "The paper's the most careful thing I've ever signed. If it's wrong anyway, I'd rather be told by somebody whose job is telling me than by a forum at two in the morning. Milk?"

The tea was very good, which she had not planned for, and the first hour ran on rails she had drawn up herself and could feel herself leaving.

He answered everything. That was the thing she had not planned for either — not the answers, the answering. She asked her auditor's questions in their auditor's order, and he took each one the way the Hall took a query: gravely, gladly, as if being asked was the point of having said anything at all. She asked about provenance and got the data's whole genealogy, licences and vintages and the one broker whose paperwork he distrusted on instinct — with the instinct flagged as instinct, unweighted, the way she flagged a teller whose hand she didn't know. She asked the artefact question — what makes this real and not a fault in your own glass — and he gave her, without knowing he was doing it, the assessment's answer, the room-emptying one: "Ask what kind of fault. A fault has a home — one vendor, one pipeline, one physics. This has none. My student's phrase is that confounds have slopes and these have cliffs; I've spent a year trying to improve on that sentence and failed. And the streams that agree about the sites share nothing — not hardware, not owners, not assumptions. When strangers agree, you're allowed to start believing it's the world."

"And if a better explanation turns up tomorrow?"

"Then I'll be relieved," he said. "An instrument you can mend. I did try to arrange the relief myself, for two years, at public expense — it's most of what you funded, if we're honest, a long attempt to be wrong. The attempt failed. That's the paper."

She wrote a long attempt to be wrong in the funder's notebook, in her registry hand, and under it, unprofessionally, true.

He showed her the pipeline because she asked, and because integrity reviews were entitled to workings, and the workings were the point on which every plan she had carried north went quietly over a cliff of its own. The screen gave her the nightly verdict, that morning's copy of a sentence the machine had been writing since before the paper was thought of:

> ANOMALY-TRIAGE · nightly run · 2033-03-29 03:47
> geo-clustered excess residual, GB grid: 41 clusters above floor
> class: DATA-QUALITY (sensor vintage / ingest fault) · confidence 0.94
> recommended action: exclude from aggregates
> status: MANUAL HOLD (A. Kessler, 2031-11-08) · hold active 507 days

"The triage layer files the whole signal as a broken instrument, every night," he said. "Standard tooling; every serious lab runs the same defaults, which is why every serious lab has been throwing this away for years. The hold is the only manual override in the pipeline, and it's mine, so if you're auditing anything, audit that. Everything else in there is checked by machinery. That line is just me, refusing the tidy answer, on my own recognisance, for five hundred and seven days."

"Why not resolve it?" She had left the auditor's order by then and knew it. "Either way. Five hundred days is a long time to be undecided."

"It isn't undecided." He said it mildly, but the qualifiers had gone out of his voice, and she understood that she was hearing the load-bearing wall of the man. "It's open. Undecided is a coin waiting for a toss; open is a question you are refusing to close without cause. My field files both under the same icon, which I'd submit is the actual finding of the audit, if the review wants one." Then, catching himself claiming, with a small parenthetical wave: "That may be grander than the evidence supports. Most things said after four are."

She looked at the line of the hold and had, for a moment, the sensation of standing in row one hundred and twelve — the flag drawn by a stranger's hand, dated, patient, wrong to close, holding a question open against the whole weight of an institution whose dashboards would be greener without it. The Hall did not guess. Neither, it turned out, did this man's one ungoverned line. Two archives, and the same doctrine at the bottom of both, and only one of them knew it was one.

The last question on the auditor's list was harms. She asked it in the folder's words — foreseeable misuse, the register of — and watched something she had not seen yet cross his face; he took his glasses off, which she would learn was what he did when a question deserved worse light and more honesty.

"I sat with one for a while," he said. "A map of holes is a list of places to dig. If the sites belong to anyone — anyone hiding, I mean, for any reason — then I've published a finding aid." The office was quiet; below the window a delivery van reversed, beeping, and neither of them was in the room with it. "What I kept coming back to is that the list already existed. It's latent in everyone's archives — that's the whole result. Publishing didn't make the list; it decided how the list arrives. With error bars and a named author who answers questions, or later, from somebody who states none and answers nothing. I couldn't choose that nobody be first." A pause, and the sentence he plainly had not planned to say went through the door his candour had left open: "I don't love lists of hidden places. Family habit. We keep the passports current."

She held her pen still over the register of foreseeable harms until it had stopped wanting to shake, and wrote, in the legend's bland hand: author has considered misuse; reasoning documented; no further action, and knew, watching the words go down — the assessor watching the auditor write — that of every line on Level Three's list, coverage as currently established, the man least in need of assessment was the source.

At the door, the interview over, both of them still standing in it: "The review will need more than one session," she said, because it was procedure, and because it was procedure she could stand on with both feet. "There's the renewal-year plan, and the appendices—"

"Nobody reads the appendices." He had the look of a man being handed an unexpected second helping. "Come back for the appendices. Thursday's bad — Thursdays the pipeline and I renegotiate our relationship — but any other day the kettle's where it was."

On the stair she passed the propped door of the lab. Priya Nair looked up from three screens of the map, and did not say anything, and was not managing anything, and watched her the whole way down.

* * *

Outside, Edinburgh was doing its weathers in rotation, and a long green crag stood over the town with the light going up it, patient as furniture. She walked to the station the slow way, by the gardens under the castle rock, because the assessor was owed a walk and the auditor had a train at five.

The playbook had died on Tuesday, in the corridor, when she told them the paper was past fetching. She had known it the way you know an arithmetic. Now she knew it the way you know a person. Somewhere behind her, up a stairwell that smelled of carpet, was the one line in the Muggle world's whole machinery that had refused the tidy answer — five hundred and seven days of refusal, signed and dated, the exact shape and grain of a flag in the Hall — and her orders were to judge whether it wanted erasing. Take the memory and the machine's excuse resumes its nightly service; the dashboards go green; the map stays, the readers stay, the countdown stays, and the only thing gone out of the world is the care. You could not erase the paper out of the world. The world was what it was printed on. You could only erase the world out of the one man who had read it first and best, and call the resulting label mended.

Her da wired houses, and had one commandment above all the others, issued over every torch-lit fuse board of her childhood: never trust a repair you can't see the workings of. The Ministry's repair was invisible by design. That was the whole art of it, the pride of the floor: no seam, no scar, the excuse writes itself and the story survives contact.

She stopped at the gardens' black railing.

She was not going to sign it. There was no version of the signature that was true. She had gone up the country an assessor with a blank line, and somewhere on the stair between the third floor and the street the line had filled itself in, and it had not consulted her, and there was nothing in it she could honestly cross out. It was not a decision. She searched herself for the moment of deciding, the way she would search a ledger for a corrected entry, and found no correction anywhere — only the record, in her own hand, already reading: no.

A gull went over the gardens, filing its one complaint. On the platform the five o'clock knew her name, and she sat down under it, and did not read a single page the whole way south, and watched the archive get dark.

* * *

Level Nine kept its winter, and had kept her a share of it: the Keeper's office was exactly as cold as she had left it, and on the blotter, squared, sat the assessment file, and on the file some passing hand had left a violet memo, wings folded, patient as everything else down here.

> Survey crews home dry. Sitrep when convenient; Wednesday will do. — T.L.

Kind, and warm, and a deadline in a good coat. She hung her coat on the hook, lit the lamp, and sat down to build the version of events.

It took four drafts, which for a document of six lines was one more than her worst day in the registry.

The first draft said the breach did not live in the subject, and that the assessment had therefore been aimed at the wrong archive from the hour of its convening, and she read it back twice and crossed it through, because it was true the way the corridor horseshoe's panic had been true — accurate, unusable, an invitation to somebody upstairs to fix the aim.

The second draft said that Obliviation would remove nothing from the world except the one man handling the material with care, and she crossed that through faster, because it had her in it, audible, the way wrong entries always carried the copyist's own hand.

The third draft was nearly right and helped nobody, and the fourth draft was the one the file wanted. She wrote it clean, in the registry hand, in the grammar the machine upstairs was built from — she had been three days on Level Three; she had the grammar the way you get a tune, without once deciding to learn it — every sentence true, every sentence load-bearing, every sentence facing the reader it would have to survive:

> BREACH (DOCUMENTARY) 2033/1 — ASSESSMENT: INTERIM MINUTE
> Assessor: QUINN, O. — Dept. of Mysteries (seconded)
> Contact established 29 March under approved legend. Access secure; subject cooperative in full; legend holding without maintenance.
> Materials obtained: methods, pipeline workings, data register (schedule attached).
> Further exposure: enumeration in progress; the documentary class propagates (reading list attached); appendix to follow.
> Status of assessment: ONGOING. Further sessions warranted and arranged under legend.
> Recommendation: none at this time. No action to be taken pending recommendation of assessor, per procedure.

Every word of it would survive contact. Lupin would read it and see the procedure working; the Committee would read it and see a door holding; Croaker would read it and see whatever Croaker saw, and file that too. It said nothing false. It said nothing. It bought — she totted it up like a column — a week at the least, sessions unnumbered, access indefinite, and not one wand moving anywhere in Britain, all of it purchased with the truth, and she was tired enough that the pleasure of the craftsmanship arrived on its own, unexamined, like change from a note — hers, somehow, this facility, this way the door built itself out of true things — and was pocketed before she could hear what it was change from.

The docket she took out last. ASSESSMENT DOCKET — BREACH (DOCUMENTARY) 2033/1. Subject: KESSLER, ADAM — Muggle, author (source, primary). Assessor: QUINN, ORLA. Recommendation of assessor, and the ruled line after it, the white space that was the point, the pen's length of Level Three's whole machine.

She did not fill it in. She squared it, and she did not fold it for the morning memo round, and she did not walk it up to the warm floor where it belonged. She opened the deep drawer of the Keeper's desk, where the previous Keepers had left what previous Keepers leave — a broken quill, a tin with two peppermints in it, forty years of blotting sand — and she laid the docket in flat, face down, and closed the drawer on it, and heard the little brass catch take.

The minute went in the tray for Level Three. The docket stayed in the dark, in her keeping, in the coldest archive in Britain, unsigned.

There was now a version of events that was hers alone.
