The knock came at eight exactly, which he had already learnt was her: a person who treated other people's time as an archive she was borrowing from.

He had brought it on himself, in the plain sense that he had started it. That afternoon, at the end of the Tuesday session, with the renewal plan closed and the pens down, he had heard himself make a disclosure the review had not asked for: that Meridian's integrity strand kept a public staff page; that he had known this for three weeks; that he had not looked at it and did not intend to; and that the review deserved to know its subject was running an unaudited manual override on his own diligence. He gave her the reason, too — the doorplate in Leipzig, the passports, the family habit read at its second face: never be the man who runs the check. She had gone very still while he said it, the stillness of a reader at a difficult label, and then she had asked, in a voice with no procedure left in it, whether she might call at his flat that evening. Off the file. He had said yes before he had decided anything, which was becoming the signature move of his year.

Now she stood on his landing with rain on her shoulders, holding no notebook, and he understood that whatever the file had been, it was over, and something with worse error bars was about to start.

She asked for tea, and drank half of it, and put the cup down.

"Before I say anything," she said, "I want to do something instead. Sayings can be argued with; I've watched you argue with better sayings than mine. Have you got something broken? Small. Something you know the whole history of."

He had the mug. Everyone has the mug: university crest, handle off since the winter before last, kept on the shelf doing pen duty because throwing away a thing that still mostly worked was against some religion he had never named. He put it on the kitchen table with its handle beside it and, because the request had the shape of a protocol, gave her its provenance unasked — bought 2027, dropped 2031, both witnesses present, no repairs attempted.

"No repairs attempted," she repeated, with a small warmth he did not understand until much later. Then she took from her coat a length of pale wood, worn at the grip like a tool that had done its owner years of service, and touched it to the break, and said a word he did not catch — said it the way you remind rather than command — and the handle went home.

There was no flash and no sound. That was the first thing his training laid hold of, afterwards: the event had no edges. The handle was off the mug, and then the mug was whole, and the two states did not seem to have a border between them, and nothing else in the kitchen noticed. The lights held steady. His screen, asleep on the counter, stayed asleep. The kettle ticked as it cooled, on schedule. The universe declined to mark the occasion in any channel he owned.

He picked the mug up. The glaze ran unbroken where the break had been — not patched, *continuous*, the crazing pattern crossing the old line like a road that had never heard of the border — and the chip on the rim, the one it had carried since 2028, was still there. She had mended the break and left the wear. Somewhere under his sternum a technical judgement formed unbidden: a forger would have made it perfect. Only an archivist would have restored the record without falsifying it.

"Again," he said. Which was, he would admit later, the whole of him in one word.

She looked at the mug, and it lifted from his hand — at no great speed, at the pace of a decision — crossed the kitchen, and settled on its shelf, the right way round, handle outward, the way shelves are kept by people who keep shelves.

Adam sat down. It was that or stand there while every sentence he owned reported, one by one, that it could not take the weight. He had spent twenty years learning to say "consistent with" and "within tolerance" and "insufficient evidence", a whole grammar built for a world that wobbled honestly around its means, and the mug sat on its shelf outside the entire language, whole, chipped, smug.

He ran the list, because the list was what he had instead of prayer. Staged: her hands had gone nowhere near it, the kitchen was his, and the mug had been in his own grip when it left. Projected: he raised a hand to his face to check for glasses he already knew he wasn't wearing — she watched him check, and let him, with the patience of a professional watching another professional work — and the mug, retrieved from its shelf, had mass and warmth and its chip, and took two of his fingers to hold. Chemical: his tea, his kettle, his water, poured by him. Perceptual: then it was a shared hallucination with object permanence, load-bearing behaviour and a preferred shelf orientation. He went down the ways of being wrong in order, the way Priya went down her rope, and the evening survived every one of them, and at the bottom of the list he found what he had found once before at the bottom of a different list: the world, containing a structure nobody had told him about.

"All right," he said at last. "I have questions."

"I know," she said. "It's why I came. You're owed the answers in order, and the order matters." And she sat down opposite him at his own kitchen table, folded her hands, and gave him the answers the way — he thought of it exactly this way, and knew the thought was borrowed from her — the way you read a ledger aloud to the auditor.

Her name was her name; he had never been lied to about a single fact, which she asked him to hold on to, because it was the only comfort on offer. She was not a funder's auditor. She was an archivist — six years the keeper of a records room he would never be shown, in a Ministry that did not appear in any directory of ministries, of a country that had been inside his country the whole of his life. The white sites were not installations. They were addresses. A school. A hospital. Villages. A street of shops. Every point on his atlas was a place where people lived — people who had opted out of being written down, three and a half centuries ago, and had built, instead of privacy policy, a civilisation.

"The paper's word was right," she said. "Inhabited-looking. You were more careful than you knew, and the care is why I'm in your kitchen."

He asked his questions, and they came out in his own dialect, because it was the only one he had. How many — and she declined the number, gently, as not hers to give away: "Fewer than you fear. More than you'd credit." Since when — and she gave him a date, 1689, a signature, an actual document, and his sense of the last month rearranged itself around the fact that the oldest data point in the study predated the telescope. And the question he asked with a gesture at the kitchen, at the steady lights and the sleeping screen: why does nothing ever register?

"You've already published the answer," she said. "Your instruments see what our concealments do. Never what they are. And here's the part your side will never guess on its own: so do we. We don't know what it is either. We work it the way you work your models — craft, apprenticeship, knowing what happens without once knowing why. Your people mapped our entire way of knowing from orbit, Dr Kessler, and called it Figure 1."

He asked, finally, what she called it — the it of all of it — and she told him, and the word arrived wearing three centuries of pantomime and had to sit at his kitchen table while it took off the costume, item by item, and became a technical term. It took less time than it should have. The kitchen smelled of rain off her coat, and of tea, and there was a witch at his table drinking it, and somewhere in the last quarter-hour the word had stopped being embarrassing the way a foreign currency stops being toy money the first time you buy bread with it.

And then, because the order mattered, she told him what she had been sent to do.

She told him about the office that manufactured forgetting — a whole floor of it, staffed by kind professionals, running for three hundred years on the belief, correct until March, that any witness could be unwritten and any evidence out-waited. She told him about the list: his name at the top of a ruled page, "subject, primary"; Priya's beneath it, "second author"; Baird's; an appendix for everyone else, ruled, dated, empty. She told him what the blank line was — the assessor's recommendation, the one signature between the list and the wands — and whose hand held the pen, and what she had written instead, four drafts, and where the docket was now: in the dark, in a drawer, in the coldest archive in Britain, unsigned, held open now for the fourth week by six true sentences renewed like a spell that had to be kept fed.

"Your machines," she said, "file what they can't explain as broken glass, and my office files it as *handled*. Same tidy answer, same icon. You kept your line open on your own recognisance. So did I. That's the whole of what I am, Dr Kessler, under the lanyard: the other archive's manual hold."

The kitchen was quiet. Below the window the street did its small hours of business; a bus ran its interval; somewhere in the building a door closed on someone's ordinary evening — the world going on writing itself down, every second, in channels she had just told him could be made to forget.

"Priya," he said. Quietly, and with no qualifier anywhere on it. "She checked the sums. That's the entirety of what she did. She's twenty-six."

"I know her age," Orla said. "It's a column on a page I hate. Nothing touches that list while my line is blank, and my line stays blank. That's not a kindness I'm offering; it's the one power I hold, and you should know I am spending it illegally. My interim minutes are true the way your funding reports are true. Assembled. My state believes it is waiting for my recommendation. It is not waiting. There will never be one."

"You're describing treason."

"I'm describing my terms," she said. "You were always going to be told; you were owed it from the day I drank your tea under a legend. But I came tonight because the politics has a clock on it now, and I'll not do the next part without you knowing every fact I know." She turned her cup a quarter-turn, her one tell, he thought; filed it. "A man stood up in a high room on Friday and proposed, decently, that my country finish the work of 1689 — seal itself under its concealments entirely and for ever. He argued it well. If his motion carries, there is no version of me still sitting at this table. And a woman at the head of that table asked the one question nobody could answer: when the finders find a hidden thing that hid on purpose — what do they do next?"

"That depends on what they find," Adam said. The answer arrived assembled, no drafting stage he could point to. "A wall, or a door."

Orla looked at him for a long moment, and something in her settled, the way an instrument settles on its reading.

"There," she said. "That's the paper she asked for. Nobody in my world can write it, and nobody in yours knows it's wanted. It needs both archives and four hands, and every institution that could commission it would first have to arrest the authors." She put her cup down. "So. Terms."

* * *

The terms were drafted between eleven and one, at his kitchen table, in nobody's hand.

That was itself the first term, and she stated it like a rubric: nothing written. He had laughed — one dry note, before he could stop it — at the two of them, a keeper of records and a publisher of records, sixteen professional years of "write it down or it didn't happen" between them, founding their entire enterprise on the one storage medium both their worlds had given up on: memory, in two heads, checked against itself weekly. ("First rule of my floor," she said. "Two readers or it isn't a record. You're my second reader now; I'm yours.") "Every archive can be seized," she said. "Every archive can be read. I've spent six years learning what a record is, and here's the sum of it: a record is a hostage. We keep none."

His term came first, because it cost him longest. No follow-ups. The atlas stopped at version one: no sharpened maps, no second paper, no method improvements quietly gifted to the replication crowd — the residual atlas would stand in the literature exactly as careful and exactly as blunt as it was on the twenty-first of March, and grow slowly out of date, and he would let it. He heard what it meant as he said it, and said it anyway, and itemised the cost aloud because unspoken costs compound: the renewal case gutted; the group steered to reliability work of reputable dullness; Priya's doctorate re-aimed at the calibration chapters, which were, he would tell her truthfully, the rigorous heart of the thing. His name would stay on one famous paper and never on its successor, and the field would call it caution, or nerves, or a lab bought quiet by a defence contract, and he would answer none of the letters.

"You're sure," Orla said. "It's your life's work I'm pricing."

"It was my life's question," he said. "The work was only ever the asking. As of tonight I know the answer's postcode; publishing the street number arms every wall-builder on both sides. That isn't science stopping. It's science declining to be a targeting product." A breath. "Also — and I want this minuted in the archive we're not keeping — I appear to have priced my life's work against a mended mug and found the mug persuasive. Peer review would have notes."

Her term he already knew, and she said it anyway, aloud, formally, the way oaths want saying: she would never sign, and never let another hand sign — no erasure, of him, of Priya, of anyone on any list she could reach, whatever it cost her to prevent, and it would eventually cost her the Hall, and she named the Hall aloud so that the price would be on record between them. Then the joint clause, the working group of two: his instruments, her archive; his forty pages, her three centuries; one question between them — "what do the finders do next" — and one paper to draft in two heads for the day one of two governments grew desperate enough to ask for it. Meeting schedule: Tuesdays, under the review's dying legend while it lasted. Communications: nothing digital, ever, which from her was doctrine and from him, now, was a defensible reading of the threat model.

"Say the rest of it," she said at the end, at the door, coat on. "You've been holding a clause back all evening; you turn your cup when you do it."

He had been. He said it.

"We're both criminals now. In our own systems, by our own hands. I conceal a hostile survey of the realm from my state; you falsify assessments to yours. Neither of us can be caught without the other falling." He held out his hand, aware it was a unit of measure from a world that signed things. "I've read that clause in a hundred agreements. It's the one they put in to make the parties honest, because nothing else does. Terms and conditions."

"Terms and conditions," she said, and shook it, her hand small and warm and certain, and was gone down the stair into the rain, and Adam stood a while in his doorway doing the arithmetic of what had just been founded — two people, two archives, one table — and finding, for the first time since the first pot of tea, no conversation left under the conversation. They had said the whole of it aloud. The table had nothing beneath it now but floor.

* * *

Friday began with a checksum.

"Small thing," Priya said at stand-up, laptop balanced on one arm, in the tone she used for small things, which was the same tone she used for large things, which Adam had never once told her was her best professional feature. "Thursday's broker extract re-verified overnight and the hashes moved. Same vintage stamp, same manifest, same row counts — different checksums on four shards. Ingest logged no new data. Nothing new anywhere in the pipeline, actually, it was a quiet night. And the nightly re-score tightened. Fourteen sites came out a hair sharper — scores up inside the noise floor, edges cleaner. Map's the same map. Just… combed."

"Vendors re-cut extracts," Adam said.

"Vendors re-cut extracts and re-stamp them, because vendors are why we can't have provenance," Priya agreed. "It's the tidiest bug I've seen all year — nothing's wrong except that nothing happened. If the audit were still live I'd chase it, but the audit's in maintenance and I have a calibration chapter due and, Adam, I am *at peace* with maintenance mode. You've no idea. My mother asked what I do now and I said 'quality assurance' and she was so happy."

He looked at the diff for the length of one mug of tea. The mug was the mended one; he had brought it in, and told nobody why, and drunk from the crack side all morning. Fourteen sites, fractionally sharper, on no new data. Once — a month ago, a life ago — he would have put a hold on it, on principle, the principle being that a result which improves while nobody is looking is a result with a private income. But the sites were the same sites; the answer was the same answer; a re-cut extract explained it to one decimal place of plausibility, and his attention, rationed now like everything else in his amended life, had standing obligations elsewhere. Five hundred and thirty-one days of refusing tidy answers, and he took this one at its word — filed it, in the moment, under the heading his whole field kept for such things, which was: fine.

"Log it and cap it," he said. "Maintenance mode."

Priya logged it — PIPE-461, *vendor re-issue suspected, no action, monitor* — closed the laptop, announced that the canteen had the good soup on Fridays and that quality assurance ran on soup, and went to lunch.

The map stayed up on the wall screen behind her, thirty-eight white points on a grey country, the same map it had been all year. Combed.
