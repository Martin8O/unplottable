"Adam Kessler," he said into the grille, and, asked his business, gave it whole. "Full disclosure."

The badge came up out of the slot with the name on top and the business underneath, in the small capitals the Ministry kept for its plainest work, and Orla read it twice while the box took them down. ADAM KESSLER, FULL DISCLOSURE. Six years she had spent among labels, and she had never once seen the machinery print a true one on the first attempt. She decided to take it as the day's omen, on the grounds that it was the only kind she trusted: verifiable, and already behind them.

The folder rode under her arm. It was grey. The matter had come into her life in March in a grey folder, and she had gone out on the Wednesday and bought this one from a Muggle stationer with her own money so that it could leave the same way, which was a joke nobody else in the building would ever be equipped to find, and therefore, by the standards of her year, a private record in excellent order. Inside: the chronology, March to November, dated, in two hands; his appendices, numbered; the fair copy of the board, five rules and a sentence, each rule written out in the hand that had first written it. She went through the inventory once more while the Atrium rose around them — chronology, appendices, board, and the item that was not paper, riding in her pocket with its face to the seam. Her seal.

The letter to Croaker had gone by the half-past-eight post, timed to open at nine: told by her, told first, and told too late to stop any of it, which was the whole of the courtesy she had left to spend on Level Nine. Being told is how you stop a friend being ambushed. She had learnt that in a corridor, off a man holding two coffees.

The lift recited the building to them on the way down, and at Level Two, Department of Magical Law Enforcement, she stepped out under the words she had ridden beneath a hundred times as a colleague and never once as a subject. The duty desk had a young Auror on it who listened to her first sentence, and stood up halfway through her second, and began looking under the counter for a form with an expression Orla recognised from the far side of her own counter in better months.

"My name is Orla Quinn. Assistant Keeper of the Hall of Prophecy; assessor of record on the Muggle matter. This is Dr Adam Kessler." She put the folder on the desk and squared it to the edge. "I have been in breach of the Statute of Secrecy since the twenty-fifth of March. This is the whole of it, dated, in two hands. We are not here to be caught. We're here to table it."

The Ministry had forms for everything except arrival by choice. The young Auror went for help.

The Deputy Head came out to them himself, which told her what the desk had said down the corridor. He looked at the folder and did not touch it.

"Eight months," he said, "this office has planned around two names it did not hold."

"Both names walked in this morning," Orla said. "One of them brought an index."

"We find it saves everyone's afternoon," said Adam.

The Deputy Head took the folder then, the way her own trade took accessions with an ugly history: two hands, no remark. He named the matter's class for his clerk while the ink was still wet on the receiving line, the full citation, the one Orla had entered in the margins of other people's files all her working life — Section 13 of the International Confederation of Warlocks' Statute of Secrecy. Nobody living had known it as a confederation of warlocks; citations keep the elder name. Labels keep everything.

They brought the assessment file up for the formalities, and she asked for it open at the ruled page, the one headed "further exposure", which had stood empty since June and had been the only false sentence anywhere in her care — false by being empty, and hers. She filled it standing, in her own hand: *Quinn, O., assessor of record — from March. Knowledge entire; participation entire.* The page stopped lying in one line, and somewhere upstream of it an April option with eleven words in it quietly lost its subject. Then she took the seal out of her pocket and set it on the desk by the folder, face down, which was not her practice but his, for the serving of bad results; they had been sharing instruments for months.

"You understand," the clerk said, plain, kind, "that nothing said today can be unsaid."

"That's its entire value," Orla said. "Two rooms, is it, or one?"

Two rooms. Statements in their own hands — she asked for that and got it without argument, both trades being agreed for once that testimony is a thing you write, not a thing done to you. The last she saw of Adam for three hours was his back at a small table, jacket off, aligning a borrowed inkwell with the table's edge as though calibrating it, and the door closing on the two of them the way doors close in buildings where everything is now procedure: gently, with a latch.

* * *

Her statement ran to items, because her nerves ran to items, and for once the two of them were pulling in the same direction. Item: the train, the paper, the interview under false pretences and the choice that was not one. Item: the terms, the mug, the seal of the second of April. Item: what the record had done, and what they had done about the record, month by month, canaries and corrections, the reading list, the compartments, the twenty-one weeks of Wednesday sentences that were true and partial. Item: the eighteenth of November, a kitchen, a board. The clerk assigned to her wrote nothing herself; she witnessed, initialled the corners, and kept Orla's teacup filled with the silent efficiency of a woman who had decided which side of the morning she was on.

Adam's appendices went over to be cross-read against hers, and came back with queries in a fine Ministry hand, and one of the queries was the twenty-first of November. She confirmed it as written. *P. Nair — told the true reason, in person, one sentence. Owed since October.* His entry; his debt; paid on the day the board had scheduled, before any of this could make paying it look like housekeeping. What Priya had said back was recorded as "noted", which anybody who knew her could hear said in full.

A few minutes after ten they fetched her out to initial the March line where the two files touched, and that was when she saw, down the length of the Auror floor, past the duty desk and the pillar with the notices on it, Teddy Lupin standing at an open office door with a folder under his arm.

Harry Potter stood in the doorway, reading. He read without decoration, at the pace the thing deserved and no faster, and held one page longer than the rest. Nobody on the floor was looking at the two of them, which was how a floor full of trained observers behaves when it is looking at nothing else. Harry finished, and evened the pages, and handed the folder back. Then he stepped aside and held the door, and stood holding it, an old speech in one motion, and Teddy came down the floor towards the statement rooms alone.

He arrived formal, which with him was information.

"Miss Quinn."

"Mr Lupin."

And then it went out of him all at once, the whole official yard of it, and he leaned on the rail beside her the way he had leaned on the wall by the lifts a month ago, and laughed — the real one, three notes, at nothing that was funny.

"You beat me by an hour."

"I didn't know it was a race."

"No. That's the bit I'll be taking home." He turned the folder over in his hands, not opening it: her year, under a treasury tag. "Register came over Tuesday, under your Department's seal. Nobody was difficult. I read it last night and again at six this morning, and it reads one way, and I've been an Obliviator half my life under a rule I never liked, which is that the subject is the last to hear." He looked at her then, kind and level. "I couldn't bring it to you. First morning since I've known you the rule made sense. So I asked Harry for an hour of his day and walked it up. Not the machine. Him. If it had to be somebody's worst Friday, it wasn't going to start as paperwork in a tray."

"Forty-one readings," she said, "and one designation."

"Two badges and a night-book. You'll have written it better yourself, but yes." He tapped the folder's corner on the rail once, softly, a man knocking on wood. "An hour, Orla. I've costed things all autumn. That's the only margin this year I'd frame."

"It wasn't a margin. We didn't know."

"I know you didn't." His hair — the steady strong-tea brown it wore for work — gave a little at that, at the temples first, towards the blue he kept for weddings, and this time she watched him notice, and leave it. "That's what makes it the good kind."

He straightened, and knocked the rail once more, and was plain. "Go on back in. You've a sentence half-finished in there. I'll be about."

She stood a moment at the rail, in the middle of the floor's professional not-looking.

She had not been caught. In May the sentence had been a thing she turned over in an empty lift, a shame with a shine on it. It ran the other way now. Nobody had caught them. They had chosen it, in their own hands, by appointment, with an index. The difference between the two sentences had turned out to be one hour wide. It was everything they owned. The life the size of a kitchen had gone to pay for it — spent, not taken.

She went back in and finished the sentence.

By early afternoon the machinery had stopped asking what they were and started asking where things went, which in her experience of institutions was the sound of digestion. The folder got a docket number, read aloud to her for confirmation, and she confirmed it. Accessioned. The state had no other way of eating anything; she had spent six years feeding it, and had not once expected to be food. The fair copy of the board was taken away and returned and taken away again, and a request came in for four further copies by Monday: the Minister's box, the Head's box, the file, and the briefing for the January delegation. A summons was drafted, closed session, for the first Monday in December, and nobody consulted her about the date; the machine had stopped needing them by two o'clock. A grey-sleeved wizard from Wizengamot Administration Services read the board's five rules standing at the statement-room door, twice, and asked one question — "The instrument at rule three. Does it exist, or is it wanted?" — and Adam said: "It ran once. August. Five for five." The wizard wrote *exists*, and then, after a visible negotiation with his own conscience, *(once)*.

Nobody had arrested anybody. She understood that to be a decision with a shelf life, renewed hourly, above her floor.

And at some point in the afternoon, through a propped door, she heard a voice she had never met say "the withdrawal clause" — mid-sentence, unremarked, with the definite article — and she stood very still over her teacup, because that was the sound of the thing leaving them. On the eighteenth it had been a board in a kitchen. It had a docket number now, and strangers said its clauses with *the*, and it would be argued over by people who would never know which rules were written in which hand. Her trade had a word for that too. Published.

* * *

At two the lift opened on Ron Weasley, backwards, hauling a crate.

"Merlin's beard, the state of your visitor queue," he announced to the floor at large, by way of a greeting. "Shop's shut. Sign says stocktaking. George is telling everyone I've been carried off by owls — let him, it's the most romance the pair of us have had in years." He set the crate on the duty desk with the authority of a man delivering to his own loading bay. "Right. Sandwiches. Feed the ones holding forms first — learned that in retail. Forms go faster on beef."

The crate was full, wrapped in greaseproof, labelled in a heavy hand. BEEF — THE GOOD MUSTARD. CHEESE AND PICKLE. EGG — DOES WHAT IT SAYS. The duty Auror declined on principle and was overruled on precedent, and the floor, which had spent the morning being careful not to look at anything, discovered it could look at lunch.

Rose came in on her father's free arm.

Three weeks home had put her back on her feet and taken the spare flesh off her patience; she was upright by intention, pale at the edges, and made for the witness desk like something released from a bow. Orla had told her on the Thursday evening — because Rose's price had been inclusion since the day it was set, and you do not stop paying a debt because the creditor is tired — and Rose had listened to the whole of it with her eyes shut and said, at the end: "Friday. I'm coming in Friday."

The witness clerk was gentle, and had guidance.

"Since October, miss, the guidance is that persons in your position are entered in the record as casualties of the matter. It's meant kindly."

"It's taken kindly," Rose said. "I'm electing to testify."

"The list is voluntary—"

"Good. I volunteer."

"There's a box for witness," the clerk said, at the edge of his form and his experience together, "and a box for— there isn't a box for both."

"There's a margin," said Rose Weasley, and held out her hand for the quill. "Sorry," she added, meaning nothing by it whatever.

She signed, and dated it, and got the date right, and stood over the form a moment with the quill still in her fingers as though auditing her own signature for provenance. Then she came and sat by Orla on the visitors' bench, and for a while the two of them let the floor eat sandwiches.

"You gave them everything," Rose said.

"Everything."

"Then we're level. One confession each." A pause, the exact length of a rally. "I'm still ahead on being right, mind."

"You are."

"Good. Somebody in this business should be." She said it lightly, and then she was quiet, and the lightness sat down too. "Ten Galleons," she said at last, to her father, who had appeared with two wrapped parcels in one hand, "that the chamber's first question is whether I'm fit to be asked its second."

"No takers," Ron said.

He put one parcel in Rose's lap — cheese and pickle — and held the other out to Adam, who had come to the bench between rooms, sleeves still down, and Ron looked at him for a long moment, up and down, the frank retail appraisal of a man pricing stock, and Orla watched her whole year balance on a sandwich.

"You'll be the fella," Ron said. "Beef. The good mustard."

"Thank you," Adam said, with precision.

"Don't thank me, it's cupboard love, I'm feeding everyone my daughter's testifying at." He said it at full shop volume, and then, in the other voice, the one from under the counter, not looking at either of them: "Mental, the pair of you. Took me the best part of two months once to walk back into a tent and own up to a tenth of this." He straightened the greaseproof on the crate with two enormous careful hands. "Rosie says you went to her Thursday. Before the government. That's the right order of a thing, that's all I came to check. Eat your sandwich."

Rose stayed forty minutes past what she had, which everyone on the bench could count, and left on Ron's arm with her witness copy folded in her coat, and at the door she turned and held up one finger — *ahead by one* — and was gone before Orla could concede it.

Between her second room and his third, the day gave the two of them eleven minutes on the visitors' bench, two teas in Ministry crockery, and no obligation to perform anything for anybody. Neither performed. He sat with his shoulder against her arm, the way the whiteboard had taught them both, and drank his tea at exactly his own speed.

"What did Priya say?" she asked. "On the twenty-first. The unabridged edition."

"That it was her keyboard, so it was her result too, and I was to file it under both names." He drank some of his tea. "I have complied."

"They've queried the fair copy," he said at last.

"On what ground?"

"The ink. Two blacks. They want to know whether a page was added later."

She looked down the floor at the machine digesting their year. "Nobody asked about the hands."

"No," he said. He turned his cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, squaring it to a table that was not there. "Good."

At half past six a runner came down the floor for the two of them.

The Head of the DMLE's office had a lamp lit and the window's weather off — plain dark glass, the Ministry's own November. Hermione Granger sat behind a desk with one folder on it, theirs, open past its middle, and did not look up. Two chairs had been placed. They sat. The building emptied downwards around them, floor by floor, lift by lift, and she read to the last page in silence — no quill, no flags, one page turned back once, at the board's fifth rule, and read again — at the pace of somebody who intended to be examined on it, which was not a speed but a practice. Orla's seal lay on the blotter beside the folder, face down, exactly as it had been surrendered; it had come up through the building by hands unknown, the way everything in her life now would.

Hermione closed the folder. She squared it to the edge of the desk, and set her hand flat on the cover — holding it shut, or holding it safe. Orla could not have told which, and understood that not being able to tell was the next eight weeks of her life.

"You came as the file."

Neither of them answered. It had not been asked. She had sat in this office in May, being excellent, two loyalties lying along each other so exactly that the woman behind the desk had seen one person, whole, and thanked her at the door; she had gone down through the reciting building that morning uncaught and carried the word like a stone. There was no seam left in her anywhere. That was what they had bought, and this room was where it was kept now, under a flat hand.

"Section Thirteen. You know that."

"We know."

"Good. Then we begin."
