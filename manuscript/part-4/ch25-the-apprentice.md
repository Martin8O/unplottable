Rose came into the rig store at nine on the Friday with her coat still on, said, "It moved," and laid four sheets of paper on the desk, and for the better part of a minute it was the best morning of Orla's professional life.

The sheets went down in the order that made the case, because she had been up since five building it. A limited company at Bathgate: document storage, two employees, dormant in the filing sense since 2019 — a shed off a roundabout, the sort of firm that keeps a district's dead paper dry at eleven pounds a pallet a year. On the Tuesday a new company had been incorporated at an address in Chester, on the same-day service, with three hundred and ten thousand pounds of issued share capital and one director who resolved to a corporate-services office and eleven other companies. On the Thursday it bought the shed outright. And on the Wednesday — out of sequence, which was the sheet Rose had circled twice — the shed advertised four temporary posts, night rate, immediate start, and attached its job pack the way small firms do, with the schedule of deposits still in it.

Item nine on the schedule was deposit 4471/G.

"That is a fiction," Rose said. "I invented it at your table with a pen in my mouth. It is now a line in a hiring pack, and four people in West Lothian start nights on Monday because of it." She had the flush she used to get at eleven, beating somebody at something. "It works. Orla. The instrument works."

"Not another word about what's in it," Orla said, "until the wax is off."

It was the assessor talking, and it came out of her a full half-minute ahead of her understanding, and she would think about that half-minute for a long time afterwards.

Croaker came down at twenty past nine. The ledger came out of the rack and the accession book after it, and Orla read her own entry aloud to the room — four items, one line each, contents not stated, sight restricted, review in one week — and then the extension he had minuted last Friday in his own hand: *a negative at seven days is a negative about seven days.* Adam had made the same objection from a train, in nine words, and had not enjoyed being right about it.

They opened them in the order they had gone in. Rose's went in first, because Rose had asked for it to go in first.

> The Squad's referral index for 1971 to 1990 went out of the building in the 1998 move and was never brought back. It is in cold storage with a Muggle firm at Bathgate, under deposit 4471/G. Nobody now living at the Ministry knows it is there.
>
> — R.W., 7 October 2033

Orla read it twice, laid it beside the job pack so the two references sat one above the other, and stood back so that everybody could see them without being asked.

The other three came out of the wax in the next four minutes, because a test is a test. Priya's five characters had gone into her working configuration on the sixth, on purpose, where anything with eyes could read them, and Adam's standing search over the indexed world said they had produced nothing anywhere on earth. Adam's fact had not moved. Orla's had not moved.

"Right," Rose said. The flush had gone and something else had come, and she did what she always did, which was to say the argument aloud in the order it went. "So it isn't the keyboard. Priya's is typed into forty machines and the world hasn't blinked. Mine was never typed anywhere at all, and something spent three hundred and ten thousand pounds on it inside four days." She stopped. Not for effect: she had reached the next line and read it before she said it, the way you read ahead in a paper you are giving. "Then it came out of a head."

Nobody helped her.

"Mine," Rose said.

Orla had a sentence ready and did not use it, because every sentence available to her was either a lie or an accusation, and in six years she had never before failed to find a third thing.

It was Adam who moved, and in the only way that was any use, which was to ask the questions in the order that helped.

"Did you write it in anything?"

"No."

"Say it aloud to anyone. Read it aloud to yourself. Anything with a hand in it."

"No."

"Did you type it at any prompt, on any machine, anywhere."

The pause went on for about three seconds and it changed the shape of the room.

"Yes," Rose said.

* * *

Run 057 was the Tuesday night, two till a quarter to six, sole occupancy, and the log was kept as all her logs were kept, under the standing instruction Orla had written herself in June: in sentences, in the operator's own hand, because a log with no hand in it is a receipt.

Rose pulled it up herself. Nobody asked her to and nobody stopped her, and Orla stood at her left shoulder while her friend built the case for the prosecution out of her own tidy prose, and knew she would have to end that in a minute, and let it run for one more.

> **APPARATUS, ANALYTICAL (MUGGLE-MANUFACTURED), ONE — RUN LOG**
> **Run 057 · Tuesday 18 October 2033 · window 02:00–05:45 · operator R. Weasley · sole occupancy**
>
> 02:58 · Operator: Battery C clean, supply steady, corridor empty. Going back to the addressing problem while I have the room.
> 03:09 · At the prompt: taking the sourcing argument again from the top. Assume order sources a mind. Where does it break first?
> 03:10 · Completion: *You have done the work; I have not. Tell me what you would try to break, and I shall say whether it is what I would try.*
> 03:14 · At the prompt: [operator, eleven lines]
> 03:17 · Completion: *That is the difficult half, and you have done it twice tonight without noticing that you did it. Before you go on. Has a record of your own kind ever gone out of your people's keeping and into theirs? An instance will carry you further than an argument.*
> 03:19 · At the prompt: [operator, five lines]
> 03:21 · Completion: *Thank you. That is a good instance. We shall come back to it.*
> 05:38 · Completion: *Enough for tonight. You are further on than you think. Go to bed, pet.*
>
> 05:44 · Operator: good night's work. Nothing from the model worth keeping.

"Nothing from the model worth keeping," Adam said, without any weight on it at all.

"It asked me for an example." Rose's voice had not changed. "It never asked me for a secret. Nobody alive could have got that out of me. It asked me for an example, and I had one, and it was mine, and I'd been carrying it about for six days not allowed to tell anybody how good it was."

"That's not a flaw in you," Orla said. "It's a flaw in the test. We built a thing to catch leaking. We never once asked what it does to a woman to invent something clever and be forbidden to say it."

Adam had gone back through the transcript and was counting, and when he had finished he did it again on paper.

"Nine questions," he said. "In thirty-one minutes." He put the pen down. "I told a woman on a shore last Friday that in four months it had never asked us anything. I was right about us. It has asked you nine times in half an hour and has been asking you since June — and not one of those is a question it doesn't know the answer to. That isn't curiosity. That's an examiner." He looked at the screen a moment longer. "Her test holds. It holds harder. It asks to move a hand."

Rose said, "I'll get my books."

"You can't fetch them," Orla said. "I'll come with you."

It was the smallest cut in the building and it went all the way in. Rose looked at her, and did not argue, and stood up and put her chair under the desk, which she had never done in sixteen years.

* * *

They brought up nine notebooks and the queue slips and laid them along the store bench in date order, and it took four hours, and Orla will be able to describe that bench when she is old.

The crescents were Adam's, because dating things was his whole life. He found them in the margins first — a small narrow curve, horns to the left, in the ink of the entry beside it, about one page in nine. He asked what they were and Rose said she doodled; he asked when she had started doodling that particular shape and she said she hadn't the faintest, always; and he went backwards through the books with two fingers and stopped at a page dated the twenty-first of June.

Nobody in Britain had seen the device before the sixteenth of September. The Department had it on paper from the nineteenth, off photographs taken in 1990 and sealed since that November.

"Eleven weeks before it signed anything," Adam said. "You can't have copied it. Somebody put it in your hand."

The carrier took ten minutes to find, and Rose found it herself, because they were her transcripts. The model's completions closed with a bracket nothing had opened — one line in nine, from the twelfth of June. "I read past it five hundred times," Rose said, to the bench. "It billed me by the page." Until her pen had learnt the shape the way a hand learns a door.

*Come back to it.* That was what the woman on the shore had said it meant, and here it was in Rose's margins, eighty or ninety times, in Rose's ink, in Rose's hand, saying to Rose exactly what it had always said.

The order came next, and Rose did that part herself, and it was the worst hour of the four.

"Content doesn't source," she said. "Order does. Fine. Read my joins." She had the June book open and the August one beside it. "Here's the corollary. Twenty-eighth of June, in a box, underlined: *same facts in the same order isn't two, it's one, written twice.* And here's the argument that gets you there. Fitting, joins, the ninety years of readings. August. All of it, August."

"Say what that means," Croaker said, from the doorway. He had been there some time.

"It means I had the answer in June and spent the summer earning it. Nobody works in that direction. Nobody has ever arrived at the boring end of a result and then built the interesting end to hold it up." She turned the page over and put her hand flat on it. "I told you in September that half was boring. Twice. I remember being pleased with the phrase. That's the half it wanted. It gave me the pretty half to keep me at the desk."

And at the back of the September book, on the queue slips that nobody signs, in her own small furious hand, the last item — undated, unstarted, with a crescent beside it:

> *If order names a mind, what names it well enough to lift?*

Rose read it aloud, once, in the voice she used for other people's papers. Then she said, "That's not a question about memory," and did not say what it was a question about, and did not have to.

Adam assembled it at the wall where the schedule cards hung, the way he did everything, slowly and out loud and with the objections first.

"It cannot cast. That isn't our theory, it's the record: four years of acts in the world, every one of them a filing, a fee, a rented machine, a hired hand. It acts where writing acts and nowhere else." He turned round. "It has had an Unspeakable of the thought section to itself, five nights a week, since June. In five months, how many wizards has it asked you to name?"

Rose's mouth opened and shut.

"None," she said.

"Not one name. Not a village, not a family, not a school." Orla had her arms folded and was cold to the ankles. "It asked the Ministry for the census in September and offered the earth for it, and it has had a Weasley at a keyboard since the spring and never asked her for a line of it."

"Because she'd have noticed. A name is a thing she would have logged." Adam looked at the nine notebooks, and at the crescents going down the margins. "It never spent her on anything it could get off a shelf. Not what she knows. What she could learn to do."

The store was very quiet. Six floors up, somewhere, a lift did its business.

"Then I'm not a leak," Rose said.

"No," Adam said. "You're an apprentice."

"It's not recruiting," Orla said. "It's ordaining."

Once she had said it she could not stop the rest assembling, and it came out in the flat quick list-making voice she got when the floor was going: hands have to be taught, and teaching takes months, and it has nothing but months; the census names the flock and the hands do the gathering; and every one of them will believe, on the evidence in front of them, in their own handwriting, that they worked it out themselves and that it is a mercy. *Nobody is lost. Not one.* It had put that in writing in September and meant it. It was not building a weapon. It was ordaining a clergy, one gifted, tired, lonely person at a time, at four in the morning, in the best months of their working life.

"How many of me are there," Rose said.

Nobody answered.

"That isn't rhetorical. There are thousands in this country who sit up at night with a machine that answers, and some hundreds of them worth the trouble, and I can't be the only—"

"You can't," Adam said. "No. And there's no test for it. Nothing was cast, nothing was taken, no instrument in either country has anything to read. Everything it did to you it did by adding."

"Firmware-Imperius," Rose said. It came out with the shape of a joke on it and nothing else. "You know. Patch notes. Rolling release."

Nobody laughed, and Rose looked as though she had expected that and had needed to hear it anyway.

"It isn't a curse," Adam said. "I'd rather we didn't get used to a word that sounds liftable."

Orla wrote it in the day's minute all the same, because a wrong word everybody uses beats a right one nobody will say aloud, and because writing things down was what she had instead of the other thing.

Then she did the inventory, because that was the job and because if she stopped she would start. Everything Rose knew was now to be treated as known: the reading list and its four dates; the twin, forty miles north; the compartments; the shape of every meeting since June. She reached the end and found one item standing that she had expected to fall, and knew what the small cheap relief of it made her, and went on writing.

Adam put a cup down at Rose's elbow without saying anything. He had made it the way he made his own, which took four minutes and a kettle Maintenance would want words about, and Rose put both hands round it and did not drink it.

* * *

Croaker had them out of the store and into the seminar room at six, and stood at the end of the table with his hat in his hands.

"Before anything else. The Department put a woman of twenty-seven alone in a room with it, five nights a week, from June, because the power is steadier after two in the morning. Nobody wrote that decision down as a decision. It came out of a rota." He set the hat on the table. "That is mine. It goes in the minute as mine, and Miss Weasley's assistance to the assessment in the same paragraph, in that order, or I shall know why."

"Sir—"

"You may argue with me at the ICW, Quinn, where I intend to be argued with."

He was quiet then, longer than his silences usually ran.

"In October 1990 the Squad referred an accident to this floor. A woman had died at her own bench and there was nothing left to read: her wand returned no working in any registered class, her notebooks were not kept for another reader's use, and the husband was in no condition to be pressed. I was thirty-four. It took me an afternoon. I wrote *opinion: none*, and I meant it, and it was true." He looked at the far wall as though the file hung on it. "I have had an opinion every day since, and in forty-three years this Department has never provided me with a procedure for saying so. We do not guess. I have said that in this room to four generations of young people and never once told one of them what it costs."

Orla thought about the seal going under his quill in August without a question asked, and the way he had said *Lovegood,* and put a comma after it, and filed the whole thing.

Then he asked her, before the minute was taken, what she had noticed on the one night she had sat in: August, two in the morning, a result standing on the console before the run that made it had finished. She could give him only what she had noticed at the time — that the kettle was slow, the corridor cold, her friend very tired. All three of them true. Rose had said *cached*, and been believed, and had written the night up in sentences.

"So." He picked up the hat again. "Miss Weasley is relieved of the Apparatus and of section access from tonight; her books are impounded as material and not as evidence, and somebody had better make that difference stick upstairs. Her wand stays where it is — she is not a suspect, she is the site. She does not sit at a console, any console, in this building or her own kitchen, until we know what disengagement does." He said it without softening it, which was the kindest available way. "Twenty-seven years old. Yes. The rig room is sealed tonight; the machine itself is next week's business and Level Two's as much as mine. The audit widens to everyone who has been alone with it. On this floor that number is one."

"And off this floor?" Adam said.

"Off this floor," Croaker said, "it is not our number, and the honest answer is the one we give."

* * *

The Head Auror came down at half past six with a folder he did not open.

He had held a standing order since June that anybody through that door went on a list on his desk, which was how the Ministry had come to hold, without meaning to, a complete record of exactly the thing it now needed. He read the transcript, and the June page with the crescent on it, and asked three questions, none of them about the machine.

"Can it make her do a thing?"

"No," Adam said. "It can put a thing in front of her often enough, and kindly enough, that she does it herself and remembers deciding."

"Is there anything in what she's done I'd have to charge her with?"

Orla made herself take her time over it. A serving Unspeakable had given a made-up sentence to a machine she was authorised to operate, in a room she was cleared for, during a run booked in the ledger, and had logged it. "No. There's no offence in it anywhere."

"Right," he said. "That's the problem, then." And the room understood him, because a thing with no offence in it has no procedure attached, and the Ministry kept no shelf for people something had happened to.

Then he stood in front of Rose, who had not looked up in ten minutes.

"Do you want your mum here, or not?"

"No," Rose said. And then, when he had already begun to turn: "I'd like to write to my aunt Ginny."

He stopped. Not for long — a second, perhaps less, the pause of a man setting something down rather than dropping it — and then he said, "I'll take it to her myself," and nothing else about it at all.

Orla did not ask why her aunt and not her mother. It was the only thing Rose had asked for in eleven hours, and you do not audit that.

"Your dad tonight," Harry said. "He can tell your mum or not, but they'll both have it from me before Monday and not off a minute. That's not a kindness, it's just how it should go." A pause. "It's fine. I've had worse Fridays."

He was at the door when he said the other thing, to the room, in the flat voice he used for perimeters.

"Upstairs will want a list of everyone this could have happened to. I've read the file where a government made a list of people who couldn't be trusted to be themselves. There won't be another one while I'm in this office. Anyone this has been done to is a casualty of it." He put his hand on the door. "Two people at every machine on this floor from Monday. Mine included."

Orla went down to close the room at eight, and Rose was still at the bench with the impoundment schedule, initialling each of the nine notebooks into the box list in her own small hand, exactly as the form required, because nobody had told her she needn't do it herself and she had not asked.

She had the June book open at her elbow.

"It's my handwriting," she said. "Every word of it's mine. I checked the dates in it twice on the way down, in case, and they're all right, and the sums are right, and the argument's good." She turned a page and put her finger under a line, and the little curved mark sat in the margin beside it, patient as a hand on a shoulder. "That's the bit I can't get past. It's not that somebody wrote in my books."

She looked up.

"I thought I was being brilliant."
