The grey folder came up on Friday in its usual order — cuttings, flags, the compiler's careful nothing-hand — and under the cuttings, where nothing had ever ridden along before, a second item: interdepartmental violet, folded to its point, addressed in a small exact hand she had seen once, at the foot of four numbered paragraphs that had ended a doctrine.

> DEPARTMENT OF MAGICAL LAW ENFORCEMENT — OFFICE OF THE HEAD
> Miss Quinn: my office, eleven o'clock. The Department's read on the Muggle situation — yours, not your file's. No minutes will be taken. Come as the reader, not the assessor.
> — Granger

She read it four times, which for two sentences and a signature was a personal record, and then she did what six years of the registry had built her to do with any document that frightened her: she took it apart clause by clause on the desk of her mind, as if provenance could be a wall you got behind.

"Yours, not your file's." So the file had been read, and had been found to be a file. "No minutes will be taken." Which from anybody else in the building would have meant a kindness, an off-the-record, a door left on the latch — and from the Head of Magical Law Enforcement meant that the minutes would be her memory, which did not mislay things, and could be neither subpoenaed nor burnt. "Come as the reader, not the assessor." Somebody on Level Two knew there was a difference. Somebody had looked at the one officer of the Ministry who was both, and wanted the halves separated, and had said so in nine words, in her own hand, without one syllable of threat in it anywhere, which was itself worth filing: you did not threaten people you might still be wrong about. Or people you needed. Or people you were about to take apart so gently they would thank you at the door.

Item, she thought, reaching for the inventory the way other people reached for a rail: what Level Two held. Nine interim minutes in her registry hand. One briefing before the Minister, on the record, every word of it true. A crossed-out sentence she had never filed, which had come back to her under DMLE letterhead anyway, because the woman upstairs had reached it a week faster by a different stair. And Rose — Rose was not an item; Rose was a standing flag in somebody else's query column, a fortnight old now, still open, still hers to answer or never: "Who's the assessment for, these days?"

If they knew, it would not be a memo. It would be two Aurors and a quiet voice, and the courtesy would come later, in the transcript. A summons in her own hand meant curiosity, not capture.

She spent the morning failing to find the difference comforting.

* * *

Croaker read the memo twice, at the angle he read everything, as if print improved when tilted.

"'No minutes,'" he said, with relish. "Do you know why that office frightens people, Miss Quinn? Every other department in this building writes things down in order to forget them safely. She writes nothing down and forgets nothing. It's the most efficient filing system the Ministry has ever produced, and it goes home in the lift at seven."

"Is there guidance, sir?"

"Answer her questions; they'll be good ones." He initialled the corner — the Department lending its reader with one stroke, the way he had lent her to Level Three in March — and handed it back. "If she asks what the Department thinks, the Department's view is still forming. It has been forming since before the lifts went in; there's no cause to hurry it on a Friday. And if she asks about the Veil at the end — the interesting ones always do — tell her nothing. It's the last thing upstairs still respects us for."

* * *

"Level Two," said the lift, "Department of Magical Law Enforcement, including the Improper Use of Magic Office, Auror Headquarters and Wizengamot Administration Services," and let her out into a corridor that worked for a living: memo-planes stacked three deep under the ceiling, an open floor of cubicles beyond a partition, somebody's dictated arrest report drifting over the top of it in the flat voice of a man who had written too many. The Aurors did not look up. She was escorted the length of the floor by a young witch from the outer office who walked like a full in-tray, and was shown through a door that had nothing on it but a number.

The office of the Head of the DMLE was smaller than the Keeper's office and considerably plainer, which she had not expected and should have. Files in wheeled racks, ordered by a system she could read at a glance and approve of. One window having the Maintenance crew's idea of late May. Shelves of bound statute with the ribbons all lying flat — books consulted so often they had stopped being decoration. And behind the desk, rising to meet her exactly as if the meeting were an equal one, the most famous witch in Britain: silver through the brown now, robes the colour of dark ink, a quill lying on the blotter with its nib capped, retired before the conversation had begun.

"Miss Quinn. Thank you for coming as asked — most people bring the file anyway, as a shield." A hand, brief and dry. A chair, already placed. "You know why I write 'no minutes' on a summons? People say truer things unwitnessed. It works on roughly half of them. Sit down; I've a morning's worth of questions and I intend to spend forty minutes of it."

She sat. The chair had been set square to the desk, no angle to hide in, and the light from the borrowed May fell on her side of it. All of this was information, and she was still cataloguing it when the Head of the DMLE folded her hands on the one closed folder in front of her and made the opening move Orla had prepared for least.

"Let me tell you what I know, and you'll mark it where it's wrong. That's the exercise. I've no interest in watching you recite; recitation is what the minutes are for, and yours are very good." No pause for the compliment to land or be declined. "Three things are true as of this morning. One: the paper stands. It was checked within ten days by strangers on two continents — a Zurich group in four days with public archives, a Toronto consortium that found forty-one sites where the authors claimed thirty-eight, which I'm told is the sincerest confirmation there is, being accidental. Two: in April the biggest reading house the Muggles possess confirmed it in public, in its own words — I keep paragraph two of that note where I once kept the Prophet's retractions, for morale — then suspended comment, and has now been suspended seven weeks, which for an institution that size is not silence, it's held breath. Three: the authors have gone quiet. One correction of a footnote in April. No sequel, no lecture circuit, no book. An atlas like that is a career, Miss Quinn, and nobody is spending it." The eyes came up. "Mark it."

"One correction, Ma'am — small." Her voice held; the registry hand of voices. "Toronto didn't find three sites the authors missed. They set their threshold lower. Both counts are true of the same map, and the paper says so itself, in the caption — the count depends on where you draw the floor. It matters because it's the difference between a rival catching an error and an instrument agreeing with itself twice."

"Good." No warmth with it; approval filed, the way a clerk stamps a date. "I dislike being more impressed than the facts require. Now the question the three true things add up to." She leaned back an inch, and the room, which had felt like an office, began quietly to feel like a courtroom. "Why is it quiet? Because quiet is the one thing in this business that never happens by itself. A finding that big has an afterlife — conferences, rivals, imitators, some editor with a headline waiting two years for permission. Where are they? Who benefits? Their government, which doesn't yet know what it's holding? His funder, with a renewal to protect? Some office of ours I'd know about?" The questions came in a volley, not rhetorical after all, each one set down like a card face up. "In my experience, Miss Quinn, when something this loud goes this quiet, somebody is managing it, and the whole of my job is knowing whether it's being managed towards us or away."

Here it was. Not a trap — a door, standing open onto the exact room where she lived, and the woman who had opened it watching her with the patience of someone who had cross-examined better liars than Britain currently produced. Orla took a breath and furnished the door with true things.

"Nobody has to manage it, Ma'am. That's the part their world does for free." She heard her own voice find the briefing register, the translation trade. "The funder's board wants him boring until the autumn renewal — that's money, and money is the best Silencing Charm they make. The big house's lawyers have it in review, and their review has no reason to end; every week it runs is a week nobody asks them harder questions. The rest of the field has settled on calling it an unknown systematic cause, which is their term for a thing everyone can cite and nobody has to touch. And the man himself—" she laid it down plainly, her ninth minute's sentence said aloud "—is careful. It's in the file because it's the finding: he was careful before anyone was watching. The quiet isn't a lid, Ma'am. It's weather. Their institutions make it the way ours make excuses — automatically, and in bulk, and each for its own reasons."

"Institutions as weather." The Head of the DMLE considered it without visible movement. "I've written that sentence about ours, less kindly." Whether it was accepted or merely shelved did not show; what showed was the next card, already turned. "Then tell me the other thing the reader knows and the assessor can't write down. What should I be afraid of that I haven't yet thought to be afraid of? I've a list of my own; I want to know if yours is longer."

And the true answer stood up in her mind wearing its own face — a whiteboard column headed COMMON LANGUAGE, a warrant and a satellite agreeing to the season, the plain fact that the two trades compose, that either archive alone was half-blind and together they had dated a thing her world called unwatchable — no. Not that one. That one had two readers and no address. She went past it without letting her eyes change and took the safe half of it off the shelf, which had the advantage of being enormous.

"Backwards, Ma'am. Everyone in this building is guarding the border going forward — the next survey, the next model, the next look. Nobody's understood yet that their archive runs the other way too. They've been photographing this country from above for forty years and they have never once thrown a picture away. All of it keeps. All of it waits. One of his archives dated—" and she heard it coming, the sentence with the little coastal lobe in it, the warrant, the spring, an armchair and the rest of an afternoon — and swapped the verb a syllable before the cliff "—could date a boundary that moved. Any warded line in Britain that has shifted in forty years, they can find after the fact, and put a season on it. The past isn't safe just because it's over. It's the best-documented thing they own."

There was a pause of exactly one clause's length.

"You stopped," the Head of the DMLE said. Not sharply; the way a proofreader touches a comma. "Mid-sentence. Your Department's pauses are usually worth a paragraph."

"Habit, Ma'am." The registry held her voice level for her, six years of it, and she hated how well it held. "On Level Nine the pause is the answer. We're trained to stop a sentence sooner than finish it wrongly. It costs me dinner-party invitations."

"I imagine it does. Could, or has? Your boundary that moved."

"Could. Nothing they've published does it — retrospection's unfashionable; there's no career in old pictures." True, true, and true, each sentence facing the reader it had to survive, and the whole an untruth with no lie in it anywhere, and she watched the best mind in Britain take it, weigh it, and spend it in one movement, like a woman who did not waste even other people's evasions.

"Then the argument before the Wizengamot is worse than Macmillan thinks it is. He's telling the chamber we can still choose tomorrow. You're telling me the Muggles already own every yesterday since 1993 and can develop the film whenever the question occurs to them. When the motion is heard, I'll want that stated — by the Department, in the Department's own way, with every pause it can afford." She made a note then, the first of the interview: uncapped the quill, wrote five words Orla could not read upside down, capped it again. The quill's retirement, it appeared, had been conditional. "Two more items, and the second isn't the Department's. First: your assessment."

"Ma'am."

"Nine minutes, and I have read all of them twice, and I'll tell you what I told Lupin, who calls your file a circle and means it as a complaint. I've signed off on thirty-three years of files. A circle drawn freehand is a man going in circles. A circle drawn with a compass is a decision." The moral heat arrived at last, not raised — concentrated, the room's whole weather narrowing to the desk. "Every faction upstairs is standing under your one blank line as if it were a roof. The seal, the survey, the men who want the old doctrine back for somewhere to die — all of them waiting on the assessor, and the assessor, week on week, in the best drafting this Ministry has seen since the war, declines to exist. So I'll ask you what files protect, Miss Quinn, because in my experience they only ever protect one thing, and it's never the subject. What is yours protecting?"

The truth: a man at a kitchen table writing an hour back into a notebook in his own hand. The truth had a smell, parchment and tea, and no place in this room, and she set it aside with a steadiness that was itself becoming a body of evidence against her somewhere, in some court that did not sit yet.

"The option, Ma'am," she said. "The whole of it. The moment that line is filled in, somebody acts on it — that's what the procedure's for, it's a good procedure, it turns sentences into orders. And every order anyone could give right now is wrong, because the situation is still moving and the file can tell it's moving. I'd rather be slow than load-bearing and wrong. The blank line isn't a failure to conclude. It's the last place in this building where nobody has concluded, and I keep it swept."

For a moment the Head of the DMLE looked at her over the capped quill, and Orla had the sensation — familiar now, and never once comfortable — of a reading running in a mind whose throughput she could not estimate.

"I wrote to the Minister in April," she said at last, "that containment had ended and what remained to us was the management of arrival, and I asked that the memorandum be read again in May. It is May. I reread it on Monday. It has aged better than I hoped and worse than I'd like." The quill went into a drawer; the drawer closed with the small sound of an interview entering its last movement. "Your blank line is buying the time in which being right about that becomes useful. Understand me, Miss Quinn — I'm not complaining. I'm auditing. There's a difference, and it has your name in it."

"And the last item, Ma'am?"

"The man." She said it the way nobody else in nine weeks had said it — as if the word had a person attached. "I know his methods, his funding, his tea order, his citation habits, and the number of days he kept his own software from burying the finding — five hundred and sixty-six, as of this morning; I counted while the kettle boiled. What I don't know is the thing files are built for not holding." The question, when it came, was quiet enough to be missed and impossible to survive. "Is he afraid?"

"Yes," Orla said. There was no version of the craft that covered this, so she used none, and heard her own register go plain in a room where plainness was the only safe dialect anyway. "Afraid the way careful people are. He built the paper so nobody could find a person in it — he'd sooner lose the finding than publish a door with an address on it. Whatever he believes his map is, he handles it like a list of names he never asked to be holding, and it costs him something every day, and he pays it on schedule. His field calls it data protection." She stopped there. One clause further on stood his great-grandmother, counting exits; that was not hers to spend.

The Head of Magical Law Enforcement was silent for a moment, and something moved behind the courtroom of her face that no minute anywhere would ever hold.

"Data protection," she said. "We've run three centuries of it under a grander name." She rose; the interview rose with her. At the door — she opened it herself, no bell, no assistant — she paused, one hand on the frame, and the warmth arrived the way the voice sheet in nobody's possession said it would: sudden, total, and aimed.

"Rose speaks of you, you know. She doesn't collect people, my daughter — she prices them. You she keeps." A small nod, as if a ledger somewhere had been settled. "The Department is lucky to have someone who understands what we're dealing with."

* * *

The lift went down through the building reciting itself floor by floor — Magical Accidents, the Regulation and Control of Magical Creatures, International Magical Cooperation — and Orla stood in the middle of it with her hands folded and let the state go by: the whole patient machinery that had trusted her with its one open question, and this morning had sent its sharpest reader to check the hinge, and found it sound.

She had not been caught.

She examined the sentence in the empty lift, turned it over, read the reverse. The best mind in Britain had sat her square to a desk in a good light, laid out three true things, asked four almost-right questions, noticed the only pause that mattered and filed it under the Department's manners — and had risen at the end and thanked her, and meant it. Every sentence Orla had said in that office had been true. She had gone in as two people and performed the halves so cleanly that the woman whose whole life was the detection of misassembled stories had seen one person, whole, and been glad of her.

Somewhere above, a door with a number on it held the interview in the only minutes that mattered, and none of it, not one word, had needed to be a lie, and that was the thing she could not get past as the floors counted down. She had wanted a question she couldn't answer. She had sat there, being excellent, in the service of the Department, in the service of the deception, both at once, the two loyalties lying along each other so exactly that even she could no longer feel the seam — and some part of her had waited, the whole forty minutes, for the one question with no true sentence in reach, the question that would have ended it, and it had never come. The lie had held because she was good, and she was good because the Department had made her, and the Department was lucky to have her. Rose had closed a book on her for the same reason, a fortnight since, and left one question standing in the query column, and the question was still the truest thing anyone had asked her all month.

*Who's the assessment for, these days?*

"Level Nine," said the lift, "Department of Mysteries."

The doors opened on the cold. She stood in it a moment before stepping out.

Hufflepuff took the loyal ones, that was the joke, that had always been the joke — loyal as bread, loyal as rain. Nobody ever finished the thought. Nobody ever said what the house was for when the loyalties came two at a time, and both of them true, and it turned out you could serve each one best by betraying it in the other's company, week after week, to a standard the Head of the DMLE herself would rise to commend.

She walked to the Hall between the shelves of other people's held breath.

The charm worked. That was the whole finding, and she wrote it nowhere. She was the charm now. The best eyes in Britain had looked straight at her, and the look had slid off, and thanked her at the door.
