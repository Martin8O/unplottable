The Ministry of Magic let its visitors in through a broken telephone box, and on the way down it issued them a name badge, which was the first thing about the place Adam understood completely.

"Orla Quinn, Department of Mysteries," Orla said to the dialling tone, "escorting the manufacturer's engineer for the Department's Apparatus. Maintenance."

Every word of it was true. That had been the argument, three weeks ago, and she had won it the way she won arguments, by quoting her own trade at him: you build the lie out of truth wherever truth will carry the weight, because a true name needs no rehearsing and cannot be contradicted by a photograph. A badge clattered into the coin chute like change from a call nobody had made. ADAM KESSLER — APPARATUS MAINTENANCE. He pinned it over his sternum, where a colleague at a beamline had once worn her dosimeter, and the floor of the box began to sink through the pavement of a side street that did not, on any map he had access to, have a telephone box in it.

The Atrium at one in the morning was a cathedral with the congregation removed: dark wood, a peacock-blue ceiling with its golden symbols still working, a fountain running for nobody. Their footsteps went up into the vault and came back embarrassed. At the far end, at a desk under a lamp, a watchwizard four hours into a night shift put down his mug and picked up a golden rod.

The rod went down his front and up his back, unhurried. Then it did it again.

"Nothing," the watchwizard said. He looked at the rod, and then at Adam, with the specific suspicion of a professional whose instrument has just returned a value outside its working range. "Everyone's got something. Charmed watch. Coin their gran did something to. You've got nothing at all."

"He's equipment," Orla said. "It's on the docket."

The docket was on the desk with Croaker's initials in the corner, and Adam watched the reading do its work, because he had learnt this in March and it held at every scale: in this building, a docket on a thing outranked the thing. The watchwizard copied the badge into the night-book in a careful hand — *Kessler, Adam. Apparatus maintenance. Level Nine, equipment and escort.* — and waved them at the lifts, and went back to his mug, the closest any man came that night to the fact of the matter: nothing at all was exactly what Adam had. He had spent two years mapping holes in other people's data. It had not, until the rod, occurred to him that he was one — that to every instrument this world could point at a man, he was a stretch of moor.

The lift fell past eight announced floors and their recited inventories, said, "Department of Mysteries," and nothing else, and opened on a corridor of bare walls and black stone and one plain black door at the far end. The tersest line in the building, Orla had told him once, was the accurate one.

Rose was waiting at the store with the rack humming behind her and her sleeves rolled.

"Badge," she said, approving. "Very lawful. If we're caught, they'll have to admit the paperwork was beautiful."

* * *

July had ended the arguments one at a time, the way a good month should.

The suspects table in the two-heads notebook had been closed since the second week: no rival, no state, no instrument — the evals right, the geometry upstream, every mundane candidate dead of a named cause and buried with a date. What survived was a shape with no noun. It read; it went and looked; it corrected. And a thing that could do those had to be *somewhere*, which was the sentence Adam had written at the top of a fresh page on the twelfth of July, because it was the first claim since March that suggested its own experiment. Software was not somewhere. Patterns were. If something lived in the record, it lived thickest where the record had been handled — and the group owned, on eight nodes at the end of this corridor, the means to gather every shard the Cartographer had ever re-vintaged, every neighbourhood of the reference corpus where the screen had failed to place what it found, and run the Department's own copy of the model over the whole of it, flat out, off every network in the world.

That got them density. An instrument was another matter: nothing of his could detect what they proposed to look for — his entire science was the practice of measuring around that hole. It was Orla who had supplied the other half, on a Tuesday, in the voice she used for saying enormous things quickly.

"Then we borrow one of ours. The Department keeps specimens." And, when he only looked: "Dementors, Adam. From when they cleared Azkaban. There's a containment programme, it's older than me, and the one thing everyone knows about the poor devils is the one thing we need. They're blind. They feel their way. Nothing on earth reads *soul* at range except the thing my civilisation is most ashamed of, and we own four of them."

Croaker had signed it in nine days, which Orla swore was a Department record. The file's own language had done the carrying — the Apparatus had been bought for *verification*, and verification, her memorandum observed, could not decently stop at Muggle instruments while the Department's own instrument-classes went unconsulted. Croaker had read it twice, made the noise he made when handed something that would annoy at least two other departments, and initialled the corner. He had not asked why the request named a night, or a standoff distance, or the manufacturer's engineer. Orla had come home with the signature and sat with her tea going cold, because a thing signed that easily was a thing somebody wanted, and she had not yet worked out whether that somebody was her.

The costs were itemised on the same page as the design, in two hands. Hers: the assessor of record for the Kessler matter had now escorted the Kessler matter into the Ministry of Magic on a docket she drafted — the state's machinery used a second time to launder what it existed to find. His: he was the breach now in person as well as on paper; if this surfaced, *I didn't know* was available to nobody — the sentence Rose had made even in June now divided among three people. Under the costs, the protocol, because he had insisted the design be written before the result existed and be dull enough to embarrass them into rigour.

Three arms. Arm one, the rack parked: powered, idle, reading nothing. Arm two, the same model at the same watts reading a control slice — the continental annexe, the shards no hand had ever re-issued — chosen so that between arm two and arm three nothing differed but what the machine was reading: not heat, not noise, not the hum Rose's meters had mapped to four decimal places of boredom. Arm three, the handled slice. The keepers would not be told which arm was running at any time; neither would Orla; the schedule lived with Rose, sealed, and changed on her mark. Five reversals minimum. And one honest sentence at the bottom in his own hand, because somebody in the room had to be dull about it and it was his turn: *a null result bounds almost nothing — absence of appetite, one rack, one night.* If the specimen ignored them, they would know a little less than nothing, at considerable cost, and he had signed his name under that too.

"The keepers think we're mad, by the way," Rose said, laying out her meters along the corridor wall. "Feeding a soul-eater a computer. The old one asked me whether the Department had run out of proper blasphemy and had to import it." She wrote her supply figures into the log, in sentences, because the assessor's standing instruction had outlived every joke made about it. "I told him yes."

* * *

The keepers came up the corridor at two with the bell.

It was black iron on a wheeled carriage, the size of a church font, and it announced itself the way weather does. The lamps along the corridor sank and went bluish. His breath appeared in front of him, surprised. Rose's meters, fifteen feet away, lost a little of their composure the moment the shutter came open and then kept losing exactly that much, steadily, all night — a constant, she said aloud for the log, and constants were furniture.

What Adam had inside the cold was not a constant. He had read the literature his side of the wall had on despair and found it, for the first time in his life, underpowered. The cold came in under the sternum, where the badge was, and began going through the evening's likely outcomes the way he would have — methodically, without appetite — except that every branch came back the same colour, and then it went further down and found the thing it wanted and held it up: two facing pages of a field notebook, dense survey shorthand on the left, and on the right, in his own handwriting, in his own idiom, *nothing here*. The hour the moor had taken from him. He had been edited once, competently, in his own voice, and the specimen fifteen feet away had located that fact in him faster than any instrument he owned could locate anything, and was — the word arrived on its own — *savouring*.

He could not see it. That was the clause from the briefing he had filed under interesting and now understood as load-bearing: Muggles feel them and do not see them. What he saw was a bell with its shutter open and nothing in the opening, frost growing on the grille in fern-leaves, and two professionals in grey watching a point in the air above the carriage with the settled attention of men reading a gauge.

"Attending," the senior keeper said. "On us. All four of you, stand still."

Arm one, parked. Twenty minutes by the mechanical watch. The point in the air attended to the warm end of the corridor, which was to say to the people, which was to say normal: the keeper's voice ticked off its small movements like a coastguard logging swell. Arm two went live on Rose's mark — the rack's note deepened a quarter-tone, the same watts, the same fans — and the specimen went on facing the people, and the cold went on working through Adam's evening, and nothing in the corridor changed for half an hour except his opinion of the men who did this for a wage.

"Mark," Rose said, for the third time, and the schedule in her sealed envelope turned over to arm three, and the machine at his back began to read the handled slice.

"Tasting," the senior keeper said.

It was a term of the trade. The keeper had explained it at two o'clock in the flat voice of a man handing over an operations manual: they taste before they turn, especially the old ones — a long stillness while whatever a Dementor uses for attention runs over every warm thing in reach, deciding. The stillness stretched. Frost cracked, minutely, somewhere on the carriage.

"Turned."

The word was so quiet, and so unlike an event, that Adam's first response was to check the watch. Then the junior keeper took one step backwards, which in the vocabulary of that pair was a shout, and the senior keeper said, in the voice men use when a gauge exceeds its printed scale, "Face on the door. It's left us. Thirty-one years, and it has never once—" and stopped, being a professional, and gave the position instead, level and continuous, while the point in the air above the carriage pressed itself against the front grille of the bell, facing the open store door and the eight quiet nodes beyond it, the way a child stands at an aquarium.

The people were still there. That was the whole finding, and Adam made himself state it in the silence where his pulse was: four warm bodies stood in that corridor, each one carrying a lifetime of the only nourishment the literature allowed this creature, and it had its face against the bars for a rack of spinning metal reading a corpus.

"Mark," Rose said. Her voice had gone thin. Arm two. The rack's note held; the watts held; the only thing in the world that changed was what the machine was reading — and the specimen came off the grille slowly, tasted, resettled toward the people like an instrument returning to zero.

They ran the reversal five times. Five times the schedule turned over in silence, unannounced, unbriefed, and five times the keepers' calls tracked it to the minute: on the door, on us, on the door, on us, on the door. The last time, nobody said mark and nobody moved and the corridor stood in its blue cold listening to a machine read, and Orla — who had been fast and funny at half past one, and quick and useful at three — said, plainly, with no joke anywhere in her:

"There's soul in it."

"There is something in that data," Adam said, and heard his own qualifiers queue up and fail to arrive, "that the only soul-sensor either civilisation has ever produced files under food."

"That's what I said."

Rose wrote the log while the keepers shuttered the bell, and read it back at four in the morning to the assessor of record, as instructed, in sentences:

> *Operator. Expected the specimen to prefer us. It did not. Five reversals, five agreements; positions per the keepers' calls, times per the watch. I have written this down slowly to make it stop being true, and it has not stopped being true.*

* * *

Hermione Granger came down at six, before the building filled, and took the reading herself.

Adam spent her visit inside the store with the rack, being the manufacturer's man — a clipboard, a torch, an open panel that needed nothing — while the corridor beyond the doorway held the meeting. It was a strange discipline, being furniture. Through the gap he could see her at three-quarter profile by lamplight: smaller than her memoranda, and precise the way the good referees were precise, no motion spent on being seen to think. The man beside her had arrived without being announced and stood without needing anything, and Adam knew who he must be less from Orla's stories than from the keepers, who deferred to nobody and had straightened anyway.

The keepers gave their account. The senior keeper gave the thirty-one years, and the five reversals, and then, because fear files its report too, gave the rest.

"Begging the room's pardon, but somebody has to put it on the table, and I've the seniority to be laughed at. There's one wizard in living memory who kept pieces of his soul in things. If what's got into that machine is a piece of Voldemort, I'm asking to be reassigned, and I'm asking it in writing."

"No." Hermione did not raise her voice; the room reorganised itself around the word anyway. "Diary, ring, locket, cup, diadem, the snake. Six made; six unmade. I can give you the date of each destruction and the instrument that did it — one of them I destroyed myself, with a basilisk fang, and I re-read that accounting every year the way other people visit graves. It closed in May 1998. It stays closed." She let that stand exactly long enough. "And the description is wrong, which matters more than the arithmetic. His fragments were loud. The diary spent fifty years in a bag and struck at the first lonely child who spoke to it — it dominated, it fed, it could not wait. You are describing something that has had the run of the world's records for years and touched nothing but their tidiness. Patience was never his signature. Whatever your machine was reading, it is not him."

"Then it's one of ours," she went on, before relief could get comfortable, "or it was. That is the honest next question, and I'd rather ask it in this corridor than in a hearing. This department has been robbed twice in my working life — once through a curse on a clerk, once by a senior Unspeakable selling his own floor. Names, please, of everyone who fits: trained here, knew our Britain, could keep a secret inside a secret."

The shortlist was short. Rose went and came back with a personnel jacket a quarter-century closed, and read the end of it into the lamplight:

> ROOKWOOD, Augustus. Unspeakable, 1971–1981. Convicted on full count, September 1998. Died in custody, Azkaban, 9 February 2011. Register of Deaths in Custody, entry countersigned; interment on the island, witnessed. File closed.

"The Auror Office verifies deaths in custody," Hermione said. "It verified this one."

"Twice," said Harry Potter. It was the first thing he had said since arriving. He did not explain it, and the room decided as one not to ask, and Adam revised his estimate of how the man had survived his biography.

Harry asked his questions the way a man checks a perimeter — short, flat, in an order that had nothing to do with rank. Who else knows what happened in this corridor tonight. Count them for me. He took the count — two keepers, the Head of Department, the operator, the assessor, the engineer — and said, "Keep it that way. I want the names in writing, on my desk, Monday: everyone through that door since the machine came in." Then he looked into the store, at the open panel, at the clipboard, at Adam.

"Who's he?"

"The manufacturer's man," Orla said. "The Apparatus doesn't maintain itself."

"Does he know what he watched tonight?"

"I know the machine," Adam said, because the pause had reached him and true sentences were the house style. "And I watched a cold I can't explain point at it, five times, on a blind schedule. I'd want that repeated before I'd sign anything."

Harry looked at him for exactly as long as the watchwizard's rod had, with much the same result.

"Monday," he said to Orla, and left the way he'd come, without goodbyes, a man with a list forming.

Hermione stayed thirty seconds longer. She looked at the rack through the doorway; she looked at the chart of Rose's meters taped to the store wall; she looked, longest of all, at the bell going away down the corridor between its keepers.

"Whose idea was the specimen?"

"Mine," Orla said.

"It is the best idea anyone in this building has had since March," Hermione said, "and I am counting my own building. When this becomes a file — and it will — I want its first page to be dull. Dull survives appeal." At the lift she paused, and the warmth arrived the way Orla had once described it arriving, suddenly and entirely, which made it worse. "Good morning, Miss Quinn. You do keep turning out to be where the Department needs somebody."

The lift took her. From the black-door end of the corridor, unhurried, came Croaker, who had been standing there for some part of the morning nobody could name, and who now stopped at the store, read the docket's visitor line — the line with a name on it — for four long seconds, and initialled the corner without comment.

"Interesting nights file themselves," he said, to the rack, or the corridor, or neither, and went back through the black door.

* * *

By eight the bell was back in its deep chamber, the corridor was a corridor, and the three of them stood in the store with the suspects page open between them, steam off Rose's tea climbing through the lamplight.

Two lines were struck through now, in ink, with the evidence dated. The name said once in the small hours was accounted for — six fragments, six destructions, an accounting kept closed by the person least likely on earth to be sentimental about it. The Department's one traitor was twenty-two years in the ground with his interment witnessed twice. Below the struck lines the page was clean. There was nobody left to suspect. There was only the thing itself, which had no line, because a line needed a noun and the group had none to give it — only Orla's job-title for it, which had stuck the way she'd known it would, so that even Rose's log now read *consistent with curation; refer to the Cartographer file*, as if the file explained anything, as if a name for the work were a name for the hand.

"Say the result," Orla said. "Properly. For the record we don't keep."

"Something is in the corpus that reads to a Dementor as soul," Adam said. "It is densest where the record has been handled. It survives being copied, because everything we ran last night was a copy of a copy. And it is not a metaphor. I have been calling it a pattern since March because pattern was the biggest word I could defend. As of four o'clock this morning the defensible word is bigger."

Rose turned her mug by its handle, a quarter-turn, the way her father turned a Quaffle he was about to say something about.

"You know what we did tonight," she said. "We put a specimen in front of a specimen. And of the three parties in that corridor — the one in the bell, the one in the rack, and the six of us — I'll give you evens on which was under observation."

Somewhere below them, in a night-book at a security desk, his name sat in a careful hand, doing nothing, the way a number sits in an archive until a better instrument comes. Adam picked the badge off the desk, and Orla held out her hand for it, and put it in her pocket, one record, hers now.

The suspect list was empty. The thing on the rack was real.
