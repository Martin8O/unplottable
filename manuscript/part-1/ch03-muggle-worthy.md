On Friday morning, for the first time in six years, Orla got out of the lift before the bottom.

"Level Three," said the lift's cool voice, with the satisfaction of a list well kept, "Department of Magical Accidents and Catastrophes, incorporating the Accidental Magic Reversal Squad, Obliviator Headquarters and Muggle-Worthy Excuse Committee." She had ridden past that announcement five mornings a week for six years and never once stepped out into it. Level Nine kept its winter; Level Three, it turned out, had been hoarding the summer. Lamps everywhere. Tea everywhere. Memos in flocks, banking round corners with a swagger the ones downstairs never allowed themselves. Somewhere out of sight a man said "before lunch, or the excuse writes itself" in the voice of a person who loved his work.

The briefing room at Obliviator Headquarters held about twenty people and one wall. The wall was the point: Great Britain in relief, hip-height to ceiling, stuck with a century of pins whose little enamel heads had the sheen of things polished by worry. It was beautiful, and it was the Hall's opposite number — everything this map knew, it knew because somebody had flown there and dealt with it.

Pinned up beside it, on Muggle paper that looked wrong in the room, like a bandage on a portrait — Figure 1. Somebody had made copies of the copy; the greys had come out too dark, so that Britain sat in shadow and the white sites burned.

"Miss Quinn." The man running the room came round the table to meet her with his hand out. Tall, her side of forty, hair a steady strong-tea brown; robes with the Squad's crossed-wands-and-daisy on the shoulder, which she would have found funny yesterday. "Ted Lupin. You're the Department's reader." His handshake was glad, and the gladness landed on her like a chair pulled out. "You've no idea the relief. We've spent three days pretending we could read it, and we were about to be found out."

"Orla Quinn. I mostly dust things, sir."

"On this floor everybody mostly tidies up," Lupin said. "You'll fit." And to the room, without seeming to raise his voice at all: "Right. Convene."

What she had expected — panic worn institutionally, the corridor horseshoe scaled up — was not what she got. What she got was a machine coming up to temperature, and the machine was old, and the machine was loved.

Lupin ran the stand-up off three fingers. The sites: a survey of every listed concealment on the wall map, wards walked, Muggle-Repelling Charms tested and where wanted reinforced — the crews already rostered, the first out that afternoon. The source: assessment of the author, of which more shortly, and the room's eyes crossed her like a draught. The story: through the open door at the room's end, the Muggle-Worthy Excuse Committee sat in session around a blackboard, arguing in the low pleasant murmur of thirty years' colleagueship, the board behind them carrying its column of struck-through lines with the dignity of a war memorial.

"For the benefit of our guest," Lupin said, "the floor's credentials." He said it kindly, and the room sat up the way a family does when asked for the good story. "The World Cup, ninety-four: a full campsite of Muggles woke up under a burnt sky with not one memory they weren't issued. New York, 1926 — an American matter, but the trade cites it the way Miss Quinn's floor cites its prophecies: the better part of a city saw the impossible, and by morning the city had agreed on the weather. That was a century ago, with 1926 staffing. The Statute has been leaking since 1689. It has never once sunk. There is a procedure, the procedure is old, and the procedure has never lost."

It was — and she made herself hold the two halves of the thought, because both were true — the best working practice she had ever seen presented, and it was the registry of a library that had already burnt down.

She kept her face still. Three days on this floor; a guest; the only person in the room who had needed the lift announcement. On the roster board behind Lupin the field strength stood in two neat columns, fifty-two names, some double-underlined for standby, and she caught herself waiting for the board to refresh, and folded the thought away where the rest of them lived.

Fifty-two. On Tuesday she had told the corridor the paper was past fetching — copied beyond any counting the Ministry could do. The room had heard her; the room had minuted her; the machine had translated her into its own grammar, and its grammar said: then we hold the connection instead. Muggles could keep their map. What they could never be allowed to keep was the word for it. Assess the author. Reinforce the sites. Issue the excuse. Every stroke of it executed with the competence of a century, and all of it aimed, she thought, at the one man in Britain who no longer mattered — because the paper did not live in the author any more; it lived in the world's own records, and records were the one thing Muggles had never once agreed to stop keeping.

She had the argument whole, ordered, indexed, the way she ordered things when she was frightened. She put none of it in the room. You did not tell a machine mid-stroke that the river had moved; and besides — the honest half of the reason — she had been wrong before about what old institutions could do. The Hall had taught her that too.

Croaker sat at the far end of the table with his hands folded, attending the way the Hall's candles burned — present, cold, giving nothing to read — and when a Squad officer asked what the Department of Mysteries advised, he said, "The Department has no view; it never has. It has, however, lent you its reader, and I advise the room to use her," and went back to being furniture.

"The Committee will want the reader," said the Committee's chairwoman from the doorway, a round grey witch with chalk on her cuff. "Before we waste a fourth day. Miss Quinn — the excuse. What survives contact?"

The board behind her listed the candidates. Instrument fault. Military exercise. Weather anomaly, persistent. Mapping error, propagated. Each struck through, and under them, fresh and not yet crossed out, planted retraction — author withdraws method.

"None of them, ma'am," Orla said. "And the first one especially not — the fault line. Their machines have been making that exact excuse for years." The room went interested. "Their reading engines saw these sites long before any person did, and filed them as broken instruments, every night, because that's the tidy answer. The paper is forty pages of why the tidy answer died. The strongest excuse anyone will ever write for us was theirs, and automatic, and free — and it's spent. Anyone who checks the sums now arrives at the paper's answer, not ours." She nodded at the last line on the board. "And a retraction retracts nothing. The method's public. It's like — " careful, translate " — like recalling one owl after you've posted the letter in every fireplace in Britain."

"Hm," said the chairwoman, in the tone of a professional revising an estimate, not surrendering it. "Then we need a better class of broken instrument." She went back in and shut the door, and Orla stood there holding the sentence, unable to decide if it was magnificent or a headstone.

"The concordance," Lupin said, and stood back for the Squad's cartographer.

The cartographer was an elderly wizard with the ink of fifty years on his cuffs, and he took up station between the relief wall and the Muggle map with the unhurried grief of a man introducing two relatives who should never have met. Three days of matching, he said, white mark by white mark, against the wall's registry of everything Britain hides. He read the marriages out loud, and an assistant inked each true name onto the fair copy in a fine registry hand, and the room watched its own life written onto Muggle paper.

The great blot in the Highlands: the school, with Hogsmeade inside the same white — no surprise, the largest thing they kept, and even so a sound went round the room at seeing it there, the sound you make when the Healer says the word you came in already knowing. The stitch of pinpricks in London: the Alleys, Diagon and Knockturn together. St Mungo's. "And this building," the cartographer said, laying his wand-tip on the third pinprick, and the room looked at the small white wound over Whitehall with an odd shy pride until Lupin said, "We make an excellent hole," and let them laugh.

The station platform, someone asked — the platform was checked? Below the map's own floor, the cartographer said: it only surfaced in the September panels, term traffic, a seasonal ghost. The room took the small mercy and held it.

The villages, then, the scatter of white points. Godric's Hollow. Nobody said anything after Godric's Hollow; the name did its own work, and the room stepped round it the way you step round a grave. Ottery St Catchpole and its outliers. Tinworth on the Cornish coast. Upper Flagley — "Yorkshire," the cartographer added, to the room's blank, and half of them had to be told what they had been concealing there for three hundred years, which Orla filed under sentences with more in them than they were saying.

And one mark out on the edge of the Fens that the wall had no marriage for. No listing, no ward on record, no name. The cartographer had checked it three times. The room shifted, uncomfortable, and Orla — who could have told them that being flagged honest-empty was the registry behaving — loved them a little for hating it, and said nothing. The assistant inked a small (?) beside the mark, and it was the first thing on the fair copy she felt at home with.

"Last," the cartographer said. "Offshore. Three marks in open water, or as the paper will have it — " he checked his crib, gamely " — 'sustained anomalies in maritime channels'. Two we make out to be the beast reserves — listed, warded, expected." His wand moved north, tapped the empty grey of the North Sea. "This one we presumed an artefact of their sums. There is nothing out there to find."

He said it as a closing line. He was already turning back to the wall.

"It found Azkaban."

She hadn't meant it to come out plain. She had matched the mark on Wednesday night, alone with the coastal panels, and carried it since like a stone in the pocket; there were kinder ways to hand it over, and she used none of them.

The room stopped. Not a gasp — worse: the particular silence of twenty professionals each privately re-deriving the same result. Lupin's hair went one shade darker, like tea left steeping, and she watched him notice it and put it back.

"Go on," he said. The warmth had not left his voice. Something underneath it had gone still.

"There's nothing out there to be absent from — that's the point, that's why it should have been safe. No footfall, no roads, nothing carried in a pocket. But their ships write down where they go, and have done for sixty years, and the routes bend. Round a point on no chart, in weather their buoys can't account for, the same bend every year since before I was born. The sea traffic files a curve and gives no reason." She touched the mark once, where the assistant had not yet inked anything. "Absence was the school's mistake. Azkaban's mistake is older. It's the bending."

An Obliviator near the back — one of the double-underlined names, a woman with thirty years of fieldwork in her stillness — said, to the map rather than the room: "There is no Muggle-Worthy Excuse for that island. There is nothing out there to tell the excuse to."

"Thank you," Lupin said, easy, catching the room before it could tip. "Then it goes top of the survey and bottom of the excuse queue, and the Committee can have the afternoon off." The laugh he got was thin, but he got it; she saw, then, what the manner was for, and that it was a tool, and that he was better with it than anyone she had ever watched work. "Assignments as listed. Crews out at two. Miss Quinn — a word."

The assistant, before the room rose, wrote the island's name onto the fair copy in the fine registry hand, and Orla watched the ink go down and thought: three hundred years of nobody to practise on, and the first honest record of what we are is being drafted on their paper, in our hand.

* * *

The side office was small and looked like Lupin: tidy, warm, a kettle, a photograph of a woman in Healer's lime-green laughing at whoever held the camera. He did not offer tea, which she understood two sentences later, because the manner went off like a lamp being covered, and what sat across the desk from her then was the other thing, the thing the manner carried around all day: a senior officer of the Statute's last line, being exact.

Later she would learn the rule the whole floor knew: Ted Lupin went formal the way other men went pale.

"Assessment file," he said, and put it on the desk between them, squared to the edge. "Breach, documentary, 2033 stroke one. Coverage as currently established."

The list was short and ruled in the Squad's careful columns:

> KESSLER, A. — author (source, primary) — assessment: ASSIGNED
> NAIR, P. — author (second) — assessment: pending source
> BAIRD, E. — programme director (funder of record) — assessment: pending source
> FURTHER EXPOSURE — see appendix

The appendix was one sheet. Somebody had ruled columns on it, dated it, and stopped; the columns stood empty the whole width of the page, and of everything she had seen on Level Three it was the only thing that looked like the truth.

"Nair, P.," she said. The file gave the second author twenty-six years of age. A year younger than herself, and on a Ministry coverage list because she had checked another person's sums and signed her name to say so. Which was, on Level Nine, the whole moral law. "She's a student, sir."

"She is exposure," Lupin said, without unkindness and without give. "As is the funder, as is the author's landlady if the assessment finds she heard him rehearse the talk. That is what the list is for: so that nothing is done to anyone who is not on it, and nothing is done at all until the assessment says." He turned the file to face her. "Which brings us to the assessment. The recommendation is not mine, Miss Quinn. The office executes; it does not judge. Under the procedure, judgement belongs to a designated assessor with the languages to make it — and the floor has no one who reads Muggle, still less one of the Muggles in question." A beat. "The Department of Mysteries has exactly one."

The docket he slid across last was a single page, and the page was mostly white space, and the white space was the point:

> ASSESSMENT DOCKET — BREACH (DOCUMENTARY) 2033/1
> Subject: KESSLER, ADAM — Muggle, author (source, primary)
> Assessor: QUINN, ORLA — Dept. of Mysteries (seconded)
> Recommendation of assessor: ____________________
> Action (Obliviator Headquarters use only): per recommendation of assessor

"Nothing happens that you have not signed," Lupin said. "No wand goes near the man, or the student, or anyone, on any line of that list, except over your recommendation. That is not a courtesy. It is the procedure protecting itself: the one who judges must be the one who understood." He said it the way the Hall's doctrine deserved to be said, and she sat with the horror of being agreed with. The signature was the mercy. The signature was the trap. The Hall had spent six years teaching her to put her name only under what was true, and here was Level Three, courteously, procedurally, handing her the pen.

"And the access," she said, because her voice wanted somewhere procedural to stand. "I don't imagine I knock and say the Ministry of Magic sends its regards."

"You are an integrity auditor," Lupin said, "for the body that funds him." He laid the last sheet on the desk: her legend, in the Committee's bland perfect English — a review ahead of renewal, routine, cooperative. "His award is up for renewal in the autumn. A man with a renewal pending makes time for his funder, answers fully, and checks nothing. The Committee builds every door out of what the subject is already worried about. It is the oldest tool on the floor."

"You're using the thing he's frightened of."

"We always are," Lupin said. The formality thinned for a moment, and underneath was something she had not expected to find here: the specific tiredness of a man very good at a job he hoped never got easier. "Frightened people ask fewer questions, Miss Quinn; it's why the trade works, and I've never once liked it. So I am quick, and I am kind, and I get them back to their lives before the fear has time to set." He squared the papers, and the manner came back up like the lamp uncovered, and the interview was over. "Tuesday morning, then. Go by whatever means your cover would go by."

"The train," she said. "An auditor would take the train."

"There," he said, warm as the corridor outside, "you see — a natural."

* * *

Level Nine kept its winter, and at the end of the day she went down into it gratefully, like cold water, past the black door, down the corridor where the memos flew joylessly and at the proper altitude.

Rose came out of the thought section's corridor wheeling nothing for once, hands in her pockets, which on Rose meant the day's winnings were already banked. She fell into step without asking, which was the whole of what Orla needed.

"Sickle from Tuesday," Rose said. "You owe me. There was a leaving do — there were biscuits and everything. You just weren't at it."

"That was a Squad briefing I was at."

"Mm. And a Galleon, new bet, says the excuse they land on is weather." Rose's eyes went sideways, doing the arithmetic on her the way her whole family did it, each in their different currency. "Level Three gave you homework. You've got the face. Like a label that's just been re-inked and doesn't trust the new hand."

"I can't—"

"—say. I know. We're a fun floor." They had reached the Keeper's office; Rose stopped at the threshold, respecting it the way the Department respected all its locked rooms: by pretending not to want to. For a moment the mischief sat down. "You're all right, though."

It was not really a question, and Orla, who could not answer any of the questions it was standing in for, said, "Ask me Wednesday," and Rose said, "I'll have odds by then," and was gone up the corridor, keeping score of something, aloud, all the way to the lifts.

The Keeper's office was cold and dark and hers for however long nobody remembered to take it away, and she lit the lamp and put the assessment file on the blotter where the grey folder had sat on Tuesday, in the middle, squared, the way consequential paper arranged itself down here.

She opened it once before going home.

On top, printed from the university's public directory by the same careful unnamed hand that compiled the folders, a staff photograph. A tall man, jumper under a jacket, smiling the exact polite width of a man told to smile for a directory; behind him a wall of printouts he had refused to tidy for the occasion. KESSLER, ADAM, said the caption's borrowed grey letters. Lecturer, World Model Reliability Group. And beneath the photograph, in the Committee's bland hand, the door they had built for her: award renewal pending, autumn 2033.

He didn't look like the end of the world. But then nothing in her keeping ever had. That was what labels were for; that was why you flagged them, dated them, and left the question open — and her question had a date now, Tuesday, the nine o'clock from King's Cross — no. She caught the thought being tidy and made herself say it the way the record would want it said.

The interview was scheduled. The target had a name, a staff photo, and a pending grant renewal.
