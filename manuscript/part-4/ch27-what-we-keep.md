By the second week the fourth floor had learnt her step, which was what a corridor did instead of asking how you were. The witch on the ward desk had stopped reaching for the visitors' book when Orla came up the stairs; the book lay open anyway, one line a visitor, the neatest register in the building, and every Friday it said the same two things. *Quinn, O.* And four lines up, in a hand that leant hard to the right and stopped for nobody: *Potter, G.*

"She's better on Fridays," the witch on the desk said, not looking up, in the tone of a woman recording rainfall. "Sleeps, after. We don't write down why."

The aunt was in. Orla had meant to leave the drafted card and go — a family's hour was a family's hour — but the door stood a hand's width open, and the first thing through the gap was the smell of scorched pasteboard, and the second was a voice that turned out, when she found the angle, to belong to a face off the back page.

"—and that's the seven, which you knew was coming, which is why you've been guarding the jug since the deal."

Exploding Snap, on the over-bed table, at speed.

Ginny Potter was smaller than her byline and harder-wearing, and she dealt the way the Healers took blood: no ceremony, no warning, no sympathy to clean up afterwards. Rose sat in the chair — of course in the chair — with her sleeves pushed up and the old flush on, and marked Orla's arrival with two lifted fingers, in, sit, wait, without leaving the game. So Orla sat on the radiator shelf and watched the only treatment on the floor that was working.

They did not talk about it. That was the first thing. In eleven days Orla had watched everyone who entered that room pay a toll at the door — the lowered voice, the weather remarked on, the how-are-we-this-morning — and the aunt had walked past the toll like a season-ticket holder. They played three hands. The deck went off twice and took the corner off a get-well card up from the Fens crew, and was shuffled straight back. Rose lost the third hand to a guarded jug and swore with some care, and Ginny said, "You get that from your father. Your mother swears like she's citing it," and dealt again.

It was somewhere in the fourth hand, eyes on her cards, that Ginny said, "Sleeping?"

"They ask me that every morning."

"I'm not asking what they ask." The cards went round. "Three o'clock. What does it want you doing?"

The pause was small, and it was not the pause of somebody deciding whether to answer. It was the pause of somebody who had waited eleven days for the question to arrive in units she recognised.

"Reading," Rose said. "There's no console in the building. So: reading."

"Mm." Ginny put down a pair. "And you miss it."

It was not a question. Nobody in eleven days had dared it even as a question, and here it was laid on the blanket as a fact, between the three of spades and the water jug, by a woman keeping score.

"Yes," Rose said. "At three o'clock, every night, I miss it. That's the sentence I can't say in this building, because they'd fetch somebody."

"You've just said it."

"You're not somebody."

"No," Ginny agreed, and turned a card, and the deck cracked and put a thin blue smoke ring up towards the ceiling, and neither of them looked at it. "It was the best work of your life and it was done to you. Both of those. You're allowed to keep both halves." She gathered in the trick with one hand. "That's the whole of it, there isn't any more, and anyone who tells you there's a trick to carrying it is selling something."

Rose looked at her aunt for a moment with her mouth doing the thing that was not a smile.

"The Healers say I should be patient with myself," she said, in the voice she kept for quoting.

"The Healers are lovely," Ginny said, "and they've a ward full of people a spell went wrong on, and you're not one of them, and it's making everybody itch." She dealt the next hand. "Play your sevens properly."

She stayed the hour. She left the way she had evidently arrived, coat over one arm, no flowers, no advice left hanging in the air — and stopped in the corridor at Orla's shoulder, eyes on the ward door, and the friendliness went off her the way heat goes off a road.

"They'll offer the clean fix soon," she said. "This week, next. It's the only thing on their shelf."

"I know."

"It's not the only thing." She put her coat on. "Four o'clock, I'm told. Rose wants you in the room. Be in the room." And she went down the stairs at a deadline's pace, past Harry Potter at the window end — two paper cups going cold in his hands, nothing at all on show — who fell in behind her without either of them saying a word.

* * *

The Healers asked for the family at four, in a room at the corridor's end with eight chairs and a window painted shut, and the family, it turned out, was three people and a closed door. Rose. Her mother. And Orla — "She wants her registrar," the senior Healer had reported, passing on the word with the shrug of a man who didn't own it. Her dad had been in that morning; the greaseproof evidence of it was still folded on the sill. On the far side of the door's glass panel stood the Head Auror, not in the meeting, not away from it, studying the corridor as if it had applied for something.

Hermione Granger came as the mother and did not entirely manage it. She had no papers with her. Orla understood, before the end, that this was the visible effort — a woman who carried files the way other people carried keys, sitting on a hospital chair with her hands empty in her lap.

The senior Healer was big and slow and kind, and had done this conference many times with worse news, and did it plainly.

"The assessment's finished. I'll not dress it up: we have found nothing to treat, because there is nothing in her that any instrument of ours has an opinion about. What there is" — one hand flat on the folder, which stayed shut — "is five months that come back every night at the same hour. We can't take away what was done. We can take away her having to carry it."

He let that sit, and then gave them the shape of it, because he was a decent man and the shape was the honest part. The window was well defined: June to the twenty-first of October. The work was clean and old and well understood; it was done on consent, at St Mungo's, perhaps a dozen times a year. Her training stayed. Her results stayed in the record. Her section would give her a plain account of the gap — that she had been ill, that she was well — and before any of it she would write herself a letter, under seal, in her own hand, saying that she had chosen it, so that nobody ever woke to a hole with no floor in it.

Orla knew the shape of that sentence. She had read it a hundred times in the Committee's files, pointed the other way.

"She would sleep," the Healer said, to Hermione, and it was an offer of mercy and he meant every word of it.

Hermione was quiet long enough that the radiator ticked.

"Show me the seam," she said. "You would take June to October out. She built the case against her own compromise in four hours at a bench — her examiners spent those hours failing to find one join in five months. Everything it did to her, it did in her handwriting. Where does your charm stop?"

"We would take the window entire. Work at that scale isn't — surgical. It's the window or nothing."

"The window is where the order rule was finished. Her section will cite it for a century. When a colleague thanks her for it in February, what is she remembering having done?"

"There are judgement calls at the edges," the Healer said. "There always are. We make them with the family."

"Whose judgement? Name for me the person who decides which pages of my daughter's best year were hers."

Nobody named one. The Healer, to his credit, did not pretend the silence was anything but an answer; he sat with it, and then said, gently, the sentence he had been keeping for last.

"Nobody who loves her can want her to keep this."

"I want to be exact about my qualifications," Hermione said, "before I disagree with you."

She did not raise her voice at any point in what followed, and she did not hurry, and her hands stayed empty in her lap.

"In the last war, before I went where they could not follow, I modified my parents' memories. Both of them. New names, a new country, a new — " and there it stopped, and she was a woman Orla had never once, on the wireless or off it, heard restart a sentence, and she restarted it " — a new life, entire. No daughter in it anywhere. I was seventeen. I did it well. It held for the best part of a year, and nothing found them, and they were happy. That's the piece that gets left out when people tell it, because it doesn't fit anywhere: they were safe, and they were happy, and they had never had a child.

"Afterwards I put it all back. That was the phrase I used for years. Put it back. As if it had been kept for them in a drawer." She was looking at the painted-shut window. "My mother came home to a kitchen with eleven tea towels she'd bought in a country she had never meant to live in. She kept them. She keeps them where I will see them when I visit, and in thirty-five years neither of us has said one word about it. And that is what this looks like when it goes well. When the caster loves you. When you get everything back."

The room was very quiet. Down the corridor a trolley went by with one squealing wheel, taking its time.

"There is no working in that folder that makes her the woman it didn't happen to," Hermione said to the Healer, quite gently now, which was worse. "There is her, minus five months, plus a letter. I have met the people the letters are for. We keep what happened to us. All of it." She said it the way she cited statute, no louder than the rest and no slower, and then she turned to her daughter and the courtroom went out of her entirely, and what was left was somebody's mother on a hospital chair.

"I made this choice once, for two people who could not be asked. I have been told I was right about it for thirty-five years, by everyone, reliably, and it has never once helped. You can be asked." Her hands stayed still. "So I'm not choosing. Whatever you choose, I will be in the room."

Rose had not moved through any of it. She moved now, and it was to look at the Healer with the expression she kept for other people's papers.

"No," she said. "Thank you. It's good work and I don't want it."

"You're certain."

"Write declined against advice if that's the form." The old flush was up, and her voice stayed at the bottom of its range, and it did not shake. "I'm keeping the five months. They're the worst thing I own. Everything I do for the rest of my life gets checked against them, by me, at three in the morning, which as far as I can tell is what they're for." A pause you could have dealt a card into. "Ask me again in ten years. If I'm wrong, I'll pay out. I keep score; it's the family failing."

The Healer looked at her for a moment, and then did the finest thing Orla had heard from a stranger all autumn, which was nothing. He opened the folder, wrote *declined*, dated it, and asked no further question. "Discharge Tuesday," he said. "The bed's wanted anyway."

Orla stayed behind to put the chairs straight, which was not a job, which was the point of doing it.

Item: in a drawer on Level Nine, under two seals, there was a page with eleven words on it in her own hand, and the eleven words were an option, and the option had a name, and the name was not Rose's. Item: she had initialled the sight of them in April and consented to nothing, and had been proud of that distinction for half a year, the way you're proud of a clean bandage. Item: the doctrine was written now. It had been written in front of her, by a woman with empty hands, in a room with the window painted shut, and nowhere in it was there a clause that said *held in reserve pending assessment*.

Through the glass, when Rose came out, the Head Auror took the door and held it, which from him was a speech.

Orla put the last chair straight and did not go down for a while.

* * *

The buzzer went at ten past seven, while Adam was marking the term's second problem sheet and losing, on balance, to question four.

Two men on the step, in the kind of coats that have a job. The older one held up a card with a first name, a surname, and a department whose title had been assembled by a committee tasked with producing a name that contained nothing. The younger one held a folder, and throughout what followed held it as if being assessed on the holding, which he possibly was.

"Dr Kessler? Nothing's the matter." The older man said it first, before anything, the way the trade evidently taught now. "We'd like four minutes, and we're happy to take them here on the step."

"The step suits me," Adam said.

Down towards the water something went up with a soft crump, a night ahead of itself, and dragged a green stripe over the rooftops, and none of the three of them looked at it.

It was, the older man explained, a notification. Not a sanction; not an order; not — he was scrupulous on the word, and had plainly said it many times — an allegation. There was an Act, the 2029 one, and under the Act certain work could be designated, and his had been, as of Wednesday. The folder contained the schedule and a copy was his to keep. He held the page at the angle the stairwell light allowed and read it twice.

> The work described at Schedule A is designated under section 7 of the Act. From the date of service, continuation, publication or communication of designated work other than by arrangement may constitute an offence. This notification is protective in purpose and in effect. It records no allegation and implies no wrongdoing.

Schedule A ran to four numbered items. The first three were his published life rendered into a controlled vocabulary by somebody competent — the residual thread came out as *statistical characterisation of systematic absences in Earth-system model outputs over the territory of the United Kingdom*, which was his own work wearing a lanyard. The fourth item was the vintage-differencing method for dating structures from archive imagery.

The fourth item had never been published. It had never been submitted anywhere. It existed in one grant report, one internal talk, and a job name on the national allocation, and he stood on his own doorstep with the page in his hand and a complete, useless understanding of which of the three had been read.

"You'll have had correspondence in October," the older man said. "About the arrangement. This evening doesn't replace any of that. It frames it."

"Am I under investigation?"

"No." He seemed genuinely pleased to be able to say it. "If anything, Dr Kessler, it's the reverse. From tonight there is a great deal that it is difficult for anyone to do to you over this work, provided the work stays inside the arrangement. It protects you."

"From what?"

"Principally," the man said, with the small pause of someone doing the arithmetic on how much honesty a doorstep would bear, "from the Act."

"And the work?"

"Kept," the man said, as though that were the kindness he had come out on a cold night to deliver. "Nothing is lost and nothing is burned. It's kept."

There was a receipt. Signing acknowledged service and agreed to nothing; he read the small print twice anyway and signed with their pen, which was a better pen than the occasion deserved. The younger man took the pen back and handed over his copy, squared to the folder's edge, and the older one wished him a quiet weekend in the voice of a man who had once been told to soften the ending and had worked out his own way of meaning it.

The stairwell light clicked off behind them on its timer, and clicked on again at the half-landing, and then the close was quiet.

Adam went in. He filled the kettle and stood with his hand on the switch and did not press it. On the whiteboard by the fridge, the term's marking rota went on being true about a world that was three weeks old. He squared the folder to the edge of the table, because leaving it crooked turned out not to be a thing he could do, and found that at some point in the last ten minutes, standing on his own doorstep on an ordinary Friday, he had established without meaning to exactly where his passport was.

He put the thought back where it lived. It went quietly. It had had four generations to learn the way.
